Medical Practice Multi-Scenario Analysis: Owner Q&A

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A proposed practice expansion can look attractive in one forecast and unaffordable in another. Neither result tells the owner what to do until the assumptions, cash exposure and commitment terms are clear.

Medical practice multi-scenario analysis helps owners compare plausible financial outcomes before hiring providers and administrative staff, signing a lease or purchasing equipment. The purpose is not to pick the most appealing forecast. It is to decide which commitments the practice can support, what evidence is missing and what would change the decision.

This Q&A focuses on interpreting an existing analysis—not building a spreadsheet, setting departmental budgets or maintaining a forecast. It ends with an annotated owner-review memo for a synthetic staffed-practice expansion.

What should I ask when the scenarios point to different decisions?

Start with the decision itself. “Should we expand?” is too broad. “Should we sign the second-location lease and authorize provider and administrative hiring before payer participation timing is confirmed?” identifies the actual commitment.

Then ask the preparer to explain the disagreement between scenarios:

  • Which assumptions account for most of the difference?
  • Does the difference affect profitability, available cash or both?
  • When does cash reach its lowest point?
  • Which costs become unavoidable before receipts arrive?
  • Could a smaller or staged commitment remain workable across the plausible cases?

A base case is a planning reference, not an instruction to proceed. A downside case is not automatically a reason to reject a project. The owner needs to compare the potential return with the size, timing and recoverability of the exposure.

For a staffed practice, the critical issue may be whether existing operations can fund payroll while a new location begins collecting—not whether that location eventually reports a profit.

How do I know whether the scenario combinations are plausible?

Ask for a short business explanation behind each case. Labels such as “upside” and “downside” are not enough.

A plausible expansion case connects provider availability, appointment capacity, patient demand, payer receipts, patient-balance collections and staffing costs. It also explains when cash moves. A scheduled visit, recognized revenue and a bank deposit are different events.

Review question Plausible relationship Warning sign
Can the practice deliver the assumed activity? Volume reflects provider start dates and staffed capacity Full activity begins before the provider or support team starts
Can that activity produce the assumed receipts? Collection timing reflects the payer mix and available practice evidence All revenue is treated as immediately collected
Does staffing support the activity? Provider and administrative costs match the opening plan Higher activity appears without associated staffing or supply costs
Is expansion truly incremental? Transfers from the existing site are separated from new business The same receipts appear at both locations
Is funding available where needed? Cash is identified by account and entity Consolidated cash is assumed available to every entity

Do not combine every favorable assumption merely because each is individually possible. Faster opening, immediate provider productivity, prompt collections and minimal staffing costs may not form a credible joint outcome.

Likewise, distinguish a plausible adverse case from a separate severe stress test. Both can inform the decision, but they serve different purposes.

Is a sensitivity test enough, or do I need a full scenario?

A sensitivity test isolates the effect of changing an assumption while holding others constant. It can reveal whether the decision is especially exposed to collection timing, provider compensation or build-out spending.

A full scenario describes a connected operating situation. Several assumptions change together because the underlying event affects them together.

Owner’s question Appropriate analysis How to interpret it
What happens if payer receipts arrive later? Collection-timing sensitivity Shows exposure to that assumption, not a complete alternative future
What happens if opening is delayed but payroll has already started? Full delayed-opening scenario Captures the combined effects on receipts, payroll and occupancy
How much equipment spending can we support? Spending sensitivity with a cash constraint Identifies a potential commitment limit
Should we open fully or stage the expansion? Compare both choices under the same scenarios Tests the decision itself rather than just the forecast

A two-variable sensitivity table can be useful, but it is still not necessarily a coherent scenario. Ask what real operating conditions would produce the combinations shown.

How should correlated assumptions change my interpretation?

Some assumptions move together because they share a cause. A delayed provider start may reduce both receipts and compensation, depending on the arrangement. A delayed opening may reduce receipts without reducing rent or already-committed administrative payroll.

Ask the preparer to distinguish three things:

  1. Linked operating effects: Lower delivered activity may reduce some supply costs.
  2. Contract-dependent effects: A provider’s delayed start may or may not defer compensation.
  3. Management responses: Delaying an additional hire is an action, not an automatic consequence of weak collections.

This distinction prevents hidden optimism. A downside case that quietly assumes immediate staffing reductions can understate exposure if notice periods, employment terms or operating needs make those reductions impractical.

Request a comparison between the downside before corrective action and the downside after a feasible response. Document who could authorize that response and how long it would take to affect cash.

Which outputs matter most before a staffing or capital commitment?

Review profitability and liquidity separately. A location can generate an accounting profit while consuming cash through collection delays, equipment purchases or debt principal payments.

The owner-review packet should show:

  • The lowest projected available cash balance and its timing.
  • Headroom above an owner-approved operating reserve.
  • Cumulative cash committed before the expansion supports itself.
  • Provider and administrative payroll included in the projection.
  • Per-location results, with shared-cost allocation explained.
  • Consolidated and entity-level cash where ownership uses multiple entities.
  • Exit, cancellation or staging options and their remaining costs.

Use a reserve that reflects the practice’s obligations and risk tolerance, not an unexplained universal rule. Also distinguish available operating cash from money already needed for payroll, taxes, debt service or other commitments.

Reliable starting balances matter. IRS Publication 583 discusses complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those records help establish the financial starting point; they do not validate the forecast’s expansion assumptions.

Should I assign probabilities and choose the highest expected return?

Probabilities can organize judgment, but unsupported percentages create false precision. Ask whether the weights come from comparable practice experience, other evidence or management judgment. Also ask whether the scenarios cover the important possible outcomes without overlapping.

A probability-weighted average cash balance does not show whether the practice can meet payroll in the adverse case. Nor does a positive expected return establish that a large, irreversible commitment is affordable.

Use probabilities as a secondary lens:

  • Disclose the basis and uncertainty of the weights.
  • Check whether reasonable alternative weights change the recommendation.
  • Review reserve breaches separately from average outcomes.
  • Do not treat favorable average results as a substitute for committed funding.

If defensible weights are unavailable, compare the cases without assigning probabilities. A decision that remains workable across plausible outcomes may be more useful than an apparently precise expected value.

What should an owner-review memo look like?

The following is an illustrative example using invented assumptions and synthetic model outputs, not a case study, benchmark or recommended reserve level. All dollar amounts, timing and decision limits in this memo are illustrative.

Illustrative memo: second-location expansion

Decision requested: Authorize a second-location opening, including provider and administrative hiring and equipment spending.

Scope: One practice entity operating an established location and a proposed second location. The model covers the first twelve months of the expansion. Results below are for the whole practice, not the new location alone.

Common assumptions: Opening available cash is $500,000. An initial $120,000 expansion payment occurs within the forecast period and is shown separately from ongoing cash outflows. There are no additional borrowings, owner contributions or distributions. The owner’s illustrative minimum operating reserve is $200,000.

Annotation—check the cash bridge: Each ending balance equals opening cash plus receipts, less ongoing outflows and the expansion payment. The cash low comes from the underlying monthly schedule; annual totals alone cannot establish it.

Annotation—explain the downside: Receipts arrive later while provider and administrative payroll continue. Temporary staffing overlap increases outflows. The preparer must show whether missing receipts are deferred beyond the forecast or are no longer expected to be collected. Those are different risks.

Owner interpretation: The base case stays above the selected reserve, but the downside breaches it materially. Upside performance does not provide protection if delayed collections occur. An unconditional approval would therefore rely on accepting the reserve breach or securing additional support that is not currently included.

Questions to ask the preparer before approval

Question Why the answer matters
Does the monthly cash low conceal a tighter payroll-week balance? Monthly reporting can hide short-term payment pressure
What supports the payer receipt timing? Collection assumptions drive the downside exposure
Are provider taxes, benefits and administrative staffing fully included? Salary alone may understate employment cash costs
How much activity moves from the established location? Transfers do not create equivalent new practice-wide receipts
Which shared costs are incremental, and which are allocated? Location profitability and total cash impact answer different questions
Are equipment payments counted only once? Capital payments and related accounting expenses must not be confused

Illustrative decision record: Do not authorize the full opening yet. Permit only a separately approved, cancellable planning phase with a $15,000 spending cap. That phase is an alternative decision and is not included in the full-opening outputs above. Before signing irreversible commitments, obtain the missing evidence and compare the full opening with a staged option under the same adverse conditions.

Evidence that could change the decision: Confirmed receipt timing, enforceable flexibility in major commitments, a feasible lower-cost opening plan or documented committed funding sufficient to address the cash shortfall. None should be treated as available until verified.

What evidence is strong enough to change the recommendation?

Match the evidence to the uncertainty. A stronger referral expectation does not resolve an uncertain lease obligation. A favorable annual profit projection does not resolve a near-term payroll gap.

For each unresolved assumption, record the present evidence, what would count as stronger evidence, who will obtain it and which decision it could change. Distinguish facts from judgments: an executed agreement and an owner’s confidence in demand should not carry the same evidentiary weight.

This is a decision checkpoint, not a requirement to keep revising the model until it produces approval. If the critical evidence remains unavailable, deferral, a smaller commitment or rejection may be the appropriate documented outcome.

How can finance support make the review more useful?

A finance team can help separate accounting results from cash timing, reconcile the starting position, explain shared costs and challenge assumptions before the owner commits.

The final owner decision should stand on its own: what is approved, what remains unapproved, how much exposure is accepted and what evidence would change the choice. A useful multi-scenario review does not eliminate uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty—and the resulting commitment—explicit.
