Cost of Debt Comparisons for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A financing quote with the lowest interest rate is not necessarily the least expensive loan. And the smallest monthly payment may simply mean your medical practice will remain in debt longer—or face a large balance at maturity.

For owners and managers of staffed U.S. private medical practices, the useful benchmark is a set of actual quotes based on the same borrowing profile. Compare the same funding amount, borrowing entity, repayment period and security requirements before deciding whether a price is competitive.

This guide explains how to normalize quotes, calculate borrowing costs and identify terms that deserve review with your CPA, lending adviser and attorney. The goal is to evaluate financing already under consideration, not to decide whether to expand or choose debt over equity.

Start With One Consistent Borrowing Profile

Before requesting quotes, prepare a short financing request that every lender receives. Otherwise, one lender may price an equipment-secured loan while another prices a broader working-capital facility. Their rates would not represent equivalent offers.

Specify:

  • Borrower: The exact legal entity seeking financing, including any proposed co-borrowers.
  • Funding requirement: The usable proceeds needed and whether closing costs will be paid separately or deducted from the advance.
  • Purpose: For example, purchasing equipment or refinancing a specified existing balance.
  • Funding date: When proceeds are needed and whether funding occurs once or in stages.
  • Repayment structure: Requested amortization, maturity and any interest-only period.
  • Security: Assets available as collateral and whether owner guarantees are being considered.
  • Expected holding period: Whether the practice expects to keep the loan to maturity or may repay it earlier.

For a multi-location practice, distinguish the borrowing entity from the locations benefiting from the funds. A location-level P&L is not automatically the same as the financial statements of the legal borrower. Identify shared payroll, administrative expenses and intercompany balances so lenders receive a consistent financial picture.

If a lender proposes a different structure, retain it as an alternative—but mark the differences rather than treating its rate as directly comparable.

Separate Interest Rate, Payment Size and Total Cost

These measures answer different questions.

Measure What it tells the practice owner What it does not tell you
Stated interest rate How interest is calculated under the agreement The effect of fees, payment timing or restrictions
Scheduled payment The cash required on a payment date Whether the loan is inexpensive overall
Total financing cost in dollars Interest and included financing charges over a defined period The annualized cost or every contractual risk
Annualized cash-flow cost A timing-sensitive comparison of proceeds and required payments Whether guarantees or covenants are acceptable
Balance at maturity The amount still due when the loan ends Whether refinancing will be available

Principal repayment is a cash obligation, but it is not itself a financing expense. For a fully repaid loan, total financing cost generally consists of interest plus the financing fees and charges included in the analysis.

Keep this comparison before tax initially. Ask your CPA whether any tax treatment changes the result for the specific borrowing entity; do not assume a universal deduction or apply an arbitrary tax-rate discount.

Read the Interest and Repayment Structure

Fixed, variable and quoted charges

For a fixed-rate quote, confirm how long the rate remains fixed and when the commitment expires. For a variable-rate quote, obtain the reference index, lender spread, reset frequency, floor and any cap. A quoted spread without these details is incomplete.

Ask how interest accrues, including the day-count convention and payment frequency. If a quote uses a flat charge or repayment multiple rather than an interest rate, model the actual dated cash flows. A repayment multiple is not an annual percentage rate.

For variable debt, compare offers using the same assumed reference-rate path. Then model a higher-rate scenario to show how the payment or maturity balance could change. These are planning assumptions, not rate forecasts.

Amortization is not maturity

Amortization describes how principal is scheduled to be repaid. Maturity is the date the remaining balance becomes due.

A loan can have payments calculated over a longer amortization period but mature earlier, leaving a balloon payment. An interest-only period can also reduce initial payments without reducing principal.

Request a complete payment schedule showing:

  • Interest and principal for each payment.
  • When principal repayment begins.
  • Any payment increase after an introductory period.
  • The final balance due at maturity.

For a staffed practice, map payment dates against payer receipts, patient collections and provider/admin payroll. This tests the payment schedule—not just whether the annual P&L appears profitable.

Illustrative Example: A Lower Payment Can Cost More

All figures below are invented illustrative inputs, not market rates, lender offers or medical-practice benchmarks.

Assume a practice receives two fixed-rate, fully amortizing quotes for $100,000 of usable funding. Fees are paid separately at closing, not financed or deducted from proceeds. Payments occur monthly, with no balloon, ongoing charges or prepayment penalties. Figures are rounded.

Illustrative term Quote A Quote B
Principal received $100,000 $100,000
Fixed annual interest rate 6% 7%
Repayment term 36 months 60 months
Upfront fees paid separately $2,000 $500
Approximate monthly payment $3,042 $1,980
Approximate total interest $9,519 $18,807
Total financing cost: interest plus fees $11,519 $19,307
Approximate total cash paid, including principal and fees $111,519 $119,307

Quote B requires about $1,062 less each month, but costs approximately $7,788 more over its full term. It has both a higher rate and a longer repayment period. Its smaller upfront fee does not offset the additional interest in this example.

This does not establish which quote is preferable for the practice. It establishes that monthly payment and total cost are different decision criteria.

To isolate pricing from term length, ask both lenders to quote the same maturity and amortization. Do not assume a lender will preserve its offered rate when the term changes.

Calculate a timing-sensitive cost as well

Build a dated cash-flow schedule containing:

  1. Funds received at closing.
  2. Fees paid or withheld at closing.
  3. Every scheduled payment and required ongoing charge.
  4. Any balloon or final fee.
  5. The payoff amount and applicable charges for an early-exit scenario.

An annualized cash-flow calculation can account for fees and when money changes hands. Your CPA or lending adviser can calculate an effective annual borrowing cost using those cash flows. Label the method clearly; an internal comparison is not necessarily a lender-disclosed APR.

Do not estimate an amortizing loan’s all-in annual cost by simply dividing total interest and fees by the original principal and number of years. That shortcut ignores the declining balance and payment timing.

Compare Security, Guarantees and Covenants Separately

Not every financing risk belongs in an interest-rate calculation. Keep a separate assessment of contractual terms rather than assigning unsupported dollar values to them.

Collateral and guarantees

An equipment-only lien and a broader lien covering practice assets are materially different commitments. Ask which assets are pledged, whether existing liens conflict and what must happen for the lender to release its security.

For guarantees, ask counsel to explain who is liable, whether liability is limited, and whether release or reduction provisions exist. A lower rate does not automatically justify broader owner exposure.

Financial and operating covenants

Obtain the actual definitions used in each proposed agreement. A debt-service coverage test may treat owner compensation, distributions, leases and other obligations differently from the practice’s internal reporting.

For multi-entity owners, clarify whether testing occurs at the borrower, combined-group or another specified level. Also identify restrictions on additional borrowing, distributions, asset sales and intercompany transfers.

Ask what happens after a breach, including notice requirements, cure provisions and possible charges. Have counsel review the agreement rather than relying only on a term-sheet summary.

Prepayment provisions

Request payoff calculations for the same potential exit dates. Check for penalties, minimum-interest requirements, exit fees and whether partial prepayments reduce future payments or shorten the term.

If refinancing is plausible, include its estimated transaction costs separately. Do not assume refinancing will be available or cheaper.

Account for Borrower Circumstances Without Inventing Benchmarks

Two practices can receive different terms because their borrowing profiles differ. Relevant factors may include collection consistency, existing debt, available collateral, owner guarantees, financial reporting quality and concentration in particular payers or providers.

Explain material circumstances consistently to every lender. For example, identify unusual payer-payment delays and distinguish recurring provider payroll from a documented one-time expense. Do not present unsupported adjustments as established earnings.

Provide reconciled financial statements, a debt schedule and relevant aggregate collection information. The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations; it is not a source for lending-rate benchmarks.

Use aggregate financial data where possible. Avoid including patient-identifiable information in ordinary lender correspondence, general-purpose AI tools or routine finance discussions.

Questions for Your CPA and Lending Adviser

Before accepting a quote, resolve these questions:

  • Are all offers based on the same usable proceeds, borrower and repayment structure?
  • Have we included every known fee without counting financed or withheld fees twice?
  • What are the full-term cost, annualized cash-flow cost and expected early-payoff cost?
  • How do variable rates or a balloon affect the comparison?
  • Does the payment schedule fit the practice’s collection and payroll cycle?
  • How does the lender define each covenant, and can our books support its reporting requirements?
  • Which provisions need an attorney’s review?
  • Could entity-specific accounting or tax treatment materially change the analysis?

FAQs

What is the best benchmark for a medical practice’s cost of debt?

Comparable, current written quotes for the same borrowing profile are more useful than an unsupported industry average. Align usable proceeds, term, amortization, collateral and guarantees. If those differ, explain the differences before comparing rates.

Is the loan with the lowest monthly payment the cheapest?

Not necessarily. A longer term, interest-only period or balloon can reduce scheduled payments while increasing total interest or leaving a substantial final obligation. Compare payment size, full-term financing cost and maturity balance separately.

Should a practice compare loans before or after tax?

Start with a consistent before-tax cash-flow comparison. Then ask the CPA whether the specific entity’s circumstances and applicable treatment of interest and fees change the result. Do not assume that every charge produces an immediate tax benefit.

What should a practice request before signing?

Request the complete payment schedule, itemized charges, collateral and guarantee terms, covenant definitions, prepayment provisions and final loan documents. Resolve differences between the quote and final agreement before committing.
