SaaS Cash Flow Benchmarks: What Fits Medical Practices?

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A staffed medical practice needs to know whether payer receipts and patient payments will cover provider payroll, administrative wages, rent and other scheduled obligations. A SaaS benchmark may answer a different question: how efficiently a subscription business is growing with investor funding.

The useful lesson from SaaS benchmarking is disciplined measurement—not its numerical targets. Subscription retention, annual recurring revenue and venture-funded burn comparisons can obscure a practice’s actual cash position when their definitions are transferred without adjustment.

For a practice owner, the decision is straightforward: reject metrics built on an incompatible business model, adapt useful financial concepts and replace external targets with internal baselines from reconciled records. Start with three measures: available cash coverage of scheduled obligations, collection conversion by revenue period and operating cash flow adjusted for owner transfers.

Why SaaS cash benchmarks can mislead a practice

SaaS businesses often measure recurring contractual revenue. A medical practice generally collects from services delivered, with payment amounts and timing affected by payer terms, claim processing and patient balances. Repeat visits do not make patients subscribers, and an appointment schedule is not contracted recurring revenue.

Three differences matter most:

  • Subscription retention is not collection reliability. Repeat visits do not establish when a payer will pay or whether an outstanding patient balance will be collected.
  • ARR is not available cash. Annual recurring revenue describes a revenue run rate, not a bank balance. Advance billing may bring a software company cash before service delivery; many practices incur payroll and operating costs before receiving related collections.
  • Venture-funded burn is not a practice liquidity standard. An investor-funded company may deliberately spend more than it collects. A practice cannot assume comparable access to capital or use that spending pattern as evidence that its own deficit is acceptable.

Profitability measures also need care. A favorable margin does not establish that cash will arrive before payroll. Conversely, a weak cash month may reflect collection timing rather than a lasting operating loss.

The denominator—the quantity below the division line—often determines whether a metric is useful. Collections divided by gross charges answer a different question from collections divided by expected collectible revenue. Cash divided by historical average expenses answers a different question from cash divided by payments actually coming due.

1. Measure available cash against scheduled obligations

For near-term decisions, use:

Available cash coverage = available operating cash ÷ scheduled cash obligations over a defined horizon.

Available operating cash should begin with reconciled balances, updated for known activity since reconciliation. Account for outstanding payments and avoid counting the same commitment both as a cash deduction and as a future obligation.

Exclude funds that are restricted or unavailable for the operating decision. Show management-designated reserves separately, with a clear statement of whether the calculation excludes them. Do not count undrawn borrowing capacity as cash; present it separately as a possible funding source.

The denominator should include payments due within the chosen horizon, such as:

  • Provider and administrative payroll, payroll-related remittances and benefits.
  • Rent, supplies, insurance and scheduled vendor payments.
  • Debt service and committed equipment payments.
  • Other known obligations, including tax payments identified with the CPA.

Pair coverage with a dated cash schedule

Illustrative example: A practice has $240,000 of available operating cash and $200,000 of scheduled obligations over the next four weeks. Its cash coverage is 1.2 times those obligations. That is a calculation, not an industry target.

The ratio alone cannot show whether a large payment falls due before expected receipts. Maintain a weekly schedule alongside it:

Projected closing cash = opening cash + expected receipts − scheduled payments.

Keep expected receipts out of the current-cash numerator. Otherwise, the report stops distinguishing money already available from money still subject to collection timing.

For a multi-entity practice, calculate coverage by entity before reviewing consolidated totals. Cash in one entity should not be assumed available to another without confirming transfer arrangements and obligations. A location P&L measures performance; it does not establish where spendable cash sits.

2. Track collection conversion by revenue period

Dividing this month’s receipts by this month’s revenue can create a misleading ratio. Current receipts may relate to earlier services, while much of the current service period remains unpaid.

A more useful internal measure follows a service-period cohort:

Collection conversion at a defined age = cumulative net receipts attributable to that service period ÷ expected collectible revenue for that same period.

Define the expected collectible amount using documented payer and patient responsibility estimates after expected contractual adjustments. Gross charges are generally a poor substitute because changes in charge levels can change the ratio without changing cash performance.

Use receipts net of related refunds and reversals. Document how unapplied receipts are treated, and disclose any amounts that cannot yet be assigned reliably.

Keep the denominator visible

Illustrative example: A service-month cohort has expected collectible revenue of $300,000. At a defined measurement age, net receipts assigned to that cohort total $210,000. Conversion is 70%. This is not a recommended target.

If the practice later reduces expected collectible revenue, the percentage could improve without another dollar arriving. Preserve the original baseline and show revisions separately. That distinguishes better cash realization from a changed estimate.

3. Separate operating cash generation from owner transfers

A rising bank balance does not necessarily mean operations generated cash. An owner contribution or loan draw can conceal an operating deficit. A distribution can make a cash-generating practice appear to be consuming cash.

Build a bridge from the change in reconciled cash to operating cash flow. Identify owner contributions and distributions, borrowing and principal repayments, capital purchases and transfers among included accounts. Transfers between accounts within the reporting group should not create consolidated cash inflows or outflows.

Illustrative example: Cash increased by $20,000 during a month. The owner contributed $50,000 and received a $10,000 distribution. With no other nonoperating movements, operating cash flow was negative $20,000: $20,000 minus the contribution, plus the distribution.

Do not automatically remove all owner-related payments. Compensation for an owner’s work in the practice remains an operating cost in this management view. Confirm classification with the CPA rather than treating every owner payment as a distribution.

For comparisons across periods, present two views:

  1. Actual operating cash flow, after separating financing, investing and ownership movements.
  2. A clearly labeled management-adjusted view, with each unusual operating item shown separately.

Do not normalize away recurring costs merely because they are inconvenient. Annual insurance payments, periodic bonuses and routine equipment maintenance still require funding. Explain their timing instead of hiding them.

4. Use margins for operating decisions, not as cash substitutes

Margin analysis can help an owner compare locations or evaluate staffing commitments, but SaaS cost structures are not appropriate practice targets.

Define a practice contribution measure consistently. State whether provider compensation, clinical supplies, administrative payroll and occupancy costs are included. For location reporting, document how shared administrative costs are allocated and keep the method stable unless there is a justified change.

A location’s margin may improve while its cash collections slow. Likewise, higher collections from older balances may temporarily strengthen cash without improving current service economics. Read margin and collection conversion together, then test the resulting receipt forecast against scheduled obligations.

Worksheet: establish internal cash-flow baselines

Begin with reconciled records rather than a dashboard assembled from mismatched reports. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping and bank reconciliation provides a foundation for complete records and separate business accounts; it does not establish a practice cash target.

Build a history long enough to reveal the practice’s normal timing variation. Compare equivalent collection ages and similar operating periods. Mark unusual events rather than allowing them to silently reset the baseline.

An internal baseline describes what has happened; it does not prove that the result is adequate. Set action triggers from upcoming obligations, receipt uncertainty and available funding options. For example, require owner review before a new commitment if the dated forecast shows cash falling below the practice’s approved operating floor.

Turn the review into an owner decision

Use a regular near-term review to answer three questions:

  1. Can available cash cover scheduled payments? If not, identify the timing gap before approving discretionary spending or distributions.
  2. Are comparable revenue periods converting to cash differently? If so, investigate timing, payer mix, unresolved balances and estimate revisions before assuming revenue has deteriorated.
  3. Did operations generate cash without owner support? If not, separate a temporary collection delay from a persistent mismatch between receipts and operating commitments.

FAQs

Can a medical practice use SaaS cash runway benchmarks?

The runway concept can help evaluate a planned cash deficit, but SaaS targets should not be imported. A practice with uneven collections and scheduled payroll is better served by a dated cash forecast and available cash coverage. If calculating runway, document the spending assumptions and do not assume future financing will arrive.

Why not divide monthly collections by monthly charges?

The amounts usually refer to different service periods, and gross charges may differ substantially from expected collectible amounts. Track net receipts against expected collectible revenue for the same service cohort, measured at comparable ages. Report denominator revisions separately so the percentage cannot improve invisibly through estimate changes.

Should owner distributions be excluded from the cash forecast?

No. Include planned distributions in the dated cash forecast because they reduce available funds. Separate them from operating cash flow when assessing whether the practice funds itself. Owner compensation for work performed should not automatically be treated as a distribution; confirm classifications with the CPA.

What if the practice does not have reliable historical records?

Start by reconciling cash accounts and identifying scheduled obligations. Establish a current liquidity view first, then complete catch-up bookkeeping and reconcile collection reports before treating historical ratios as reliable baselines. Label incomplete measures as provisional rather than comparing them with external targets.
