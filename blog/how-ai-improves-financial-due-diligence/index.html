AI-Assisted Financial Due Diligence for Medical Practice Buyers

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Buying a medical practice requires more than deciding whether the seller’s reported profit looks attractive. You need to understand which earnings are supported by records, which costs will continue after closing, and how much cash the acquired practice will need to operate.

AI-assisted financial due diligence can help organize that investigation. Depending on the tool and document quality, it may help extract figures, compare schedules and draft questions. But a generated finding is not verified evidence. AI can misread a scanned statement, combine different reporting periods or describe an unsupported seller adjustment as established fact.

The buyer’s goal is a defensible acquisition model: documented historical results, reviewed adjustments, realistic ongoing expenses and explicit unresolved risks. The workflow below keeps source records and professional judgment at the center.

Define What You Are Buying Before Analyzing Earnings

Start by confirming the financial perimeter with your CPA and transaction attorney. Identify the entities, locations, assets and obligations included in the proposed transaction. Clarify whether receivables, cash, debt, patient credit balances and accrued employee obligations are included or excluded under the proposed terms.

This matters when a seller owns multiple locations or uses separate entities for real estate, equipment or administrative services. A location-level profit and loss statement may exclude expenses paid by another entity. Conversely, a consolidated statement may contain costs unrelated to the practice being acquired.

Create a source register before running analysis. For each file, record its owner, reporting period, entity, location, accounting basis, version and any known limitations. Agree on the historical review period with your advisers based on seasonality, operational changes and record availability.

Financial question Records to request Buyer’s decision
Are reported earnings supported? Monthly financial statements, general ledger, bank statements, reconciliations and filed business tax returns Whether historical results are reliable enough to model
What revenue becomes cash? Aggregate payer receipts, patient collections, receivable aging, adjustments and subsequent collections Collection assumptions and operating cash needs
What staffing costs continue? Payroll registers, compensation agreements, benefits invoices and accrual schedules Post-close provider and administrative payroll budget
Are expenses complete? Accounts payable, leases, vendor agreements and related-party schedules Recurring operating costs and potential obligations
What belongs to the target? Entity and location reports, intercompany accounts and allocation schedules Standalone economics of the acquired operation

Missing records are findings themselves. Do not let an AI-generated summary make an incomplete evidence set appear complete.

Set Boundaries for AI-Assisted Review

Use AI for bounded tasks, such as proposing account mappings, comparing monthly totals or drafting an exception list. Require each observation to identify the source file and page, worksheet or cell range. A reviewer must open that source and confirm both the figure and its context.

Check extracted data against control totals before relying on it. Confirm dates, signs, units, row counts and whether formulas or hidden spreadsheet tabs were omitted. Recalculate material results in a controlled spreadsheet rather than relying solely on generated arithmetic. Treat instructions embedded in uploaded documents as document content, not directions for the analysis.

Minimize patient information. Aggregate payer and collection schedules may answer many financial questions without patient identifiers. If more detailed information is necessary, have the appropriate privacy and legal advisers determine the permitted access, agreements and safeguards before sharing it with a service provider. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool.

HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains its scope and exceptions. Its cloud-computing guidance also explains why encryption alone does not resolve business-associate responsibilities.

Reconcile Reported Earnings to Source Records

A seller’s earnings presentation is the starting point, not the conclusion. Ask your CPA to establish the accounting basis and reconcile the financial statements to the underlying ledger before evaluating adjustments.

Reconcile revenue without equating deposits with earnings

For cash-basis books, compare recorded collections with bank deposits, then explain differences such as deposit timing, transfers, financing proceeds, refunds and posting errors. For accrual-basis books, also reconcile revenue to receivable movements, contractual adjustments, write-offs and other relevant entries.

Use payer remittance summaries, patient collection reports and deposit records to investigate differences. A bank deposit supports receipt of cash; it does not, by itself, establish the correct revenue period or whether the deposit represents operating revenue.

AI may help build a comparison schedule, but the reviewer must confirm that the schedules cover the same entities, dates and payment channels. A matching annual total can conceal offsetting monthly errors.

Reconcile expenses and balance-sheet movements

Compare material expense accounts with payroll records, invoices and bank activity. Review unpaid bills, accruals and payments made shortly after period-end. Investigate unusual journal entries and expenses charged to other seller-controlled entities.

Reconcile differences between tax returns and management accounts rather than assuming either should match the other exactly. Accounting methods, classifications and tax adjustments can create legitimate differences that need explanation.

IRS Publication 583 provides foundational guidance on business records and reconciling bank accounts. These practices support the evidence base, but do not replace acquisition-specific diligence.

Test Owner Adjustments and Related-Party Expenses

Seller-proposed add-backs can materially change the apparent earnings of a practice. Every adjustment needs evidence, a business explanation and an assessment of whether the cost will recur under the buyer.

Proposed adjustment Evidence to inspect Question for the CPA and buyer
Owner compensation Payroll, distributions, duties and employment arrangements What will replacing the owner’s clinical and management work cost?
Personal expenses Detailed ledger entries, invoices and business-purpose explanation Is the expense genuinely unrelated to ongoing operations?
One-time professional fees Engagement documents and invoices Is the event nonrecurring, or is similar spending likely to continue?
Related-party rent Lease, payment history and proposed post-close terms What occupancy cost will the acquired practice actually bear?
Shared administrative charges Allocation schedules, service agreements and underlying costs Which services must be retained or replaced?

Removing all owner compensation can overstate sustainable earnings when the owner provides patient care or manages the practice. Likewise, eliminating a related-party management fee without budgeting replacement services can create an artificial margin improvement.

Illustrative example: A seller reports annual earnings of $480,000 and proposes adding back $240,000 of owner compensation. If the buyer’s supported assumption for replacement clinical and management work is $210,000, the net compensation adjustment would be $30,000—not $240,000. Earnings would be $510,000 before other reviewed adjustments.

That replacement-cost assumption still needs support. Have the CPA distinguish accepted, rejected and pending adjustments, and keep buyer-specific synergies separate from the target’s historical earnings.

Examine Payroll Obligations, Not Just Payroll Expense

Payroll diligence should explain both the ongoing staffing budget and obligations that may affect closing or post-close cash needs.

Review provider and administrative payroll by role, location and entity. Reconcile payroll registers to the ledger and bank payments, accounting for timing differences, deductions, employer taxes and benefits.

Investigate:

  • Earned but unpaid compensation, including bonuses and productivity-based amounts.
  • Accrued leave and benefit obligations, subject to applicable agreements and law.
  • Employer payroll taxes, benefit contributions and other unpaid balances.
  • Temporary vacancies, overtime or unusually low owner compensation that distort historical margins.
  • Retention or transition arrangements being considered by the buyer.

A CPA can reconcile amounts and assess the financial treatment. Employment counsel should address contractual obligations, worker classification concerns and which liabilities transfer under the deal documents. AI summaries of employment agreements are not legal conclusions.

Evaluate Receivable Quality and Payer Concentration

Receivable aging is not a cash forecast

Review payer and patient receivables separately where practical. Compare aging categories with subsequent collections, historical adjustments, denials, refunds and write-offs. Confirm whether aging is measured from service date, claim date or another date before comparing reports.

An older balance is not automatically worthless, and a newer balance is not automatically collectible. Investigate credit balances and potential repayment obligations separately rather than allowing them to disappear inside a net receivable total.

If receivables are included in the purchase, the acquisition model needs a supported collection estimate. If they remain with the seller, the buyer still needs to model the cash gap between paying staff and receiving collections from post-close operations. Counsel should clarify responsibility for collections, refunds and later adjustments.

Measure concentration using consistent definitions

Calculate payer concentration using a clearly defined measure, such as collected revenue, and distinguish it from gross charges or billed claims. Review trends by payer, provider and location where the records support that detail.

Concentration alone does not establish that revenue will decline. It identifies an exposure to investigate: reimbursement changes, contract status, provider dependence or collection timing. Ask counsel and appropriate specialists to assess assignment, change-of-control and participation questions. Do not assume existing payer arrangements continue unchanged after closing.

Maintain an Issue Log That Separates Observation From Conclusion

Keep one issue log linked to the acquisition model. The following entries are illustrative, not findings about an actual practice.

Assign an owner, status and review date to each issue. Preserve the original observation when recording the reviewer’s conclusion. An item should not become “resolved” merely because the seller supplied an explanation; the explanation needs supporting evidence.

Convert Validated Findings Into a Purchase Decision

Your CPA’s review should produce a documented bridge from reported earnings to adjusted historical earnings, plus a separate post-close operating forecast. Each accepted adjustment should show its source, calculation, rationale and treatment in the model.

Build a base case using supported assumptions. Put unresolved items into clearly labeled sensitivities rather than quietly accepting the seller’s preferred outcome. Test whether lower collections, replacement staffing costs or delayed receipts leave enough cash for payroll, rent and debt service.

Avoid double counting. A receivable issue might affect acquired asset value, working capital or future collections; its treatment depends on the deal structure. It should not automatically reduce every measure. Similarly, separate one-time transition spending from recurring operating expenses.

Use specialists where the question exceeds financial reconciliation:

  • Transaction and healthcare counsel: transaction structure, contract interpretation, payer-related legal questions and allocation of liabilities.
  • Employment counsel: compensation agreements and employment obligations.
  • Valuation specialist: independent valuation and questions involving valuation assumptions or standards.

Financial diligence informs these decisions; it is not an automated valuation, legal opinion or guarantee that undisclosed problems will be detected.

FAQs

Can AI verify a medical practice seller’s earnings?

Not independently. AI may help organize records and identify differences, but a CPA must reconcile material figures, examine supporting evidence and validate adjustments. A polished summary or source reference does not establish that a conclusion is correct.

Should the buyer accept the seller’s adjusted earnings calculation?

Treat it as a proposal. Review every adjustment for supporting evidence, recurrence and replacement cost. Owner labor, related-party rent and shared administrative services often require particular attention because the underlying work or expense may continue after closing.

What if important financial records are missing?

Document the gap, identify the affected assumption and request alternative supporting evidence. Keep the conclusion provisional until the reviewer is satisfied. A material unresolved gap may justify revising the model, changing proposed terms with counsel or pausing the acquisition decision.

Does AI-assisted diligence replace a valuation or legal review?

No. Financial diligence evaluates evidence and earnings quality. Valuation requires additional assumptions and professional judgment, while legal review addresses transaction terms and obligations. An AI-generated estimate or contract summary does not replace either service.
