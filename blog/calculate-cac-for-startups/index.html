Calculating Patient Acquisition Cost for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can generate more inquiries and still spend more acquiring new patients than their visits contribute financially. Advertising reports alone cannot answer whether that spending is sustainable. Owners need to connect acquisition expenses, completed first visits and the collections associated with those patients.

Patient acquisition cost is the scoped cost of acquiring new patients divided by the number of new patients who complete a first visit in a matched cohort. It is a management calculation—not a standardized accounting measure—and its usefulness depends on consistent definitions.

For a staffed private practice, the decision is practical: should you maintain a campaign, change its budget, investigate scheduling friction or wait for more complete collection data? This guide provides a calculation and worksheet for making that decision without importing subscription-business benchmarks.

Define What You Are Measuring

Use this formula:

Patient acquisition cost = Marketing and attributable acquisition-administration costs ÷ New patients completing a first visit in the matched cohort

Before collecting numbers, document four boundaries:

  • Practice scope: One location, one entity or the entire practice group.
  • New-patient definition: Generally, someone completing their first visit with the practice—not an existing patient visiting a different office.
  • Acquisition cohort: The campaign or inquiry-origin period whose spending and resulting patients you will follow.
  • Completion window: How long you will allow those inquiries to become completed first visits before closing the cohort.

This internal definition does not need to match a billing code's definition of a new patient. Keep the two concepts separate.

For a multi-location group, count each person once in the group-level calculation. Location reports can show where their first visit occurred, but a transfer between offices should not become another acquisition in the consolidated report.

Why inquiries and bookings are different denominators

An inquiry indicates interest. A booking indicates a scheduled appointment. Neither establishes that a first visit occurred.

Track them as separate operational measures:

Measure Calculation What it helps assess
Cost per inquiry Scoped acquisition costs ÷ attributable inquiries Expense of generating initial interest
Cost per booking Scoped acquisition costs ÷ new-patient bookings Expense of generating scheduled appointments
Patient acquisition cost Scoped acquisition costs ÷ completed first visits Expense of acquiring patients who actually attended

Exclude canceled appointments, no-shows and duplicate records from the completed-first-visit denominator. Include completed visits even if the payer or patient has not paid yet; payment timing belongs in the contribution analysis, not the acquisition count.

Decide Which Costs Belong in the Numerator

Use a written cost policy so that changes in the number reflect operating performance rather than shifting definitions.

Allocate shared administrative work without counting all payroll

A front-desk employee may handle both acquisition and ongoing patient service. Charging the entire salary to acquisition overstates the cost; ignoring inquiry handling understates it.

Use a reasonable time study or another documented activity measure to assign the acquisition portion. Revisit the allocation when responsibilities change. If owner time is significant but not captured in payroll, disclose any estimated management-time cost separately rather than presenting it as a cash expense.

Shared marketing costs also need an allocation rule. Directly assign identifiable campaign costs first, then allocate shared costs using a consistent basis that reflects the work performed. Label estimates as estimates.

Keep broad fixed overhead outside the core measure. You may show a separate fully allocated view, but do not compare it with another period's direct-cost-only result. Acquisition economics must ultimately support the whole practice, which is why the location and entity P&Ls remain essential.

Match Spending With the Patients It Helped Acquire

Dividing this month's marketing bills by this month's completed first visits can combine unrelated activity. Some visits may result from older campaigns, while current campaigns may produce visits later.

Build an inquiry-origin cohort

For each acquisition period, connect the campaign or inquiry source with subsequent first-visit completion. Keep patient-level matching within the practice's approved operational environment; the owner-facing financial worksheet needs aggregate counts and dollars.

Illustrative example: A practice groups inquiries originating from January through March, follows their completed first visits through June and then closes that acquisition cohort. Visits completed after the cutoff are recorded as late conversions rather than silently added to a previously final report.

Choose the completion window using the practice's observed inquiry-to-visit timing. Do not assume that all channels or locations have the same lag. Label newer cohorts as preliminary and compare them with older cohorts at an equivalent stage.

For ongoing brand or website spending that cannot be connected reliably to individual inquiries, use a documented period allocation and disclose the limitation. A rolling practice-wide measure can provide context, but it does not establish precise campaign attribution.

Handle channel overlap honestly

A patient may encounter an advertisement, search for the practice later and finally call after hearing about it elsewhere. No single source field necessarily captures that entire path.

Choose a reporting rule, such as first recorded source or last identifiable source before inquiry. Apply it consistently and retain an unknown-source category. If you use fractional attribution, the weights across channels must total one for each patient.

Never count the same completed first visit in full under multiple channels. Channel totals should reconcile to the practice-wide total. Unknown sources should remain visible rather than being assigned to whichever campaign appears most successful.

Worksheet: Compare Acquisition Cost With Collected Contribution

A low acquisition cost is not automatically financially attractive. The next question is whether collections from the resulting cohort cover service-delivery costs and acquisition spending.

Use these calculations:

Collected contribution before acquisition = Cohort collections, net of refunds and recoupments, minus attributable service-delivery costs

Contribution after acquisition = Collected contribution before acquisition minus acquisition costs

This is a management contribution measure, not net profit. Define whether service-delivery costs include only incremental costs or also allocated provider and staff compensation. Apply the same method across channels and do not deduct the same administrative cost in both acquisition and service delivery.

Keep collections and service costs aligned

Use payer receipts and patient payments for the cohort's included services, net of refunds and recoupments. Do not substitute billed charges for collections. Outstanding patient balances and unpaid claims are not collected cash.

Include attributable delivery costs for all included visits, even when payment remains outstanding. Otherwise, unpaid visits could disappear from the cost side and make the result look better than it is.

If you later include subsequent visits, identify that broader service scope and give each cohort the same follow-up window. Do not compare a mature cohort's extended collections with a newer cohort's first-visit-only receipts.

This contribution worksheet is not a complete cash-flow statement. Collections can lag payroll and advertising payments, so review payment timing separately before increasing spending.

Turn the Calculation Into a Budget Decision

Use the result to identify the next action rather than applying a universal acquisition-cost target.

  • High inquiry volume but few completed visits: Review duplicate inquiries, appointment availability, scheduling follow-through and cancellations before buying more traffic.
  • Low acquisition cost but weak contribution: Check collections maturity, service-delivery costs and whether unpaid balances explain the gap.
  • Positive contribution with limited capacity: Assess available appointments and staffing costs before expanding a campaign. Additional volume may require step-up costs that the historical worksheet does not capture.
  • Different results by location: Compare local spending, completion timing and delivery costs. Do not assume one office's economics apply to another.
  • Small or immature cohorts: Gather more evidence before making a large budget shift. A few visits can materially change the calculation.

For multiple entities, assign expenses to the appropriate entity and document shared-cost allocations. Reconcile consolidated spending so intercompany charges do not duplicate the same underlying expense.

Keep Acquisition Cost Separate From Other Metrics

Patient acquisition cost answers what it cost to generate completed first visits. It does not measure retention, forecast future bookings or establish lifetime value.

Lifetime-value analysis requires additional assumptions about future activity, collections, costs and the period being modeled. A subscription-business lifetime-value-to-acquisition-cost ratio is not a default target for a medical practice.

Likewise, inquiry and booking forecasts are prospective planning tools. Keep those estimates separate from completed-visit results and realized collections. Financial analysis should assess spending sustainability, not determine clinical recommendations or assume that additional visits are needed.

Establish a Repeatable Monthly Review

Assign responsibility for three inputs: finance reconciles acquisition spending, operations validates aggregate first-visit counts, and the collections team supplies cohort receipts. Review new cohorts as preliminary and update older cohorts until their measurement windows close.

Retain invoices, allocation schedules and reconciliation support. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping provides a foundation for complete business records and bank reconciliation; the cohort worksheet supplements those records rather than replacing them.

Use aggregate reporting wherever possible. Follow the practice's privacy procedures when matching operational records, and avoid putting patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. HHS guidance on minimum necessary information explains the standard and its exceptions; specific workflows require appropriate review.

FAQs

What is a good patient acquisition cost for a medical practice?

There is no universal dollar target. Evaluate acquisition cost against collected contribution over a defined observation window, payment timing, available capacity and the fixed expenses the practice still needs to cover. Compare channels only when their cost definitions and cohort maturity are comparable.

Should unpaid first visits count as acquired patients?

Yes, if the patient completed a first visit and meets your documented new-patient definition. Count the completed visit in the acquisition denominator. Separately report collections actually received and outstanding balances when assessing contribution.

What if a campaign produces no completed first visits?

Report the acquisition spending and zero completed visits. Acquisition cost per patient is undefined—not zero. First check whether the cohort is still immature or the tracking is incomplete. If the completion window has closed, the spending produced no observed completed first visits under your measurement rules.

Should I calculate acquisition cost monthly or quarterly?

Review spending monthly, but choose cohort and completion windows that reflect actual inquiry-to-visit timing. A quarterly cohort may reduce volatility, while a monthly cohort can reveal changes sooner. Neither fixes mismatched attribution by itself; label preliminary results and compare cohorts at equivalent maturity.
