AI and Recency Bias in Medical Practice Treasury Decisions

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A strong collections month can make a new equipment deposit feel affordable. A recent rise in interest rates can make moving operating reserves seem urgent. A market rally can make cash held for payroll feel unproductive.

These are different decisions, but they share a behavioral risk: recency bias, or giving the latest experience more weight than it deserves. For a staffed private medical practice, the consequence is not simply a disappointing return. It may be too little accessible cash when provider and administrative payroll, rent and vendor payments come due.

AI can help organize historical comparisons and question the reasoning behind a treasury decision. It cannot make recent results representative, establish an appropriate reserve by itself or guarantee an unbiased recommendation.

The practical goal is to separate three questions: What changed recently? Does that change justify a new assumption? And can the practice still meet its obligations if that assumption is wrong?

Where Recency Bias Enters Practice Treasury Decisions

Treasury decisions concern how the practice holds, protects and deploys cash. They include setting operating reserves, matching cash availability to payment dates and deciding when to commit funds to equipment, renovations or another location.

Recency bias can push an owner toward either excessive confidence or excessive caution.

Recent event Recency-driven interpretation Better question before acting
An unusually strong collections month “This is our new normal, so we can release reserves.” How much came from older payer balances rather than repeatable current activity?
A weak collections month “Receipts are deteriorating, so every capital project must stop.” Is this a timing disruption, a seasonal pattern or evidence of a lasting change?
A rise in available cash yields “We should move most of our cash now.” What access restrictions, terms and risks would apply when payroll is due?
Strong recent market returns “Keeping reserves in cash is wasting money.” Is this money available for investment risk, or already needed for operations?
A recent rate decline “Financing will keep getting cheaper, so we should wait.” Does delaying the commitment make operational and liquidity sense without a rate forecast?

A recent event can be meaningful. A new provider, changed payer arrangement or permanent expense reduction may justify revising assumptions. The mistake is treating the latest result as durable without investigating its cause.

This article focuses on that single bias. It is not a broader behavioral-pattern assessment or a general cash-forecasting guide. A cash forecast supplies payment and receipt estimates; the recency-bias review challenges why those estimates and the proposed decision seem credible.

Compare Observation Windows Before Changing Reserve Policy

Do not choose a historical window solely because it supports the desired purchase or transfer. Establish the comparison before reviewing the recommendation.

For an established practice, a useful review can include the latest month, a recent rolling quarter, a trailing year and comparable seasonal periods. These are comparison tools, not universal requirements. A newer practice may have less history and should make that limitation explicit.

Use consistent definitions across periods. Cash collections are not charges, and transfers between practice accounts are not receipts from patients or payers. Borrowing and owner contributions should also be separated from operating collections.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. That foundation matters here: an AI summary of unreconciled data can make a timing error look like a trend.

Explain differences rather than automatically averaging them

A longer period is not automatically more accurate. Historical collections from a smaller practice may understate current capacity after a provider joins. Conversely, recent collections may overstate ongoing receipts after an old receivables backlog is resolved.

Ask the finance team to distinguish:

  • Timing effects: delayed payer batches, accelerated patient-balance receipts or payments crossing month-end.
  • Nonrecurring items: financing proceeds, owner contributions or unusual refunds.
  • Structural changes: added clinical capacity, changed staffing costs or a location opening.
  • Unexplained differences: items requiring investigation rather than an optimistic assumption.

For multi-location owners, compare both consolidated and location-level results. One site's strong collections can conceal another site's recurring cash needs. For multi-entity owners, identify which entity holds the money and which owes the bills; a consolidated balance alone does not establish that funds are available for every entity's obligations.

Keep Market Performance Separate From Operating Liquidity

Recent market gains do not change the date payroll must be funded. Likewise, an attractive quoted yield does not establish that a cash arrangement fits the practice's access needs.

Before changing where reserves are held, document:

  • When the money may be needed, including overlapping payroll and vendor dates.
  • Whether its value can fluctuate before that date.
  • Withdrawal, settlement, maturity or early-access terms.
  • Current account terms and any protections or limitations, verified with the institution or adviser.
  • Who can authorize transfers and how operating accounts will be replenished.

Do not assume that the latest return or interest rate will continue. When comparing alternatives, distinguish contractual terms from uncertain future returns. Money needed for near-term obligations should not depend on a favorable market exit to become available.

This is a liquidity and risk review, not a recommendation to buy securities or a forecast of market direction. If a proposal introduces investment risk, review its suitability with an appropriately qualified adviser separately from the operating-cash decision.

A Treasury Decision Memo That Challenges the Recent Story

A short written memo makes assumptions visible before money moves. It should name the proposed action, compare observation windows, list upcoming obligations and show a downside liquidity case.

Illustrative example only: All amounts and circumstances below are invented to demonstrate the workflow. They are not practice benchmarks, a customer case study or a recommended reserve level.

A staffed practice is considering a $120,000 equipment deposit after a strong collections month. The owner believes recent receipts show enough capacity to proceed.

1. Compare the evidence windows

Observation window Illustrative collections What the review finds
Latest month $330,000 Includes a substantial release of older payer balances; not all receipts reflect repeatable monthly activity.
Latest three months $280,000 monthly average Stronger than the longer-period average, but includes the unusually strong month.
Trailing twelve months $240,000 monthly average A broader reference point that includes slower periods.
Comparable prior-year quarter $230,000 monthly average Helps assess seasonality, but needs adjustment for current staffing and capacity.

The memo should not simply declare the twelve-month figure correct. It should explain why a particular assumption is selected and what evidence would justify changing it.

2. Identify accessible cash and upcoming obligations

In this illustrative example, the practice has $600,000 of unrestricted, accessible operating cash. Its next three months of planned outflows are:

Obligation Illustrative three-month cash outflow Review point
Provider and administrative payroll, related taxes and benefits $450,000 Verify payment dates, not just monthly expense totals.
Occupancy and utilities $120,000 Include all operating locations.
Supplies and recurring vendors $90,000 Separate existing commitments from discretionary purchases.
Debt service $60,000 Use scheduled cash payments.
Other scheduled payments $60,000 Identify the underlying items and avoid double counting.
Total before proposed equipment deposit $780,000 Excludes the proposed $120,000 deposit.

The owner has selected an illustrative minimum liquidity floor of $300,000 for this decision. An actual practice should set its floor using its payment cycle, collections variability, obligations and access to funding—not this example's amount.

3. Test the proposed commitment against a downside case

Three-month liquidity bridge Base case Downside case
Opening accessible cash $600,000 $600,000
Collections during the period $720,000 $570,000
Planned operating and scheduled outflows ($780,000) ($780,000)
Proposed equipment deposit ($120,000) ($120,000)
Ending cash $420,000 $270,000
Difference from illustrative $300,000 floor $120,000 above $30,000 below

The base case uses the trailing-year monthly collections average. The downside case assumes slower receipts during the decision period; it is a stress assumption, not a prediction or probability estimate.

The memo also needs a weekly payment schedule. An acceptable ending balance can hide an interim shortage if receipts arrive after payroll. Do not count uncertain receipts as available before they clear.

4. Record the decision and its conditions

In this example, the deposit passes the base case but breaches the selected floor in the downside case. Deferring the deposit would leave $390,000 at the end of that downside scenario, before considering interim timing.

Possible actions include deferring the commitment, negotiating a different payment schedule or reducing the initial scope. None should be treated as available until the terms are confirmed.

Record the approver, the reason for the decision and the evidence needed for reconsideration. That evidence might include confirmed receipt of delayed payer funds or a revised vendor schedule. “Another good month” is not enough unless the memo explains why it changes the underlying economics.

How AI Can Help Without Becoming the Decision-Maker

AI can assist with this review when the finance team supplies defined, reconciled inputs. It can organize monthly comparisons, draft explanations for differences and generate questions that challenge the owner's preferred narrative.

Useful tasks include:

  1. Compare windows consistently. Request the same measures for each period, with account definitions and exclusions stated.
  2. Separate facts from assumptions. Require the draft memo to distinguish recorded receipts, confirmed commitments and hypothetical scenarios.
  3. Challenge both directions. Ask why the recent improvement might persist and why it might not. Apply the same test after a weak month.
  4. Identify missing evidence. Ask which conclusion depends on unverified payer timing, account access or vendor terms.
  5. Check the decision rule. Compare the proposed action with the practice's approved liquidity floor and payment schedule.

A useful prompt is:

Using these reconciled, aggregated monthly figures, compare the latest month, recent quarter and trailing year. Identify where the proposed equipment commitment relies on recent results continuing. Draft reasons for and against that assumption, list missing evidence and show a downside liquidity bridge. Do not invent transactions, forecast market returns or recommend securities.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary Slack messages and general-purpose AI tools. Aggregated financial information is usually sufficient for this analysis. Where protected health information is involved, use an appropriately reviewed workflow; HHS privacy guidance explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities.

How AI Can Amplify Recency Bias

An AI-generated memo can sound balanced while relying almost entirely on recent data. That can happen when the user uploads only the latest quarter or asks, “Explain why we can afford this purchase.” The framing already favors a conclusion.

Other failure points include summarizing a recent market rally as evidence of future returns, overlooking an older seasonal shortfall or treating a one-time payer release as recurring revenue.

Require human review of source periods, calculations, exclusions and account availability. Ask the reviewer to identify what would reverse the recommendation. AI output is an analytical draft, not proof that bias has been removed.

Put the Review Into the Approval Workflow

Use this memo before material reserve transfers, discretionary distributions or capital commitments—not only when markets are volatile. Keep the original memo so later results can be compared with the assumptions actually used, rather than a revised memory of the decision.

FAQs

Does recency bias mean we should ignore a strong collections month?

No. Investigate what produced it. Separate recurring activity from older payer balances, timing shifts and nonoperating inflows. A strong month may justify revising assumptions, but the explanation matters more than the headline total.

How much history should a medical practice compare?

Use enough history to expose relevant variability and seasonality, while accounting for changes in practice size and operations. Comparing the latest month, recent quarter, trailing year and comparable seasonal period is a useful starting point when those records exist. No single window is automatically correct.

Can AI decide how much operating reserve the practice needs?

AI can organize inputs and test scenarios, but it should not set the reserve independently. The owner and finance team need to evaluate payment timing, collections variability, account access and commitments. Verify the calculations and approve the decision through the practice's normal authority structure.

Should recent market returns influence where payroll reserves are held?

Recent returns alone are not a sound basis for changing payroll reserves. Review when cash is needed, whether its value can fluctuate and whether access terms match payment obligations. Evaluate investment risk separately rather than assuming recent gains will continue.
