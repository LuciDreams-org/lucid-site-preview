Combining Fraud Detection Models in Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A vendor-payment change or unexpected payroll adjustment deserves attention, but unusual activity is not proof of fraud. For a staffed private medical practice, the useful question is whether a detection method helps the owner find transactions that need independent verification—without overwhelming the people responsible for reviewing them.

Supervised and unsupervised models answer different questions. A supervised model learns from reliably classified historical examples, including confirmed fraud and legitimate activity. An unsupervised model identifies activity that differs from the patterns in its data, without requiring confirmed fraud labels.

Combining them may be useful when a practice has some reliable case history but also needs to identify unfamiliar patterns. It is not automatically the right investment. A smaller practice with limited history may get more value from reconciliations, approval controls, and a manageable exception report before adding statistical models.

This guide explains how to choose complementary methods for vendor payments and payroll changes, establish a review workflow, and distinguish an alert from a confirmed finding.

Start with the financial decision, not the model

Define what the practice wants reviewers to examine. “Find fraud” is too broad to guide model selection or measure usefulness.

Better starting questions include:

  • Which vendor payments need verification before release because payment instructions recently changed?
  • Which payroll adjustments differ from an employee’s approved compensation or normal pay pattern?
  • Which transactions require review because they combine unusual amounts, unfamiliar counterparties, or missing approvals?

Keep the scope narrow enough that a reviewer can obtain supporting records and reach a documented conclusion. Vendor payments and payroll changes usually need separate baselines because they follow different schedules, authorization processes, and accounting treatments.

Detection also depends on reliable books. Duplicate imports, inconsistent vendor names, and incorrectly assigned locations can create apparent anomalies that are really data problems. The IRS guidance on business records supports complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide a foundation for analysis; they do not establish that a model can detect fraud.

A supervised score is not necessarily a reliable probability of fraud unless it has been appropriately calibrated and validated. An anomaly score is not a fraud probability at all. Keep their meanings separate when presenting them to reviewers.

Sparse labels: the main constraint on supervised learning

A practice may have years of transactions but very few confirmed fraud cases. Transaction volume alone does not make a suitable supervised training set.

Historical labels may also be incomplete. A corrected duplicate payment is an accounting error unless an investigation establishes otherwise. An unreviewed transaction is not automatically legitimate, and a suspicious transaction is not automatically fraudulent.

Before selecting a supervised model, ask a qualified analyst to assess whether the available examples are sufficient, representative, and reliably classified. If not, begin with controls, exception reporting, and possibly anomaly detection. Do not manufacture fraud labels to make training possible.

Changing patterns: why anomaly detection can help—and mislead

A new location, payroll-system transition, provider hire, or annual insurance payment can change normal financial activity. An anomaly model may identify that change without understanding its business purpose.

Use comparison groups that make operational sense. Salaried administrative staff should not automatically share a payroll baseline with providers whose compensation varies. Likewise, a new location’s purchasing activity may differ legitimately from an established office’s spending.

Anomaly detection is useful when the practice can investigate unfamiliar activity. Without that capacity, it can become an expensive list of unexplained exceptions.

Model-selection decision tree

Use this sequence before committing to a combined approach:

  1. Are the underlying records reconciled and consistently identified? If no, repair vendor, employee, entity, location, and transaction records first. If yes, continue.
  2. Can a reviewer explain the decision and obtain supporting evidence? If no, narrow the use case. A model should not produce accusations that nobody can meaningfully verify.
  3. Are reliable confirmed-fraud and legitimate examples available? If no, favor rules and, where useful, an anomaly-detection pilot. If yes, evaluate a supervised model against the existing process.
  4. Does unfamiliar activity remain a meaningful blind spot? If yes, test an unsupervised method alongside the supervised model. If no, a second model may add little value.
  5. Does the second method identify useful additional cases within review capacity? If no, simplify the approach or revise its scope. If yes, pilot a combined review queue.
  6. Are ownership, independent verification, escalation, and monitoring defined? If no, resolve governance before operational use. If yes, proceed with a controlled rollout.

The objective is not to deploy two models. It is to demonstrate that each method contributes useful information beyond existing controls.

Apply the methods to vendor payments and payroll changes

Vendor-payment example

Illustrative example: A practice receives a $9,600 supply invoice shortly after a vendor’s payment instructions change. The invoice amount is consistent with past purchases, so an amount-based anomaly model may see little unusual activity. A supervised model might prioritize it if the combination of timing and change history resembles reliably labeled prior cases.

A different payment might have no resemblance to known cases but be unusual for its location, vendor category, and payment schedule. That is where anomaly detection could add a separate review signal.

Neither result establishes wrongdoing. The reviewer should inspect the invoice, purchase authorization, vendor-change request, and payment history. Verify changed instructions through an established contact method independent of the change request—not simply by replying to the message requesting the change.

Payroll-change example

Illustrative example: An administrator’s gross pay rises from $2,400 to $3,200 for one pay period. An anomaly model flags the increase. Review finds an approved bonus and documented additional hours, so the alert is resolved as legitimate.

In another case, an unusual adjustment lacks an approved change record. That is a reason to investigate, not a confirmed fraud label. A payroll preparer’s explanation should be checked against independently authorized compensation records and underlying payroll documentation.

Detection should support timely verification without making unsupported changes to earned pay. Any payroll action must follow established procedures and applicable requirements, with appropriate professional advice when necessary.

Combine signals without hiding their meaning

For an initial pilot, a transparent routing matrix may be easier to govern than a single blended score. Scores from different models are not automatically comparable and should not be averaged without validation.

Supervised signal Anomaly signal Suggested review treatment
Elevated Elevated Prioritize independent review; agreement is still not proof
Elevated Not elevated Examine the known-pattern reason and whether it remains relevant
Not elevated Elevated Investigate business context and possible unfamiliar activity
Not elevated Not elevated Continue ordinary controls and sample some transactions for oversight

Missing data should appear as its own condition, not as a reassuring low score. For example, an unavailable vendor-change history means the related assessment is incomplete.

Existing approval requirements remain in force regardless of model output. A low score should not waive authorization, and a high score should not automatically trigger an accusation or adverse employment action.

Use a triage worksheet that separates signal from finding

The review record should distinguish what was flagged, what evidence was examined, and what was established. Preserve the original alert even if later evidence explains it.

Assign a review owner, due time, and escalation contact. Record who approved the conclusion and when supporting evidence became available. If a suspected issue involves the usual reviewer, route it to an independent owner or adviser under the practice’s established escalation procedure.

Correcting an error, confirming fraud, and deciding on accounting treatment are separate decisions. The finance team should document supported adjustments rather than recording a fraud loss merely because a model generated an alert.

Validate against workload and changing operations

Test on chronological data: develop the method using older records, tune it on a later period, and evaluate it on a separate, newer period. Use only information available at the original decision time. A later investigation outcome can help classify a historical case, but it cannot be treated as an input that existed before the investigation.

Start in observation-only mode without changing payment or payroll decisions. Compare the current process, each model separately, and the combined queue.

Illustrative example: If an office manager has capacity for six documented reviews each week, a queue generating thirty alerts is not operationally workable. Narrow the scope, improve context, adjust thresholds, or assign more review capacity. Do not silently discard overflow.

Review model behavior after acquisitions, location openings, compensation changes, or accounting migrations. A sudden increase in alerts may reflect changed operations or broken data—not increased fraud. Document threshold changes and retain a way to return to the prior review process if performance deteriorates.

Establish ownership and information boundaries

Name the person responsible for data quality, model evaluation, transaction review, escalation, and approving changes. The person who initiates a vendor or payroll change should not be its sole verifier. Where staffing makes separation difficult, arrange independent owner or adviser review.

Use financial identifiers and aggregated information where possible. Vendor-payment and payroll analysis generally should not require patient-level clinical information. HHS explains the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions; assess the applicable obligations before sharing health information with any service provider. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Conclusion

Combine supervised and unsupervised methods when reliable historical labels and unfamiliar-pattern detection each contribute useful information. Keep simpler controls when they meet the practice’s needs more effectively.

The governing principle remains the same: an alert requests review; evidence supports a finding. Independent verification, documented decisions, and established escalation procedures matter more than the number of models deployed.

FAQs

Does a medical practice need both detection methods?

No. Start with reconciled records, clear approvals, and defined review responsibilities. Add a second method only if testing shows useful additional findings within the practice’s review capacity.

Can supervised learning work with very few confirmed fraud cases?

It may not be reliable enough for the intended use. Have a qualified analyst assess label quality and representativeness. Rules and anomaly detection may be more practical while the practice builds a documented history of review outcomes.

Does an anomaly mean an employee or vendor committed fraud?

No. Bonuses, new locations, payment timing, data errors, and approved purchasing changes can all create anomalies. Verify the underlying records independently and follow established escalation procedures before reaching a conclusion.

Should every reviewer decision become a training label?

No. Separate confirmed legitimate activity, accounting errors, confirmed fraud, and unresolved cases. Record the evidence and resolution date. Use only appropriately verified labels for supervised training, and do not treat an unreviewed transaction as confirmed legitimate activity.
