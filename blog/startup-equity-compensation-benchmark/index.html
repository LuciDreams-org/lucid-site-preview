Benchmarking Equity Compensation in Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

An equity-linked offer can mean very different things: ownership in a practice entity, an opportunity to purchase ownership later, or a contractual payment tied to business value. A headline percentage does not tell an owner or candidate which economic package is better.

For a staffed US private medical practice, useful benchmarking starts with a narrower question: What does this person receive, what must they contribute, and under what conditions can they realize value? Compare those answers alongside salary, incentives, benefits and responsibilities before discussing proposed terms with valuation, legal and tax advisers.

This guide provides a comparison workflow—not market ownership percentages or a recommendation about who should receive equity. Ownership restrictions, permitted arrangements and tax treatment require case-specific professional advice.

Why Startup Option Percentages Are Not Practice Benchmarks

Startup compensation reports often describe options as a percentage of fully diluted shares. Those observations reflect particular company populations, financing structures, roles and award terms. They do not establish an appropriate ownership stake for a physician joining a private practice or an administrator managing several locations.

A practice comparison must account for its own economics and structure:

  • Whether the arrangement concerns the operating practice, a management entity or another specifically identified entity.
  • Whether compensation rewards clinical work, administrative leadership, capital investment or some combination.
  • Whether value depends on ongoing distributions, a future purchase, a sale or a contractual settlement.
  • Whether the recipient contributes cash, accepts lower cash compensation or assumes other obligations.
  • Whether the interest includes voting rights, information rights or restrictions on transfer.

Even two offers described as “equity” can have different outcomes. One may provide a purchased ownership interest with potential distributions. Another may provide only a contingent cash payment if a defined event occurs.

Benchmark the total economic package and its conditions, not the percentage in isolation.

1. Identify What Is Actually Being Compared

Before collecting comparable offers, describe the proposed arrangement in plain language. Ask counsel to confirm the description and identify any eligibility or ownership restrictions before treating it as an available choice.

Arrangement category What the comparison should establish What not to assume
Actual ownership Entity, class or type of interest, economic rights, purchase or award terms, governance rights and exit provisions An ownership percentage automatically provides equal voting rights, distributions or sale proceeds
Opportunity to purchase ownership Conditions for the opportunity, purchase timing, pricing method, financing terms and required approvals A future purchase opportunity is current ownership or has a guaranteed purchase price
Contractual value-linked compensation Payment formula, baseline value, covered entity, conditions, settlement timing and payment limits A value-linked contract conveys ownership or voting rights

These categories are a way to organize the comparison, not a menu of legally interchangeable instruments. A contractual arrangement without issued ownership can still raise legal, tax and accounting questions. Its label alone does not determine its treatment.

For a multi-entity group, identify exactly which business is included. Participation in one location's operating entity is not equivalent to participation in the entire group. Real estate, equipment ownership and shared-service entities should not be assumed to be included.

2. Build a Comparable Role and Cash-Pay Baseline

Start with the work being compensated. A physician with patient-care duties and limited committee work is not directly comparable to a physician who also leads recruitment, supervises operations and manages a location budget. Similarly, a practice administrator responsible for one office differs from an executive managing several entities.

Document responsibilities rather than relying on titles. Separate compensation for services from returns on invested capital, even when the same person receives both.

Record:

  • Clinical and administrative responsibilities, where applicable.
  • Expected work commitment and management authority.
  • Base compensation, incentive formulas and whether incentives are guaranteed or contingent.
  • Benefits, employer contributions and other material compensation costs.
  • Capital contributions, purchase payments and financing obligations.
  • Any proposed reduction in cash compensation associated with the arrangement.

Keep the employee's gross compensation comparison separate from the practice's total cost. Employer payroll costs, benefits and a potential contractual payout affect practice cash flow but are not all take-home compensation.

Evaluate the Quality of Comparable Information

Useful evidence may include documented internal offers, existing ownership agreements and comparable transactions or compensation information reviewed by qualified advisers. Each observation needs context.

Record its date, source, role scope, entity structure, geography, cash compensation and material terms. Distinguish a completed transaction from a proposed offer, an asking price or an informal account. Do not turn a few incomplete observations into an asserted market range.

Where reliable information is unavailable, mark the comparison “Insufficient comparable evidence” and use scenarios to frame questions—not to claim a market benchmark.

3. Use a Medical Practice Equity Comparison Worksheet

Use one column per proposed or observed package. Preserve the original terms alongside your normalized summary, and identify missing information explicitly.

Comparison field What to record Why it matters
Role and scope Clinical duties, leadership duties, work commitment and locations covered Prevents unlike roles from appearing comparable
Evidence and status Source, observation date, signed or proposed status, and missing documents Shows how much confidence to place in the comparison
Cash compensation Base pay, incentive formula, benefits and any cash-pay reduction Establishes the compensation received without relying on uncertain future value
Arrangement and entity Actual ownership, purchase opportunity or contractual value-linked payment; exact entity covered Defines what the recipient may receive
Economic participation Percentage or units, calculation denominator, distribution rights and payment formula Makes the headline figure interpretable
Valuation date and basis Effective date, report reference, equity versus enterprise value, and material assumptions Prevents comparison of values measured on different bases
Vesting or earning conditions Existing proposed conditions, service requirements and treatment on departure Shows when rights are earned or may be lost
Dilution or formula changes Treatment of new owners, new capital, acquisitions and permitted amendments Shows how future changes could affect participation
Buy-in cost Purchase price, required contribution, financing terms and transaction costs Identifies the recipient's capital commitment
Distributions and settlement Conditions, discretion, timing, deferral and payment limits Distinguishes estimated value from available cash
Repurchase provisions Trigger, valuation method, discounts or adjustments, payment timing and dispute process Shows how ownership may be converted back to cash
Governance and transfer Voting, information rights, transfer restrictions and approval requirements Distinguishes economic participation from control
Tax and accounting review Questions, responsible adviser and review status Avoids presenting gross amounts as after-tax value

Record vesting and other earning conditions for comparison; do not use the worksheet to design a schedule. Have advisers resolve ambiguous provisions rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

4. Put Valuation and Financial Records on a Consistent Basis

A percentage needs both a denominator and a dated valuation basis. Ask the valuation adviser what is being measured and how debt, cash, other obligations and ownership rights affect the result. Enterprise value and equity value are not interchangeable.

For a practice, the supporting financial package may include:

  • Reconciled entity-level balance sheets and profit-and-loss statements.
  • Per-location results with a documented shared-cost allocation method.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, including owner compensation.
  • Payer receipts, patient collections and receivables aging in aggregate.
  • Debt balances, leases and material payment obligations.
  • Owner distributions and transactions between related entities.

These records help advisers evaluate the business; they do not independently establish its value. For example, cash receipts can change because of collection timing rather than a change in underlying activity. Likewise, moving shared administrative costs between locations can alter reported location profit without changing group-wide earnings.

The IRS guidance on starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those practices provide a sound financial foundation, but they are not a valuation method.

Use aggregate financial information for the comparison. Avoid including patient-identifiable information in compensation worksheets or sending it through ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

5. Compare Economic Outcomes, Not Just Headline Values

Use a common comparison period and separate recurring pay, capital invested, contingent value and cash actually received. Do not add a multiyear estimated interest value to one year's salary and call the result annual compensation.

Illustrative Example: Three Packages With Different Tradeoffs

The following hypothetical terms are illustrative only. They are not market benchmarks, recommendations or evidence that any arrangement is available to a particular recipient. Assume the same role and benefits across the packages.

Term Package A: Cash compensation Package B: Ownership purchase Package C: Contractual value-linked payment
Annual base compensation $250,000 $230,000 $240,000
Capital required None $100,000 purchase payment None under the hypothetical terms
Additional economics No ownership-linked payment Purchased interest with distribution and repurchase rights defined in an agreement Contingent payment based on a defined change in value
Key uncertainty Future cash-pay changes Business value, distributions and repurchase proceeds Whether payment conditions occur and what the formula produces

Over an illustrative five-year comparison period, Package B has $100,000 less cumulative base compensation than Package A, plus a $100,000 purchase payment. That is a $200,000 difference in gross cash position before distributions, financing costs, taxes, benefits or repurchase proceeds. The purchase payment acquires an asset; it is not simply another salary reduction.

Package C has $50,000 less cumulative base compensation than Package A over the same illustrative period. Its contingent payment must be evaluated separately rather than assumed to make up that difference.

Test each package under several conditions: stable business performance, lower collections with continued payroll obligations, departure before all conditions are satisfied, and a potential sale. Use the actual proposed formulas. Where terms are missing, identify the unanswered question instead of inventing a payout.

6. Review Dilution, Repurchase Terms and Practice Liquidity

For actual ownership, ask how additional ownership interests or capital contributions could change the recipient's percentage and rights. For contractual compensation, ask whether the formula accounts for acquisitions, new locations, owner contributions or changes to shared costs.

Repurchase provisions deserve equal attention. A valuable interest on paper may produce cash slowly if payment is deferred. Compare the valuation date, pricing method, departure triggers, payment schedule and any offsets or adjustments. Have counsel explain how disputed terms would be resolved.

The practice also needs a cash forecast. Model potential purchase proceeds, distributions, contractual settlements and repurchase payments separately from provider and administrative payroll, rent, debt service and other operating commitments. Do not assume an obligation can be funded merely because accounting profit is positive.

This review should expose financial constraints before negotiation—not substitute for legal approval of the arrangement.

7. Assemble the Adviser Review File

Create a dated file containing the worksheet, underlying documents, financial statements, valuation references, scenario assumptions and unresolved questions. Keep observed facts separate from proposed terms and modeled outcomes.

Assign the remaining decisions clearly:

  • Valuation adviser: Assess valuation basis, comparability and the financial implications of specific rights.
  • Legal counsel: Confirm ownership eligibility, applicable restrictions, governing documents, approvals and enforceability.
  • Tax adviser: Evaluate recipient- and entity-specific tax treatment, timing, reporting and withholding questions.
  • Practice finance team: Reconcile inputs and model compensation costs, capital flows and payment capacity.

FAQs

What percentage of equity should a medical practice offer?

There is no defensible percentage to select from startup option data alone. First establish the role, entity, cash package, valuation basis, recipient contribution and exit terms. Then review comparable evidence and legal eligibility with qualified advisers. A percentage without those details is not a meaningful compensation benchmark.

Is a future buy-in opportunity the same as equity compensation?

No. An opportunity to purchase ownership does not itself establish current ownership. Compare its conditions, timing, pricing method, financing requirements and approvals separately from salary and any current ownership rights. Counsel should confirm what the documents actually promise.

Can an administrator receive ownership or value-linked compensation?

Do not assume eligibility based on job title or the name of an arrangement. Applicable ownership restrictions, entity structure and contractual terms require case-specific legal review. A contractual value-linked payment is not automatically a permissible substitute for ownership.

How should we compare offers when reliable market data is limited?

Document the evidence you have and its limitations. Compare role scope, cash compensation, capital required, valuation assumptions, earning conditions and repurchase or settlement terms. Use clearly labeled scenarios to understand tradeoffs, but do not describe modeled results as observed market practice.
