Hidden Legal Costs of Medical Practice Fundraising

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A financing proposal can look affordable before the practice has budgeted the work required to complete it. Document review, negotiations, ownership-record questions and adviser coordination may create separate expenses—and some may become payable even if the financing never closes.

For owners and managers of staffed private medical practices, the practical question is not simply, “What will the lawyer charge?” It is: What work does this transaction require, who will perform it, and when will the practice need cash to pay for it?

Before accepting financing terms, obtain a scoped review from counsel familiar with the proposed transaction and the practice’s circumstances. Then connect that scope to a cash forecast that protects provider and administrative payroll, rent and other operating commitments.

This guide explains how to build that budget. It is a planning framework, not legal advice or a statement of medical-practice ownership requirements. The applicable work depends on the entities, documents, jurisdiction and financing actually involved.

Start with the transaction, not a generic legal allowance

A single budget line labeled “financing legal fees” can conceal several different assignments. An initial quote might cover reviewing a lender’s documents but exclude correcting older ownership records, negotiating an existing lender’s consent or reviewing arrangements involving a separate entity.

Before requesting an estimate, prepare a short transaction summary:

  • Purpose: What will the funds support, such as a new location, equipment or working capital?
  • Parties: Which practice entity is seeking financing, and which other owners or entities might be involved?
  • Existing commitments: What current financing, leases or agreements should counsel examine for possible implications?
  • Available records: Which signed ownership, governance and financing documents are ready for review?
  • Proposed timetable: When does the practice want access to funds, and what spending depends on that date?
  • Open questions: What has the lender or investor requested that the practice cannot yet provide?

Ask counsel to distinguish an initial assessment from the full transaction engagement. If applicability cannot be determined until documents are reviewed, budget that review as a separate decision stage rather than assuming everything is included.

Where additional legal work can arise

The following are potential workstreams, not a checklist of mandatory requirements. Have counsel identify which apply and which do not.

Document review and negotiation

Reviewing a proposal is different from reviewing and negotiating the final financing package. Ask whether the engagement covers the preliminary terms, definitive documents, supporting schedules and revisions requested during negotiations.

Also ask what assumptions support the estimate. A quote based on a defined document package may need revision if additional entities, documents or negotiation rounds enter the process. Do not assume that an estimate is a fixed fee or that a fixed fee covers every related task.

Ownership and governance record cleanup

Counsel may need to reconcile signed records with the practice’s current description of its ownership and decision-making authority. Missing amendments, inconsistent entity names or incomplete records of prior changes can create a separate assignment.

The practice manager can gather records and flag discrepancies. Counsel should determine whether corrective legal work is needed. A finance team’s ownership schedule is useful supporting information, but it is not a substitute for the underlying legal documents.

Consents and third-party coordination

Ask counsel whether existing agreements need review for consent, notice or other transaction-related provisions. Potential review areas could include existing financing, leases or arrangements between related entities.

The budget should distinguish identifying a possible requirement from obtaining the relevant response. Contacting another party, answering its questions and negotiating requested documentation may require work beyond the initial review.

Additional counsel, filings and external expenses

Depending on the transaction, counsel may recommend specialized advice or identify filing and third-party expenses. Ask for these to be listed separately from the primary firm’s fees, with applicability still to be confirmed where necessary.

Similarly, do not assume that the practice must—or will not—pay another party’s legal costs. Have counsel review the proposed expense provisions, including what happens if the transaction is delayed or abandoned.

Illustrative example: an additional entity changes the scope

Illustrative example: A private medical practice operating two locations seeks financing to expand one office. Counsel’s initial estimate assumes that one operating entity will enter the financing and that its existing records are complete.

During document collection, the manager finds that a separate related entity owns equipment listed in the proposed financing materials. An older agreement also identifies that entity differently from the current financial schedules.

Those facts do not establish what the legal solution must be. They create questions counsel must assess: Which entity owns the relevant assets? Does the proposed document package accurately reflect the arrangement? Are additional documents, approvals or negotiations needed?

The owner should request a revised scope before authorizing the additional work:

Original assumption New information Budget response
Only the operating entity needs review A related entity owns relevant equipment Ask counsel to scope any additional entity review
Records agree with the financing schedules An older agreement uses inconsistent information Assign document collection to the manager and legal interpretation to counsel
The initial document package is sufficient The financing party requests additional documentation Obtain an updated deliverable list, estimate and timetable
The expected funding date supports expansion spending Additional review may affect closing readiness Update the cash forecast and reassess spending that depends on financing

The original quote was based on assumptions that changed. The useful control is a written update showing what changed, why more work may be needed and whether the practice wants to proceed.

Build the cash budget around when bills become payable

The total estimated fee is only part of the decision. Timing matters when the practice must meet payroll and operating expenses before financing proceeds arrive.

Maintain a financing-cost schedule alongside the operating cash forecast:

Budget category What to track Owner decision
Approved legal work Estimated total, billed amount and unbilled work reported by counsel Is the remaining scope still affordable?
Conditional additional work Trigger, proposed deliverable and estimate Should this work be authorized now?
Third-party costs Responsible payer, payment trigger and adviser confirmation Must cash be available before closing?
Potential other-party reimbursement Proposed obligation and counsel’s interpretation Is the exposure acceptable before accepting terms?
Delay or abandoned-deal exposure Costs payable without funding Can operating cash absorb a failed or delayed transaction?

Ask counsel for periodic updates on work performed but not yet invoiced. An invoice-only view can understate the amount already incurred.

Keep the forecast clear about timing. Show pre-closing payments separately from amounts expected to be paid at closing. Do not rely on financing proceeds to cover a bill that may become due before those proceeds are available.

For a multi-entity practice, track which entity engages the adviser, receives the invoice and makes the payment. Have the CPA determine the appropriate accounting treatment and review shared-cost allocations. Do not assume that every financing-related expense receives the same tax treatment.

Control scope without interfering with legal judgment

The owner controls spending authorization; counsel determines the legal work needed to advise on the transaction. A workable process respects both roles.

Agree on change approval

Ask the engagement proposal to explain exclusions, the process for revising estimates and who can authorize additional work. Establish a notification point before the agreed budget is exhausted, while recognizing that the engagement terms govern the arrangement.

A scope update should identify the new issue, proposed work, revised estimate, timing implications and any available alternatives. This gives the owner a decision to make rather than an unexplained larger invoice.

Assign one practice coordinator

Use one manager or owner to maintain the document request list and consolidate questions. Separate business approvals, legal questions and accounting questions so each reaches the appropriate adviser.

Maintain a current document set. Mark unsigned drafts clearly, preserve signed versions and identify missing records honestly. Do not have administrative staff attempt to repair legal documents without counsel’s direction.

Prepare financial support without unnecessary patient information

Reconciled entity-level accounts, debt schedules and location-level reporting can help advisers understand the financial facts. The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records; it does not establish the legal requirements for a particular financing.

Use aggregate financial reports where they answer the request. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. If a request appears to require patient-level information, obtain appropriate guidance before sharing it.

Before accepting terms: make the go-or-pause decision

Before proceeding, the owner should have a written initial scope, identified exclusions, a payment schedule and a view of costs payable if funding does not occur. Unresolved items should have a named adviser and a next step.

Then test whether the practice can support the transaction work without jeopardizing operating commitments. If the answer depends on uncertain closing proceeds, revisit the timetable, spending commitments or transaction itself before authorizing more work.

FAQs

When should a medical practice request a legal-cost estimate?

Request an initial scope before accepting financing terms or making spending commitments that depend on funding. If counsel needs to inspect documents first, ask for a separately scoped assessment and an explanation of what remains uncertain.

Does the practice have to pay the lender’s or investor’s legal fees?

Do not assume either outcome. Ask counsel to review the proposed expense provisions, including covered costs, any limits, payment timing and obligations if the transaction does not close. Keep potential reimbursement separate from the practice’s own counsel budget.

What should an owner ask about an abandoned financing?

Ask which fees remain payable, how work can be paused, what happens to any unused advance and whether third-party expenses or reimbursement obligations survive termination. Reflect the answers in the cash forecast rather than assuming no closing means no cost.

Can clean books replace legal record cleanup?

No. Clean books help establish financial facts, but they do not establish whether ownership records, approvals or transaction documents are legally sufficient. The finance team should reconcile financial information while counsel evaluates legal records and any corrective work.
