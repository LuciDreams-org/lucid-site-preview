Phantom Equity Tax Preparation for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Before promising phantom equity to a practice administrator, associate physician or other team member, prepare the facts your CPA and compensation attorney will need to evaluate the arrangement. A percentage alone is not enough: the advisers need to know what it measures, which entity owes the benefit, when it becomes payable and how the practice would fund it.

Phantom equity generally describes a contractual benefit linked to a business’s value or growth in value, rather than an issuance of actual ownership. The label does not determine the tax result or establish that the arrangement is permissible for your practice.

The owner’s immediate task is therefore not to select a tax rate or promise an after-tax payout. It is to assemble a complete decision package before making a commitment. This guide explains what belongs in that package and the questions it should help your advisers resolve.

Establish What the Proposed Award Actually Represents

A cash-linked benefit and an ownership interest are different arrangements. A proposed phantom award might reference the value of the whole practice, appreciation above an initial value, or the value of a specified business unit. Actual ownership instead involves an equity interest and the rights attached to it under the governing documents.

Do not describe a phantom award as making someone a “partner” or giving them “a share of the practice” unless counsel has confirmed that those descriptions are accurate. Likewise, do not assume that avoiding an ownership transfer removes professional-practice or compensation restrictions. Ask counsel to assess the proposed recipient, entities, services and applicable requirements.

Prepare a short concept memo that answers:

  • Who would receive the award, and for which services?
  • Which legal entity would make the promise and owe the payment?
  • What value would determine the benefit?
  • Would the award track full value or only appreciation above a starting amount?
  • What would the participant need to do to earn it?
  • What event and schedule would govern payment?

Mark this memo as a proposal for adviser review—not an approved plan or employee offer.

Identify the Entity That Would Owe the Benefit

A medical practice’s operating structure can make a simple-sounding award ambiguous. A physician-owned operating entity may employ clinicians, another entity may employ administrative staff, and separate entities may hold property or serve other business functions.

Your advisers need an entity map, not just a consolidated profit-and-loss statement. Identify the participant’s employer, the proposed payment obligor and the entities whose value or earnings would enter the formula.

For example, a practice administrator may support several locations, while the proposed award references only one operating entity. That mismatch needs resolution before anyone calculates a benefit. A location-level P&L can help measure performance, but a location is not necessarily a separate legal entity.

Include existing intercompany arrangements and shared-cost allocation methods. Ask the CPA and attorney how the proposed obligation should be assigned and whether the intended payer matches the arrangement’s legal and tax structure.

Build a Complete Records Checklist

Use one indexed folder with a dated document list. Separate current records from drafts, and identify missing items rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

Record group What to collect Why advisers need it
Entity and ownership records Entity chart, governing documents, ownership schedule and relevant intercompany agreements Identify the parties, approval authority and proposed payment obligation
Proposed plan terms Concept memo, draft plan, award agreement and related communications Establish what has been proposed or already communicated
Participant status Employing or contracting entity, role, work states, service dates and any ownership interest Evaluate classification, tax reporting and jurisdiction-specific questions
Award formula Units or percentage, full-value or appreciation-only design, base value, caps and adjustments Define the benefit being evaluated
Valuation inputs Financial statements, valuation reports, debt schedules, cash balances and relevant transaction documents Support the value definition and calculation method
Vesting and forfeiture Service or performance conditions, milestone definitions and departure provisions Identify when rights change and what facts affect tax analysis
Payment provisions Proposed triggers, payment windows, installments and any participant elections Evaluate payment timing and deferred-compensation requirements
Prior actions Approvals, signed agreements, amendments, payments and previous adviser conclusions Distinguish a new proposal from an existing obligation
Cash projections Estimated award payments, employer-side costs, payroll needs and entity-level liquidity Test whether the practice can meet the obligation

Complete, reconciled business records are the foundation for this work. IRS Publication 583 provides general guidance on business recordkeeping, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. It does not determine the tax treatment of a particular phantom equity plan.

Include Informal Promises and Earlier Drafts

Do not send advisers only the latest polished document. Include relevant offer letters, emails, bonus discussions and earlier versions that may describe the benefit differently.

If someone has already been told they will receive a percentage of future sale proceeds, flag the communication and its date. Ask counsel what it means and how to address it before sending a revised promise. Do not assume that replacing a document resolves earlier commitments.

Prepare Valuation Inputs Without Assuming a Required Method

The award’s value definition must be clear enough to calculate consistently. “Practice value” could refer to an operating business, an ownership interest or a specified amount after debt and other adjustments. Those are not interchangeable.

Ask the advisers what valuation work is appropriate for the proposed arrangement. Do not assume that every phantom equity plan requires the same appraisal method, valuation schedule or startup-style valuation process.

For a staffed practice, useful inputs may include:

  • Reconciled financial statements for the entities being measured.
  • Per-location P&Ls, with the shared-cost allocation method explained.
  • Provider and administrative compensation, including owner compensation adjustments proposed for review.
  • Payer receipts, patient-balance collection trends and accounts receivable summaries, where relevant to value.
  • Debt, leases, cash balances and intercompany balances.
  • Unusual expenses, recent acquisitions, location openings or closures, and available transaction terms.

Use aggregate financial information rather than patient-identifiable records for the analysis. If supporting work would require protected health information, confirm the appropriate handling arrangements before sharing it.

The advisers should also address how future changes would affect the formula: adding a location, changing owner compensation, acquiring another practice, taking on debt or selling only part of the business.

Organize Tax Questions Around Events, Not Assumed Answers

Avoid adopting a universal timeline such as “no tax at grant, payroll tax at vesting and income tax at payment.” Your advisers need to evaluate the actual arrangement, participant status and applicable rules. Book expense, tax deduction, participant income and cash payment also need not be treated as the same event.

Use the following agenda for the review meeting.

Decision area Questions for the CPA and compensation attorney Supporting facts
Participant tax timing When could the participant recognize income under this design? Could any obligation arise before cash is paid? Award terms, vesting conditions, payment provisions and participant status
Employment taxes and withholding Which employment-tax and withholding rules apply? What amounts and dates would payroll need? Employer identity, work states, compensation records and proposed payment schedule
Practice deduction Which entity may claim a deduction, if available, and when? What conditions or limitations require review? Payment obligor, tax classifications, accounting methods and service relationships
Deferred compensation Does Section 409A or another deferred-compensation requirement apply? Is an exception available, and what supports that conclusion? Complete plan terms, elections, vesting and payment provisions
Changes and departures What restrictions affect amendments, acceleration, delayed payments or termination provisions? Proposed change rights, departure scenarios and existing commitments
Documentation What approvals, valuation support and written conclusions should be completed before an award is offered? Governing documents, draft agreements and financial support

Request a written event calendar after the analysis. It should identify each relevant event, the responsible person, the information required and the action approved by the advisers. Leave unresolved items visibly open rather than letting payroll infer an answer.

Model the Cash Obligation Separately From the Tax Analysis

A benefit tied to practice value does not necessarily produce cash when payment becomes due. A growing practice may have strong earnings while cash is committed to payroll, equipment, debt service or a new location.

Model the proposed obligation at the entity that would owe it. Include existing awards as well as the new proposal, and separate the gross participant payment from employer-side costs and other implementation expenses.

Illustrative example—not a valuation or tax conclusion: Assume a draft appreciation-only award references a practice value of $2,000,000 at the start and $3,000,000 at the measurement date. If the draft formula provides 5% of that increase, the preliminary benefit would be $50,000 before any plan-specific adjustments. This arithmetic does not establish taxable income, withholding, deductibility, vesting or a legally permissible payment date.

The useful next step is to place that preliminary amount into a cash forecast:

Scenario What to test Owner’s decision
Scheduled payment during ordinary operations Available cash after provider/admin payroll, rent and other commitments Whether the proposed obligation is affordable without relying on a sale
Higher practice value Larger benefits across all outstanding awards Whether the aggregate obligation remains manageable
Slower payer receipts Lower near-term cash collections despite a payment obligation Whether adequate liquidity would remain
Sale or partial sale Which entity receives proceeds and which owes the award Whether expected proceeds actually reach the payer
Participant departure The payment obligation under the draft departure provisions Whether the proposed terms create an unexpected cash concentration

If the model shows a shortfall, return to the advisers before changing payment terms. A cash forecast identifies the problem; it does not authorize postponing an agreed payment.

Close the Review Before Making an Offer

The final pre-award package should contain more than a spreadsheet and a verbal assurance. Obtain adviser-reviewed documents, required approvals, the accepted valuation approach and written implementation instructions.

Assign responsibility for maintaining participant records, notifying advisers of vesting or payment events, supplying payroll information and updating the cash forecast. Establish which changes require another review, such as a new award, participant relocation, acquisition, sale discussion or amendment.

The owner’s approval checklist should answer four questions:

  1. Is the arrangement permissible for these entities and this participant? Counsel should resolve this for the specific facts.
  2. Are the tax and withholding consequences understood? The CPA and attorney should identify relevant events and remaining uncertainties.
  3. Can the practice fund the obligation? The forecast should test the proposed terms against operating needs.
  4. Can the practice administer what it promises? Documents, financial inputs and assigned responsibilities should agree.

FAQs

Does phantom equity make a medical practice employee an owner?

A cash-settled phantom equity arrangement generally creates a contractual benefit rather than actual equity ownership. The documents—not the label—must establish the rights being offered. Ask counsel to review both the arrangement and how it will be described to the participant.

Can we assume taxes are due only when the award is paid?

No. Ask the CPA to evaluate participant income, employment taxes, withholding and the practice’s potential deduction separately. Provide the vesting terms, payment conditions and participant status so the analysis is based on the proposed arrangement rather than a generic timeline.

What if we already promised an award informally?

Collect the relevant communications, dates, approvals and any calculations or payments. Give them to the CPA and attorney before making another promise or changing terms. An informal commitment should be disclosed for review, not omitted from the fact package.

What should we bring to the first adviser meeting?

Bring the concept memo, entity chart, participant details, all proposed or existing terms, reconciled financial statements, valuation inputs and an initial cash forecast. Include a list of open questions and any earlier commitments. The goal is to leave with clear decisions, missing-information requests and assigned next steps—not an unsupported tax conclusion.
