Cross-Border Payment Rules: Records Medical Practices Need

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Before your medical practice pays an overseas vendor or contractor, assemble the facts that your bank, CPA and qualified counsel need to review the transaction. An invoice and a set of bank instructions are not enough to answer every question about payment restrictions, tax reporting, withholding or required documentation.

The practical goal is a documented release decision: what the practice is paying for, who will receive the money, which questions have been resolved and who authorized payment. This matters whether you operate one staffed office or several locations through separate entities.

This article provides a preparation and authorization workflow—not a legal determination, a foreign-exchange budgeting guide or a comprehensive cross-border compliance program. Your advisers must evaluate the actual transaction and applicable rules.

Start With the Payment Decision, Not the Transfer Screen

An overseas payment can involve several different locations: the vendor’s place of organization, the contractor’s work location, the recipient bank’s country and the location of any subcontractor. Those facts should not be collapsed into a single “vendor country” field.

For example, a practice might engage an overseas design agency to update its public website. The contract could name one company while the invoice requests payment to another. The work may be legitimate, but the mismatch is an unresolved fact—not something the practice manager should explain away to meet a payment deadline.

Before scheduling payment, establish:

  • The practice entity that signed the agreement and owes the invoice.
  • The legal identity of the contracting party and intended recipient.
  • The nature of the services and where they were performed.
  • The destination account, payment route and requested currency.
  • The documents and adviser decisions still needed.

Do not assume that a bank’s willingness to process a transfer resolves the practice’s tax, contractual or other legal questions. Likewise, approval of the expense in the practice budget does not establish that the payment is ready for release.

Build a Transaction Fact Sheet

Create one fact sheet for each new overseas arrangement. Reference it on subsequent invoices, and update it when relevant facts change. Link supporting documents rather than relying on a manager’s memory or scattered email threads.

Identity, services and contractual facts

Keep “unknown” visible. If the vendor has not identified where the work was performed, record that as an open question rather than filling the field with its mailing address.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping in Publication 583 supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. The fact sheet is a practical way to organize the evidence behind a payment; it is not a substitute for transaction-specific advice.

Route the Facts to the Right Reviewer

Send each reviewer the same current fact sheet. Ask for an answer tied to the actual parties, services, destination and payment method—not a general statement that overseas vendors are acceptable.

Questions for the bank or payment provider

The practice manager should ask the proposed provider:

  • Can you process this payment using the disclosed parties, destination, currency and purpose?
  • What identity, ownership, contract or invoice documents do you require?
  • Are there restrictions or additional reviews affecting this transaction or route?
  • What information must accompany the payment instructions?
  • If the transfer is delayed, rejected or returned, what additional information will you request?

Record the response, date, contact and any reference number. A response such as “submit the transfer for review” is not the same as confirmation that every outstanding question has been resolved.

If the provider declines or flags a payment, pause and seek clarification with qualified counsel as appropriate. Do not change the recipient, description or payment route merely to bypass the concern.

Questions for the CPA or tax adviser

Give the CPA the contract, invoice, counterparty information and work-location facts. Ask:

  • How should this payment be characterized for tax and accounting purposes?
  • Which tax-status documents or certifications should we request before payment?
  • Do withholding or information-reporting obligations apply to this arrangement?
  • Does a claimed exemption or treaty position require further evidence or specialist review?
  • If withholding is required, how should the payment amount, contract terms and accounting entries be handled?
  • What records should the practice retain, and what retention schedule applies?

Do not choose a tax form solely because the vendor has a foreign address. Do not assume that a foreign bank account settles the treatment of the income. Ask the adviser to identify missing facts and document the resulting instructions.

If an individual contractor’s arrangement raises worker-classification questions, flag those separately. Labeling an invoice “consulting” does not resolve the issue.

Questions for qualified counsel

Counsel’s review may be needed for restrictions, ambiguous relationships or contractual questions. Ask:

  • Do the parties, ownership facts, service locations or payment destination raise restrictions that require additional review?
  • What screening or other diligence should be completed, by whom and using what current information?
  • Is the proposed third-party recipient or changed destination acceptable under the agreement and applicable rules?
  • Do local-law, worker-status or subcontracting questions need specialist advice?
  • Are additional contract provisions or documents needed before payment?

Bank processing requirements, tax treatment and legal permissibility are related but separate decisions. Record who addressed each question and the limits of their answer.

Limit Patient Information in the Review Packet

Most payment-purpose explanations should not need patient names, clinical notes or identifiable patient-balance details. For administrative services, describe the work using the contract, service period and non-patient-specific acceptance evidence where possible.

If a vendor’s services involve protected health information, escalate that issue through the practice’s privacy and legal review process. HHS explains covered entities and business associates, including circumstances in which accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities. Its minimum necessary guidance also describes the standard and its exceptions.

Do not attach patient-identifiable material to ordinary Slack messages or submit it to a general-purpose AI tool to summarize an invoice. Ask the appropriate reviewer what information is needed and how it should be shared.

Use a Payment-Release Checklist With Named Approvers

A useful checklist records evidence and accountability. “Finance approved” is too vague. Enter the actual person’s name, decision date and location of the supporting record.

The following is an internal-control template, not a list of universal legal requirements.

Business purpose and work acceptance Authorized manager confirms the services were ordered and accepted Practice manager: [actual name]
Paying entity Agreement, invoice and proposed funding account are aligned, or an exception is explained Finance lead: [actual name]
Counterparty and recipient Identity documents and any payee mismatch are resolved Assigned reviewer: [actual name]
Bank/provider requirements Requested documents supplied and provider questions addressed Bank liaison: [actual name]
Tax review Written direction on documentation, withholding and reporting, as applicable CPA/tax adviser: [actual name]

Where staffing permits, separate preparation, final authorization and reconciliation. If the same manager must perform several steps, have another authorized owner review the completed packet before release.

Keep unresolved questions actionable

Use an exception log with these fields:

Question → missing evidence → responsible person → adviser decision needed → target review date → release status.

For example: “Invoice names an agency, but requested account belongs to an individual. Practice manager to obtain explanation and supporting documents; counsel to review; payment on hold.”

Set the status to ready, on hold or canceled. Do not mark a payment ready while a question material to its authorization remains unanswered. Escalate timing pressure rather than treating urgency as approval.

Apply the Workflow Across Locations and Entities

Consider a hypothetical practice group engaging an overseas agency for nonclinical website work benefiting two locations. The management entity signs the contract, but the vendor invoices one clinical entity and requests payment to a differently named recipient.

The manager should not simply select whichever account has available cash. First resolve the contracting and invoicing mismatch, identify the recipient and obtain the necessary adviser decisions. Then document the correct paying entity and the accounting treatment of any shared costs.

If costs are allocated across locations, retain the allocation rationale with the accounting records. That supports consistent location P&Ls, but it does not answer whether the overseas payment itself is authorized.

For recurring payments, reference the approved arrangement and check for changes in the recipient, service scope, work location, destination, currency and provider requirements. Ask advisers when a fresh review is needed; do not treat an earlier approval as permanent.

Close the Loop After Payment

Retain the final fact sheet, contract, invoice, requested documents, adviser directions, approvals and payment confirmation in the practice’s designated records system. Reconcile the bank debit to the approved payment and record fees or other differences separately.

If the transfer is returned or redirected, reopen the payment packet before another release. Preserve the original decision and document the changed facts rather than overwriting them.

FAQs

Does paying an overseas contractor mean a medical practice needs a money-transmitter license?

Do not apply payment-provider licensing guidance to an ordinary vendor payment without reviewing the practice’s actual activities. Paying for the practice’s own services is a different fact pattern from operating a business that transmits money for others. If the arrangement goes beyond paying the practice’s own obligations, ask qualified counsel to assess it.

What if the vendor asks us to pay a different person or country?

Pause release and update the fact sheet. Obtain a documented explanation and supporting evidence, then refer the change to the bank, CPA or counsel as appropriate. Do not assume the original approval covers a different recipient or destination.

Which tax form or withholding rate should we use?

Have the CPA determine the required documentation and treatment from the actual facts, including counterparty status, service type and where work was performed. This article does not prescribe a form, rate, exemption or reporting threshold.

Can the practice pay while adviser questions remain open?

Keep the payment on hold when unresolved questions affect its authorization. Record the missing evidence, decision owner and next review step. A vendor deadline or an approved budget does not resolve a tax, legal or payment-provider question.
