Payroll Tax Record Retention for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A payroll provider’s document portal is not a retention policy. For a staffed private medical practice, the owner needs a reliable way to preserve payroll-tax records, explain which requirements govern them and retrieve the supporting evidence—even after changing providers or closing an entity.

The practical objective is to connect each filed return to the wages, tax calculations, payments and corrections behind it. That connection matters when your CPA investigates a notice, reconciles payroll liabilities or reviews an amended return.

Federal minimums are a starting point, not a universal destruction deadline. Your practice needs a documented schedule that accounts for applicable state requirements, special records and preservation holds. This article explains how to build that workflow with your CPA, payroll provider and counsel; it is not a completed legal retention schedule for a particular practice.

Start With Verified Federal Requirements

Two federal recordkeeping frameworks commonly overlap in payroll files. They serve different purposes, so the shorter period does not cancel the longer applicable requirement.

IRS employment-tax records

The IRS employment-tax recordkeeping guidance, verified for this article on November 3, 2026, says to keep employment-tax records for at least four years. Its record categories include wage information, employee withholding certificates, tax deposit information, filed returns and supporting records.

The same guidance identifies at least six years for specified paid-leave credit and employee retention credit records for certain later periods. That is not a six-year rule for every payroll document, and it does not establish eligibility for a credit.

Ask your CPA to identify the applicable record category, confirm the current rule and document the retention clock’s starting event. Do not calculate a destruction date simply by adding four years to a document’s creation date.

DOL wage-and-hour records

The Department of Labor’s FLSA recordkeeping guidance, also verified on November 3, 2026, generally describes at least three years for payroll records and at least two years for records underlying wage calculations. It also emphasizes complete and accurate hours and wage records.

These are FLSA requirements, not a substitute for IRS or state requirements. For example, an administrative employee’s time record may support both wage calculations and employment-tax records. Your advisers should classify that overlap before approving disposal.

State requirements and preservation holds

The practice’s review should cover relevant state tax, unemployment and wage-record requirements. Give your advisers the employing entities, work locations and any remote employees working in other states so they can determine which authorities apply.

Also ask counsel to identify records that must remain preserved because of a notice, examination, dispute, litigation or other hold. An ordinary retention period ending does not authorize destruction of records subject to a hold.

Identify the Records Your Practice Actually Needs

Organize records around the employing entity and tax period, not just the clinic’s public-facing name. Two locations may share one employer, while another location operates through a separate entity. The archive should make that distinction clear.

Maintain a companion approval register with the source title and URL, date checked, reviewer, triggering event, calculated earliest disposal date and hold status. A minimum period without a documented start date is not an operational schedule.

Assign Responsibilities and Verify State Rules

The practice owner should appoint an internal record custodian, usually the practice manager or payroll administrator. The custodian coordinates the archive; that role does not replace CPA or legal judgment.

Give each participant a defined responsibility:

  • Practice custodian: collects exports, maintains the index, controls access and tests retrieval.
  • Payroll provider: explains available records, export formats, historical access and the process for obtaining missing documents.
  • CPA: reviews tax-record categories, reconciliations, corrections and applicable tax retention requirements.
  • Counsel: reviews state wage-record overlap, preservation obligations and disputed retention questions.
  • Owner or authorized manager: approves the policy, assigns resources and authorizes disposal only after required reviews.

For state verification, ask the advisers to consult current primary materials from the relevant agencies. Record the governing source, covered documents, required period and starting event—not just a verbal answer that the practice should “keep everything for several years.”

If the state analysis remains incomplete, retain the affected records. “Pending CPA/counsel verification” is a valid control status; it is not permission to dispose.

Build Retrieval Into the Payroll Close

A retention policy works only if the files are complete and usable. Add an archive check to the practice’s payroll and tax-period close process.

First, save the payroll registers, calculation support and relevant funding reports. When returns and payment confirmations become available, add them to the same entity-and-period folder. Keep original submissions alongside corrections, clearly identifying which version supersedes another.

Next, reconcile the records. A bank withdrawal by a payroll provider may combine net pay, taxes and service charges. It does not, by itself, explain the tax deposited with an agency. Preserve the reports needed to bridge that withdrawal to the payroll register, tax liabilities and deposit confirmations.

The IRS business-recordkeeping guidance in Publication 583 supports keeping complete business records and reconciling bank records. For a practice, the useful outcome is a traceable connection between payroll expense, payroll liabilities and cash—not simply a folder of downloaded statements.

Illustrative practice scenario: A practice operates two offices through one employing entity. The manager preserves one entity-level tax packet and separate location-allocation workpapers for internal reporting. Those allocation workpapers explain the location P&Ls, but they do not replace the entity’s payroll registers or filed tax returns.

Test retrieval periodically. Ask someone other than the regular payroll administrator to locate a return, its payment confirmation and the supporting wage register. Missing permissions, unreadable files or unclear naming should be corrected before an agency request creates urgency.

Protect Historical Access When Changing Providers

Before ending a payroll-provider relationship, obtain a written explanation of historical access. Do not assume the portal remains available after termination or that the successor provider receives every historical document.

Use a transition checklist:

  1. Inventory the periods held by the outgoing provider. Compare them with the practice’s archive and retention matrix.
  2. Export source detail and readable reports. Include returns, payment evidence, employee tax forms, calculations and corrections where available.
  3. Check completeness. Open files, confirm the entity and periods, and identify any missing records.
  4. Resolve outstanding matters. Document who will handle pending notices, rejected submissions and corrections.
  5. Confirm post-termination arrangements. Record access duration, retrieval procedures and any applicable service terms.
  6. Assign continuing custody. Ensure an authorized practice representative can access the archive if the usual administrator leaves.

Apply similar checks when closing an entity or consolidating offices. An operational change should not make historical records inaccessible.

Control Access and Approve Destruction Deliberately

Payroll records contain sensitive employee information. Limit access to people with a defined business need, review permissions after staffing changes and maintain a recovery process appropriate to the storage system. Test that stored files remain readable.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of payroll-tax support whenever possible. A wage reconciliation generally needs employee compensation information, not patient charts. Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack conversations or general-purpose AI tools.

Separate retention review from destruction approval. Before disposal, confirm that the verified period has expired, all overlapping requirements have been addressed and no hold remains. Record the approving parties, affected record categories and periods, destruction date and method. Coordinate deletion of copies and backups with the system administrator and counsel rather than assuming that deleting a folder removes every copy.

FAQs

Can a medical practice destroy all payroll-tax records after four years?

No. The IRS describes a minimum of at least four years for employment-tax records, but some specified credit records have longer requirements. State rules, overlapping obligations and preservation holds can also prevent disposal. Have the CPA confirm the starting event and applicable requirements before calculating a disposal date.

Is keeping records in the payroll provider’s portal enough?

Do not rely on portal access without checking its terms and testing retrieval. The practice should know which records are available, how to export them and what happens after termination. Maintain a practice-controlled archive with an accountable custodian and verified completeness.

What should the practice do while state requirements are being reviewed?

Mark the affected rows “Pending CPA/counsel verification” and permit no disposal until approved. Give advisers the employing entities and employee work locations, then document the primary sources, applicable periods and starting events they verify.

Should amended returns replace the original files?

Keep both, clearly labeled, along with the calculations, correspondence and submission evidence explaining the correction. Ask the CPA to determine how the correction affects retention. A final corrected return alone may not explain the original reporting or the reason for the change.
