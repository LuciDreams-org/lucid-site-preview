How do I work out my med spa's profit margin on each treatment?

December 7, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 7, 2026

No single number fits every med spa, because prices, pay plans and rent differ. To find your profit margin on one treatment, subtract the product you used and wasted, the provider's pay, the card fee and supplies from the price, and divide what's left by the price. Then check it covers your rent and staff for the room time.

Key points

  • In Example 1, a $420 neurotoxin visit leaves you $108 after product, waste, injector pay, card fee and supplies: a 25.7% margin.
  • If you lose 5 units from every 100-unit vial, each unit costs you about 5% more than the invoice says.
  • In Example 2, you earn $359 per room hour on laser and $216 on neurotoxin, but you make the most from neurotoxin over the month because you book far more of it.
  • You make money on a treatment only if you earn more per room hour on it than you spend on overhead per booked hour ($183 in the example).

What is a treatment's profit margin?

A treatment's profit margin is the share of its price you keep after paying the costs that come with that one appointment. You figure it per visit, then add up the visits to see whether you covered your overhead for the month.

  • Gross profit margin is your revenue minus your cost of goods sold, divided by revenue.
  • Cost of goods sold is the cost of the product you used in treatments or sold over the counter.
  • Contribution margin is the price of a treatment minus every cost that comes with it, including provider commission and card fees.
  • Overhead is what you spend every month whatever you book: rent, front desk pay, software, insurance and marketing.
  • Net profit margin is what's left after overhead, divided by the month's revenue.
  • A room hour is one hour of one treatment room booked with a client.

Which costs belong to the treatment, and which are overhead?

A cost belongs to the treatment if it goes up when you book one more visit and goes away when a client cancels. Product, waste, commission, card fees and supplies do. Rent, salaried staff and your medical director's monthly fee don't, so they go in overhead.

Cost Treatment or overhead
Neurotoxin, filler, laser tips, facial serums Treatment (cost of goods sold)

If you book every product order as one expense the day you pay it, you can't see any of this by treatment. To set up product cost accounts by service line, see the chart of accounts guide for medical practices.

How do I figure the real cost of a unit of Botox or a syringe of filler?

Divide what you paid for a vial by the units you actually billed from it, not the units printed on the box. For example, a vial that costs $570 for 100 units costs $5.70 a unit on paper. If you bill 95 units and lose 5, each billed unit really costs you $6.00.

You lose units when a client cancels after you've mixed a vial, when units are left at the end of the day, and in the small amount left in each syringe. The labels for Botox Cosmetic, Dysport and Xeomin say to use a mixed vial within 24 hours, and the Daxxify label allows 72 hours. So how much you lose depends on the brand and on how full your schedule is that day.

To find your own number, divide last month's product spend from your invoices by the units you billed in your point of sale reports. Each label also says its units can't be compared with another brand's units, so compare brands on cost per treatment, not cost per unit. For filler, count each syringe you open against that visit, even if you used only part of it.

Example 1: what does one neurotoxin appointment earn?

In this example, you keep $108 of a $420 neurotoxin visit after every cost that comes with it, a 25.7% contribution margin.

Example numbers, not market prices. You charge $15 a unit and the client gets 28 units. You pay $570 for a 100-unit vial and bill 95 units from it, so each billed unit costs $6.00. The injector earns 30% of the price, your card fee is 3%, and supplies cost $5.40.

Injector commission $126.00 30% of $420
Card fee $12.60 3% of $420
Supplies $5.40 Needles, gauze, numbing cream
Contribution margin $108.00 $420 minus $312 in costs
Margin as a share of price 25.7% $108 divided by $420
Per room hour $216.00 $108 for 30 minutes

At the invoice price of $5.70 a unit, you'd have put the margin at $116.40, which is $8.40 a visit you never earned.

Example 2: which treatments bring in most of your profit?

In this example, you earn the most per room hour on laser hair removal, but you make the most over the month on neurotoxin because you book 160 of those visits. You earn the least on facials, $109 a room hour, which is less than the $183 an hour you spend on overhead.

Example numbers, not market prices. Neurotoxin is Example 1, and every treatment has the 3% card fee. Filler is one $300 syringe sold at $750, with a 30% commission and $7.50 of supplies. For laser, you pay $8 for tips and gel, $25 to the laser tech, and $2,700 a month for the lease and service plan, or $30 a treatment over 90 treatments. For facials, you use $35 of product and pay the esthetician $50.

Why can a busy month still lose money when injectors earn commission?

You can lose money in a busy month if you discount the price while your product cost stays the same. A 30% commission falls with the price, but the $6.00 you paid for each unit doesn't, so when you discount, you cut your margin far more than the price.

Take Example 1 during a promotion at $11 a unit. You charge $308 for the same 28 units. Product still costs $168, commission is $92.40, the card fee is $9.24 and supplies are $5.40. You keep $32.96, a 10.7% margin, or about $66 per room hour. That's well under the $183 an hour you spend on overhead, so you lose money on every promotional visit once you count rent and staff.

Before you run a promotion, work out the lowest price per unit that still covers the room time. In Example 1, that's about $14.10 a unit.

How do I price a treatment so it covers rent and the room?

Divide your monthly overhead by the room hours you actually book, then make sure you earn more than that per room hour on each treatment. In Example 2, $41,000 of overhead over 224 booked hours comes to $183 an hour, so you lose money on a facial at $109 an hour.

The 224 hours come from 2 rooms, each open 160 hours a month and booked 70% of the time. If you book 50% of the time, the same overhead comes to $256 an hour.

If you earn less than that on a treatment, you can raise the price, shorten the room time or cut the product or pay cost per visit.

To find and cut the overhead side, see the guide to overhead as a percentage of revenue.

Questions med spa owners ask about profit margin

What is a good profit margin for a med spa?

A good margin is one where you earn more per room hour on every treatment than you spend on overhead per booked hour, and you still have a profit at the end of the month after you pay yourself. No single number fits every med spa, because prices, pay plans and rent differ so much.

How do I calculate cost per unit of Botox or filler?

Divide what you spent on product last month by the units you billed, not the units you bought. The gap is what you lost to expired vials, cancellations and leftovers. For filler, divide your syringe spend by the filler visits you billed. Rerun it every month, because it changes with your schedule.

Should injector pay count as cost of goods sold?

In your monthly reports, put commission and per-treatment pay with the treatment costs, because you pay them only when the treatment happens. Salaried staff you'd pay anyway go in overhead. Your CPA decides how these costs appear on your tax return, which can differ from your monthly reports.

How much does a med spa owner make?

What you make is the profit left after overhead, plus any pay you take for treating clients yourself. In Example 2, the owner made $8,725 in a month after $41,000 of overhead. If you inject, you also keep the commission you'd otherwise pay an injector, so add both when you compare your pay with a salary.

Is a 70% profit margin good for a med spa?

Not on its own, because a 70% margin on one treatment can still leave you a small profit for the month. In Example 2, you keep 72% of each $250 laser visit, but only if you book 90 treatments a month. On neurotoxin, you keep 25.7% after you pay the injector. After $41,000 of overhead, you end the month at a 6.3% net profit margin.

Is a membership more profitable than single visits?

You usually earn less per visit on a membership because members pay a lower price, but you can earn more over a year if members keep your rooms booked. Compare the member price per unit with your product cost per unit first. Count credits members haven't used as money you owe until they book.
