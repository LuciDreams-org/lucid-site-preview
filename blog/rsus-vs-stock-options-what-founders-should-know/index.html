RSUs vs. Stock Options for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

If a prospective practice administrator, physician leader, or management hire asks for equity compensation, the first question is not whether RSUs outperform stock options. It is whether the proposed award fits your practice’s entity structure, ownership restrictions, and ability to fund future obligations.

Stock options provide a right to buy shares at a specified exercise price. Restricted stock units (RSUs) represent a contractual promise to deliver shares or, under some arrangements, cash after the applicable conditions are satisfied. Neither is automatically suitable for a private medical practice, and neither guarantees a financial return.

For owners, the practical task is to identify what is being promised, which entity owes it, when ownership or payment could occur, and who supplies the cash. Use the comparison and document checklist below to prepare for discussions with legal, tax, valuation, and accounting advisers—not to design an award without them.

Start With the Issuing Entity and Eligible Owner

A practice’s name may cover several legal entities: a professional entity providing medical services, a management company, and separate entities associated with individual locations. An award in one entity does not necessarily provide an interest in the others.

Before discussing award size, ask counsel to identify:

  • The issuer: Which legal entity would grant the award or owe the payment?
  • The recipient: May this particular employee or manager legally hold the resulting interest?
  • The instrument: Does the entity have the shares and authority needed for the proposed award?
  • The approvals: What governing-document changes, owner consents, or other approvals would be necessary?
  • The economic scope: Does the award relate to the entire business, one entity, or a defined portion of its value?

Professional-ownership restrictions can affect who may own an interest in a medical practice. Entity form, tax classification, governing agreements, and state-specific requirements also matter. Do not assume that a nonclinical administrator can receive the same ownership interest as a physician owner, or that an LLC can simply use a corporate stock-option template.

An award in a separate management entity is not automatically an acceptable workaround. Have qualified counsel evaluate the complete structure before anyone promises ownership.

How Stock Options Work

A stock option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to purchase a specified number of shares at an agreed exercise price, also called a strike price. The award documents determine when that right becomes exercisable and when it expires.

The stages are distinct:

  1. Grant: The option is awarded under approved terms.
  2. Vesting: The holder earns rights under the stated service or performance conditions.
  3. Exercise: The holder uses the option to purchase shares and satisfies the required payment terms.
  4. Ownership and possible sale: The acquired shares remain subject to their governing rights and transfer restrictions.

Vesting alone generally does not make an option holder a shareholder. Exercise and share issuance are separate steps.

An option’s economic appeal depends partly on whether the shares become worth more than the exercise price. But a favorable valuation does not create a buyer. In a closely held practice, an employee might need cash to exercise while having no immediate way to sell the resulting shares.

Ask advisers to explain the option classification, exercise procedures, expiration provisions, departure rules, and tax consequences for both the practice and recipient. Do not rely on a standard exercise window or assume that all options receive the same tax treatment.

How RSUs Work

An RSU is generally a contractual promise tied to future delivery of shares or cash. It is not the same as receiving restricted shares immediately.

Two terms deserve separate attention:

  • Vesting describes when service or other earning conditions are satisfied.
  • Settlement describes when the promised shares or cash are delivered.

These events may coincide, but they do not have to. The award terms and applicable legal and tax requirements determine their relationship. A vested award should not automatically be described as immediately delivered, freely transferable stock.

RSUs generally do not require the recipient to pay a stock-option exercise price. That does not make them cost-free or risk-free. Tax-related cash needs may arise, the business’s value may decline, settlement conditions may remain outstanding, and shares may be difficult to sell.

Voting rights, distributions, and any dividend-equivalent rights also depend on the instrument and governing documents. Do not promise shareholder rights merely because an RSU has vested.

Keep Valuation, Liquidity, and Dilution Separate

Valuation: What is the award actually worth?

A practice’s revenue is not its equity value. Payer receipts, patient collections, provider compensation, operating costs, debt, and other obligations can affect the financial picture advisers evaluate.

For a multi-location business, confirm whether the valuation reflects the issuing entity as a whole or a particular economic interest. A profitable location’s P&L does not, by itself, establish the value of shares in an entity that also carries shared overhead and debt.

Request valuation support for the relevant share class and purpose. Ask advisers what valuation requirements apply, including whether a Section 409A analysis is relevant. A tax valuation, financial-reporting valuation, and contractual buyout formula may serve different purposes; do not assume they are interchangeable.

Liquidity: How does the recipient obtain cash?

A valuation estimate is not a cash offer. Transfers may require consent, and there may be no outside buyer. A possible future practice sale should not be presented as an assured payment event.

Ask whether liquidity would depend on a permitted third-party transfer, owner purchase, contractual repurchase, or another event. Establish who must pay, how the price is determined, and whether payment can be deferred under the documents.

Dilution: What changes for existing owners?

Request a capitalization schedule showing existing interests, outstanding awards, and the proposed award on a clearly defined fully diluted basis. Voting control and economic participation should be reviewed separately.

Illustrative example: An entity has 1,000 outstanding shares and issues 100 new shares to settle an award. Assuming identical share rights and no other interests, the recipient holds 100 of 1,100 shares, or about 9.1%. This illustrates ownership arithmetic only; it does not establish award value, legal eligibility, or tax treatment.

Do Not Confuse Actual Equity With Phantom Equity

A proposal labeled “equity” may actually be a contractual compensation arrangement.

Phantom equity generally measures a payment by reference to business value, appreciation, or another defined metric without issuing actual ownership interests. Stock options and share-settled RSUs, by contrast, can lead to actual share ownership. Cash-settled RSUs require careful review because their settlement mechanics differ from share delivery.

The label is less important than the enforceable terms. Ask:

  • Will the recipient ever receive an ownership interest?
  • Does the arrangement confer voting or distribution rights?
  • Which entity owes any cash payment?
  • How is the payment calculated, and what triggers it?
  • What happens after termination, disability, death, or a business sale?

Phantom equity may avoid issuing shares, but it can create substantial cash obligations and still requires tax, employment, and other legal review. It is not automatically a simpler or unrestricted alternative.

Request These Documents

Before approving an award, assemble a review file containing:

  • Governing agreements: Formation documents, bylaws or operating agreement, shareholder agreements, transfer restrictions, buy-sell provisions, and relevant lender restrictions.
  • Ownership records: Current capitalization schedule, existing awards, reserved interests, and rights attached to each class.
  • Proposed award documents: Plan, grant agreement, vesting conditions, exercise or settlement provisions, expiration terms, and departure or sale treatment.
  • Valuation support: Available reports, valuation dates, assumptions, financial inputs, and any contractual repurchase formula.
  • Financial records: Entity-level financial statements, debt schedules, cash forecasts, and relevant location P&Ls with shared-cost allocations explained.
  • Cash and reporting analysis: Adviser-prepared estimates of potential recipient payments, practice funding needs, payroll reporting, and accounting treatment.
  • Approvals and advice: Required consents and written adviser conclusions on unresolved ownership, tax, securities, and employment questions.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records and reconciling accounts. It does not determine whether a particular equity award is appropriate.

Test Cash Needs Against Practice Operations

Equity compensation is not necessarily cash-free compensation. Model possible obligations alongside provider and administrative payroll, rent, debt service, and ordinary operating reserves.

Illustrative example: A practice projects $180,000 in available cash before a possible $45,000 award-related settlement payment. That leaves $135,000 before other outflows. If payroll and other scheduled operating payments total $150,000 before expected payer receipts arrive, the forecast shows a $15,000 funding gap. These invented figures illustrate timing risk, not a recommended reserve or award size.

Run scenarios for delayed collections, multiple awards settling together, and a departing employee’s contractual buyback. Keep obligations with the entity that owes them; do not assume another practice entity can freely supply cash.

The CPA should also distinguish compensation expense from actual cash movement. A payment forecast alone does not establish the correct accounting treatment.

Make the Decision With the Right Advisers

Proceed only after the team can explain the instrument, recipient eligibility, ownership effect, valuation basis, reporting requirements, and funding plan in plain language. Pause if a material question remains unresolved or if the recipient cannot understand how the award might—or might not—produce cash.

Healthcare ownership counsel should address professional-ownership and entity questions. Counsel with relevant expertise should assess securities and employment issues. Tax advisers, valuation professionals, and the accounting team should address their respective areas. Recipients may also need independent advice.

FAQs

Can a medical practice offer stock options to a nonclinical administrator?

Do not assume so. Counsel must evaluate the issuing entity, recipient, applicable professional-ownership restrictions, and resulting ownership rights. Using a management entity does not remove the need to review the complete arrangement.

Do RSUs become shares as soon as they vest?

Not necessarily. Vesting and settlement are separate concepts. The agreement may provide for later settlement or cash rather than shares. Ask advisers to identify the delivery terms and tax consequences instead of assuming vesting creates immediate ownership.

Which causes less dilution: RSUs or stock options?

Neither always causes less dilution. Compare actual share quantities, settlement terms, share rights, and fully diluted ownership. A cash-settled award may avoid share issuance while creating a practice cash obligation instead.

Can employees owe taxes without being able to sell their shares?

That risk can arise with equity compensation. The relevant events and obligations depend on the instrument and circumstances. Ask tax advisers to map potential obligations against available cash and transfer restrictions before making or accepting an award.

Is phantom equity the same as ownership?

Generally, no. Phantom equity usually creates a contractual payment right rather than an ownership interest. Review the actual agreement: its payment formula, triggers, funding requirements, and legal and tax treatment matter more than its label.
