Phantom Equity Plans for Medical Practices: Pros and Cons

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A proposed phantom equity arrangement can sound like a straightforward way to retain a valued practice administrator or physician leader: reward growth in the practice’s value without transferring ownership. The financial commitment, however, can be harder to understand than a conventional cash bonus.

For a staffed private medical practice, the central question is not simply whether phantom equity avoids issuing shares. It is whether a value-linked compensation promise serves a clear retention objective—and whether the practice can fund it when payment becomes due.

This article provides a financial screening framework, not a plan template or a determination that an arrangement is permissible for your practice. Before making a commitment, have compensation, healthcare legal and tax advisers review the proposed terms, entities and participants.

What Phantom Equity Means—and What It Does Not

Phantom equity generally describes a contractual payment linked to a defined measure of business value. The participant does not receive actual ownership merely because the agreement uses terms such as “units” or “equity.” The contract determines the payment rights.

Actual ownership involves an interest in an entity, with rights and obligations determined by its governing documents and applicable law. A phantom award instead creates a compensation promise. It does not, by itself, make the recipient a shareholder or member or give them an owner’s voting rights.

That distinction does not make the arrangement legally simple. A medical practice should not assume that calling compensation “phantom equity” resolves healthcare ownership or compensation restrictions. Counsel needs to evaluate the actual arrangement, not just its label.

Two common economic designs illustrate why the details matter:

  • Full-value awards: The payment reflects a defined portion of the value measured at settlement, subject to the agreement’s terms.
  • Appreciation-only awards: The payment reflects growth above a defined starting value, subject to the agreement’s terms.

Neither design guarantees a payment or establishes when payment occurs. Vesting determines how an award is earned under the agreement; settlement determines when and how it is paid. Those are separate questions.

Start With the Retention Objective

Before discussing award sizes, identify the business problem the practice wants to solve.

A multi-location practice might want to retain an administrator through a lengthy expansion. A single-location owner might want continuity while reducing day-to-day management responsibilities. In either case, ask why a value-linked award would work better than a cash bonus tied to clearly defined responsibilities and results.

Phantom equity may merit review when the employee has meaningful influence over long-term business performance and understands the proposed value measure. It may be less compelling when the participant primarily wants predictable annual compensation or has little influence over the factors driving practice value.

Avoid treating value growth as a complete measure of contribution. A practice’s value can change because of market conditions, owner decisions, financing or a new provider joining—not just the participant’s work. Conversely, an administrator may improve staffing stability and collections workflows while a change in market valuations offsets those gains.

The owner should be able to explain the connection between the participant’s role, the retention period and the proposed reward before commissioning detailed plan work.

Phantom Equity Versus a Conventional Cash Bonus

A cash bonus is not automatically simple, but it can offer a more direct connection between performance, cost and payment. Phantom equity introduces an additional variable: the value of the business or entity being measured.

Decision factor Phantom equity Conventional cash bonus
Retention objective Can connect a future reward to continued service and long-term value Can reward annual results or a defined retention period
Participant understanding Requires a clear explanation of value, vesting and settlement Often easier to explain as a stated amount or performance formula
Cost predictability Cost can change with the valuation measure and award terms A defined award or capped formula may be easier to budget
Payment timing May be separated from vesting and tied to specified settlement terms Often paid on a shorter, specified compensation cycle
Liquidity exposure Value can rise without a matching increase in available cash Cash needs may be easier to align with the operating budget
Administration Requires award records, valuation inputs and scenario modeling Requires performance records, approvals and payroll coordination
Ownership Does not itself transfer actual ownership Does not transfer ownership

For either option, advisers must assess the proposed terms. A deferred or retention-based cash bonus can also raise legal and tax questions; it should not be treated as an unreviewed workaround.

The Main Pros and Cons for a Medical Practice

Potential advantage: a long-term reward without transferring ownership

An owner may want to recognize a key employee’s contribution without making that person a partner. Phantom equity separates the proposed economic reward from an actual ownership transfer.

Still, avoiding ownership dilution does not avoid economic cost. A payment reduces cash available for other purposes. If it is connected to a sale, it can reduce what remains for owners, depending on the transaction and agreement.

Potential advantage: alignment beyond one year’s results

An annual bonus can emphasize short-term performance. A value-linked arrangement may encourage a longer view, particularly when responsibilities include developing managers, improving location-level profitability or building a less owner-dependent organization.

That alignment depends on the formula. A measure that rewards growth without considering debt, shared costs or sustainable staffing can produce a result the owner did not intend.

Disadvantage: valuation uncertainty

A private practice does not have a continuously quoted market price. The parties need an agreed valuation process and a way to handle disputed inputs.

Important questions include whether the measure refers to enterprise value or equity value, how debt and cash are treated, and whether owner compensation or unusual expenses are adjusted. These choices can materially change the modeled payment.

For a multi-entity practice, the scope is equally important. An award linked to one operating entity is not necessarily linked to every location, management entity or property interest associated with the owners.

Disadvantage: payment can arrive when cash is tight

A higher practice valuation does not mean the practice has more spendable cash. Payer receipts can slow, patient balances can remain outstanding, and provider and administrative payroll still need funding.

A settlement obligation could also coincide with a departure, financing need or transaction that does not deliver enough immediate cash to the responsible entity. Do not assume a sale will occur—or that a sale will fund every obligation.

Disadvantage: ongoing administrative work

Someone must maintain reliable award records, reconcile the underlying financial information and track approved changes. The CPA should determine the appropriate accounting treatment under the practice’s reporting framework rather than relying on a blanket statement that every phantom award is recorded the same way.

The more entities, participants and valuation adjustments involved, the greater the coordination burden.

Decision Matrix: Does the Proposal Merit Detailed Review?

Use this matrix as an initial screen, not as approval to implement a plan.

Question Reason to explore phantom equity Reason to compare a cash bonus first
What behavior are you rewarding? Sustained contribution to long-term business value Specific annual results or completion of a project
Does the participant understand the reward? They can explain how value affects their potential payment They expect “equity” to mean ownership or guaranteed money
Can the practice support a valuation process? Entity boundaries and financial inputs are reliable Books, shared costs or owner adjustments remain disputed
Is payment funding credible? Downside cash forecasts include the potential obligation Funding depends on optimistic collections or an uncertain sale
Is the intended retention period meaningful? It matches the participant’s role and business needs The employee values near-term pay more than a distant award
Can the practice administer it? Named owners can maintain records and coordinate advisers No one is responsible for tracking awards and changes

Unreliable books or unclear entity boundaries are reasons to pause. They make both valuation and affordability difficult to assess.

Hypothetical Payout-Scenario Worksheet

All figures below are illustrative assumptions, not market benchmarks, recommended award terms or a valuation of any actual practice. The worksheet compares two alternative designs; it does not assume both awards are granted.

Assume:

  • The agreement’s defined starting equity value is $4,000,000.
  • The hypothetical award participation rate is 2%.
  • 75% of the award is vested at settlement.
  • The full-value calculation is ending equity value × participation rate × vested portion.
  • The appreciation-only calculation is positive growth above starting equity value × participation rate × vested portion, with a zero floor.
  • There are no caps, prior payments or additional adjustments in this simplified example.
Illustrative settlement scenario Ending defined equity value Full-value payment Appreciation-only payment
Value declines $3,000,000 $45,000 $0
Value stays flat $4,000,000 $60,000 $0
Value increases $6,000,000 $90,000 $30,000

For the growth scenario, the calculations are:

  • Full value: $6,000,000 × 2% × 75% = $90,000.
  • Appreciation only: ($6,000,000 − $4,000,000) × 2% × 75% = $30,000.

The difference shows why “a small percentage of the practice” is not a sufficient description of the promise.

Add a liquidity test

Extend the same illustrative example: the paying entity expects $180,000 of cash before settlement and needs $150,000 for operating commitments and its chosen cash buffer. That leaves $30,000 available under those assumptions.

The illustrative $90,000 full-value payment would exceed that amount by $60,000. The $30,000 appreciation-only payment would use all of it, leaving no margin above the assumed buffer. Neither result proves affordability.

Have the finance team add any applicable employer costs, adviser costs and other obligations identified during review. Then stress-test slower payer receipts, payroll needs, debt payments and necessary equipment spending. Test each paying entity separately; cash held elsewhere should not be assumed available without review.

Records and Questions Your Advisers Need

Prepare a review packet before discussing a final offer. The IRS’s business recordkeeping guidance provides a useful foundation for complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. A phantom equity review also needs proposal-specific information.

Review area Records to gather Questions to resolve
Entity and participant scope Entity chart, governing documents, participant role and compensation arrangements Which entity makes the promise, which value is measured, and who pays?
Financial baseline Reconciled balance sheets, P&Ls, debt schedules and cash forecasts Are starting figures dependable, and what adjustments require agreement?
Locations and shared costs Per-location results and documented allocation methods Could changing allocations alter the award value?
Vesting Proposed service or performance conditions and award history What is earned, when, and how is achievement documented?
Departures Proposed treatment of resignation, termination, retirement, death and disability What happens to vested and unvested amounts in each case?
Valuation Proposed method, inputs and any prior valuation work Who determines value, on what dates, and how are disputes addressed?
Settlement Proposed payment events, timing and funding assumptions Could payment be due without sufficient cash or transaction proceeds?

Ask compensation advisers whether the reward is understandable and competitive for the role. Ask healthcare counsel to evaluate the proposed participants, compensation basis and structure. Ask tax advisers about applicable deferred-compensation, withholding and tax-timing issues before promises are made. Ask the CPA to assess accounting treatment and cash-flow effects.

Use aggregate financial records for initial analysis where possible; patient-identifiable details generally are not needed to compare these compensation alternatives.

A Practical Next Step

Before authorizing detailed design work, write a one-page business case: the retention problem, why a conventional bonus may be insufficient, the entity whose value would be measured, and the downside funding scenarios.

If that case remains persuasive after modeling, take it to the advisers with the review packet. If it depends on an uncertain sale, disputed valuation assumptions or cash the practice cannot spare, reconsider the compensation approach first.

FAQs

Does phantom equity make an employee an owner of a medical practice?

A contractual phantom award does not itself transfer an ownership interest. Its payment rights depend on the agreement. That distinction does not establish that the arrangement is permissible for a particular practice; healthcare counsel must review the actual terms and circumstances.

Does vesting mean the practice must pay immediately?

Not necessarily. Vesting concerns how the award is earned; settlement concerns when and how payment occurs. Advisers should review both provisions together, including what happens when a participant leaves before settlement.

What if the practice never sells?

Model that possibility before making the promise. Depending on the agreement, payment might depend on a sale or another specified settlement event. Ask whether the arrangement still serves its retention purpose without a sale and whether any non-sale payment can be funded from operations.

When should an owner prefer a conventional cash bonus?

A cash bonus may be the better starting point when the objective is annual performance, predictable compensation cost or a reward employees can readily understand. Compare the alternatives using the same retention objective and realistic cash forecasts, then obtain appropriate review of the proposed terms.
