5 Steps to Prepare Cross-Border Payroll Reviews for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A U.S. private medical practice may use overseas administrative support for scheduling, bookkeeping or other back-office work. Before hiring—or when reviewing an existing arrangement—the owner needs more than a payment method and a signed agreement. The practice needs a clear record of who performs the work, where it happens, which entity pays and who is responsible for reviewing the arrangement.

A cross-border payroll review starts with facts, not assumptions about compliance. Calling someone a contractor, paying an agency invoice or using a payroll provider does not by itself establish which obligations apply. Those questions require advisers familiar with the relevant jurisdictions and the actual working arrangement.

This five-step workflow helps owners and managers prepare an organized review packet. It is not a universal compliance checklist or a substitute for country-specific tax and employment advice. It also differs from a broader cross-border AI or data review: the focus here is worker arrangements, payroll responsibilities, payment records and staff cost accounting.

Step 1: Document Work Locations and Working Arrangements

Create an inventory of everyone performing administrative work outside the United States for the practice. Include people paid directly, through an agency or through an employer-of-record arrangement. Identify the arrangement as documented today without treating the contract label as a legal conclusion.

Record the actual work location, not just the worker’s mailing address or the location of their bank. Ask workers or vendors to report location changes through an agreed process so advisers can assess whether a change affects the arrangement.

Build a payroll fact sheet

Use one fact sheet per worker or vendor arrangement. Keep sensitive identity and banking documents in an appropriately controlled location rather than copying them into a general review spreadsheet.

Fact sheet field What to document Why it matters for the review
Practice entity Legal entity named in the agreement, entity making payments and any differences Helps advisers identify the parties and reconcile costs
Worker or vendor Name, internal reference and contracting counterparty Connects the agreement to the payment records
Work location Country and locality where work is performed; known changes and dates Gives advisers the geographic facts they need
Service arrangement Direct hire, stated contractor arrangement, agency or employer of record Shows the structure being reviewed without validating it
Administrative duties Actual tasks, supervisor and departments supported Helps assess how the arrangement operates in practice
Work practices Schedule, time approval, equipment, supervision and ability to serve other clients Provides facts relevant to an adviser’s classification review
Payment terms Currency, frequency, rate basis, reimbursements and payment route Supports payroll review and cash planning
Practice locations served Single office, multiple locations or shared administrative function Supports consistent staff cost allocation
Review status Last review, unresolved questions and responsible adviser Keeps open items visible

For example, an overseas scheduling assistant might support several offices while one practice entity signs the agreement and another pays the invoices. Document that mismatch rather than assuming the entities are interchangeable.

Describe actual behavior as well as contract terms. If the agreement says the worker chooses their hours but the office manager assigns a fixed daily schedule, include both facts. Do not use a simple scorecard to make a final employee-versus-contractor determination.

Step 1 output: a dated inventory and fact sheet that the practice manager can update when duties, locations or payment arrangements change.

Step 2: Assemble Agreements and Payment Records

Build a review packet that lets advisers trace the arrangement from the agreement to the accounting records. Include the relevant period’s documents and flag missing items explicitly.

Useful records include:

  • Signed agreements, amendments and statements of work.
  • Agency, payroll-provider or employer-of-record service agreements.
  • Invoices, payroll statements, time records and approved reimbursements, as applicable.
  • Bank or payment-provider confirmations, including fees and currency conversion details.
  • Available registration, filing and remittance records associated with the arrangement.
  • Previous written advice and correspondence about unresolved issues.

The IRS guidance on business records supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. It does not establish the overseas obligations for a particular worker.

Reconcile the records to the books

Compare what the practice approved, what the provider reported, what left the bank and what reached the general ledger. Investigate differences rather than recording every bank withdrawal as a single payroll expense.

Where the supporting documents provide detail, distinguish worker compensation, employer charges, provider fees, reimbursements and transfer costs. Ask the CPA how each component should be recorded under the practice’s accounting approach.

For a multi-location practice, document the basis used to allocate shared administrative costs. For a multi-entity practice, identify which entity incurred the expense and which paid it; have the CPA review any intercompany treatment. These accounting decisions help owners understand location profitability but do not resolve employment classification.

Keep patient information out of the review packet

Payroll preparation generally does not require patient names, appointment details or account-level patient balances. Use role descriptions and aggregated workload information where sufficient. HHS provides guidance on the minimum necessary standard, including its exceptions.

Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Any separate review of overseas access to patient information needs its own appropriate privacy and legal assessment.

Step 2 output: an indexed document packet, a reconciliation and a list of missing records. Ask advisers to establish an applicable retention schedule rather than using one universal destruction date.

Step 3: Map Payroll-Provider and Adviser Responsibilities

A provider may process payments without reviewing classification, registrations or every reporting obligation. The owner should know what the engagement covers—and what it excludes.

Read the service agreement and request written confirmation of the provider’s role for the specific country and arrangement. If an agency or employer of record is involved, identify the contracting parties, stated employer and services included. Do not assume the provider takes responsibility for every issue affecting the practice.

Use a responsibility matrix

The following is a starting allocation for review, not a statement of anyone’s contractual duties. Confirm each assignment with the relevant party and add qualified local advisers where needed.

Ask providers how they document completed work, handle rejected payments and notify the practice of missing information. A payment confirmation alone should not be treated as evidence that every related filing or remittance was completed.

Step 3 output: an agreed responsibility matrix, provider scope confirmations and a list of service gaps requiring another adviser.

Step 4: Collect Questions About Classification, Withholding and Registrations

Turn uncertainty into a question log. Avoid asking only, “Are we compliant?” A useful request gives the adviser the relevant facts and asks for a defined decision.

Topic Question to take to the appropriate adviser Supporting records
Classification Does the actual arrangement support the current classification under applicable rules? Agreement, duties, schedule and supervision details
Withholding and reporting What withholding or reporting, if any, applies to this payer, worker and payment arrangement? Entity details, locations, payment history and provider scope
Registrations Does the practice or another party need registrations for this arrangement, and who handles them? Entity structure, work locations and existing registrations
Agency or employer of record Which responsibilities sit with each party, and what remains outside the contract? Service agreement and written scope confirmation
Prior payments Does the historical arrangement require further review or corrective action? Prior invoices, payroll reports, payments and filings
Location changes What should be reassessed if the worker moves or works temporarily elsewhere? Location history and proposed change dates
Records Which records must be kept, by whom and for what applicable period? Current record inventory and provider access terms

Do not ask the office manager to resolve these issues through internet research alone. A U.S. CPA may need to coordinate with local tax specialists, while employment counsel may need jurisdiction-specific support.

Separate the payroll decision from the cost decision

The owner also needs to understand the financial commitment. Build a staff cost view that distinguishes compensation, provider fees, employer costs identified by advisers, transfer charges and any necessary setup or review costs.

Use confirmed amounts where available and label estimates. Do not compare an overseas invoice with a domestic employee’s salary as though both represent total cost. Keep shared administrative costs separate from provider payroll so location-level reports remain useful.

Step 4 output: a prioritized question log showing the issue, supporting evidence, adviser, target response date and decision needed.

Step 5: Establish Adviser-Reviewed Follow-Up

Close the review with written decisions and assigned actions—not just a meeting summary saying the matter was discussed.

For each open issue, record:

  • The adviser’s conclusion and the facts or assumptions supporting it.
  • Any additional specialist review required.
  • The action owner and agreed completion date.
  • Evidence needed to confirm completion.
  • Any change that should trigger reassessment.

For a proposed hire, ask advisers which matters must be resolved before the arrangement begins. For an existing worker, request instructions for handling current payments and any historical issues. Do not improvise withholding changes, relabel past payments or stop payments without appropriate advice.

Build follow-up into the monthly close

The practice manager and finance team can review payment reconciliation, missing provider reports, changes in work location and unresolved adviser questions alongside the monthly close. Keep a separate schedule for filing and payment deadlines confirmed by the responsible advisers or providers.

Revisit the arrangement when duties, supervision, work location, contracting entity or provider scope changes. Agree on periodic review timing with advisers rather than assuming one annual check is sufficient for every situation.

FAQs

Does calling an overseas administrative worker a contractor settle the payroll question?

No. The agreement is part of the evidence, but advisers also need to review how the work actually operates and which rules apply. Document duties, supervision, scheduling and location without treating any single fact as a conclusive test.

Does a payroll provider handle every cross-border obligation?

Do not assume so. Ask for written confirmation of the countries, worker arrangements and services covered. Identify who reviews classification, assesses registrations, processes payments, submits applicable filings and supplies completion records. Assign any gaps to an appropriately qualified adviser.

What should a medical practice prepare before the first adviser meeting?

Bring the payroll fact sheet, signed agreements, provider scope documents, payment history and a reconciliation to the books. Include known location changes, entity mismatches and missing records. A clear factual packet makes the meeting more useful than a general request for compliance reassurance.

Is this the same as reviewing overseas AI use or patient-data access?

No. This workflow addresses staff arrangements, payments and financial records. If overseas staff or technology services access patient information, arrange a separate privacy and legal review. Keep patient-identifiable information out of the payroll packet unless an appropriately reviewed process specifically requires it.

The Owner’s Next Step

Start with one complete fact sheet for each overseas administrative arrangement. Reconcile the payments, confirm provider scope and assign unresolved questions to named advisers. The goal is a reviewable record of what the practice is doing, who is responsible and which decisions still need qualified advice—not a blanket assurance that every cross-border obligation has been met.
