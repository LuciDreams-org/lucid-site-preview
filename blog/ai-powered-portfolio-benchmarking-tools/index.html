AI Portfolio Benchmarking for Medical Practice Reserves

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A strong investment return does not necessarily mean your practice reserves are doing their job. If funds cannot be accessed for provider and administrative payroll, an equipment payment or a new-location buildout, a favorable benchmark comparison offers little comfort.

For owners and managers of staffed US private medical practices, portfolio benchmarking should start with a practical question: Does the comparison reflect what this money must fund, when it must be available and how much uncertainty the practice can accept?

AI may help summarize adviser-provided reports and identify missing information. It cannot establish that a benchmark is suitable merely because a portfolio outperformed it. The useful outcome is a clearer set of questions for your licensed investment adviser—not an AI-generated trading recommendation.

This guide explains how to separate reserve purposes, review performance comparisons and connect adviser reporting to the practice’s cash plan. It does not recommend securities or investment allocations.

Separate practice reserves from owner assets

Before evaluating a benchmark, identify whose money appears in the report and what it is intended to do. A combined household-and-business dashboard can obscure the amount actually available to the practice.

Pool of funds Practice-finance purpose What the review should emphasize
Operating cash and operating reserves Payroll, rent, supplies, debt payments and gaps between payer receipts and expenses Access, stability and ability to meet scheduled obligations
Planned capital reserves Equipment, renovations, expansion or another identified business expenditure Availability at payment dates and exposure to loss before spending
Longer-horizon practice funds Business funds not needed for identified near-term obligations, subject to an agreed mandate Whether risk, access and benchmark remain consistent with future business needs
Longer-horizon owner assets Personal goals funded with assets held outside the practice A separate personal investment mandate and reporting process

Do not count personal portfolio balances as practice liquidity merely because the owner could potentially contribute funds. Any planned contribution should be shown separately, with its timing and availability confirmed.

Likewise, cash held by one practice entity should not automatically be treated as available to another. Identify account ownership and ask the CPA and relevant advisers about any proposed inter-entity movement. A consolidated balance is useful context, but it does not replace entity-level cash planning.

The IRS guidance on business records and separate accounts supports keeping business finances distinct and reconciling bank records. That recordkeeping foundation helps establish which balances belong in the reserve review; it does not determine investment suitability.

Turn the cash plan into a reserve mandate

A benchmark is a reference point for evaluating performance. A reserve mandate describes the job the money must perform. The mandate should come first.

Prepare a short statement for each pool of practice funds covering:

  • Purpose: Which obligations or projects will this money support?
  • Access: When might withdrawals be required, including earlier-than-expected needs?
  • Loss tolerance: What decline would jeopardize payroll, operations or the capital plan?
  • Authority: Who can authorize transfers, and who reviews changes to the mandate?
  • Ownership: Which legal entity owns the account and bears the related obligations?

Build the withdrawal schedule from expected cash receipts and payments, not accounting profit alone. Payer receipts may arrive later than expected, patient balances may remain uncollected and a profitable location may still face a temporary cash shortfall.

For multiple locations, review both local operating needs and shared costs. Centralized administrative payroll, insurance or technology expenses can make individual location cash needs look smaller than the practice’s total commitments.

Illustrative example: one balance, several obligations

Suppose a practice has $600,000 across its business cash and reserve accounts. Its cash plan identifies $250,000 for operating needs, $200,000 for an equipment payment in nine months and $150,000 not assigned to a specific expenditure.

These invented amounts illustrate the review process, not a recommended reserve level or allocation. A single performance figure for the entire balance would conceal the different jobs those funds must perform. Before discussing returns, the owner should ask the adviser to distinguish the operating and equipment needs from the remaining funds and explain the benchmark used for each mandate.

The unassigned balance is not automatically long-term investment money. The practice should first consider possible payer delays, staffing changes and other business commitments.

Decide whether the benchmark is a fair comparison

A benchmark can be familiar and still be unsuitable. Comparing operating reserves with an equity index, for example, does not answer whether payroll funds are available when needed. Comparing assets with materially different interest-rate sensitivity can also make performance differences difficult to interpret.

Ask the adviser to explain benchmark suitability in plain language across three dimensions.

1. Does it reflect the permitted risks?

The benchmark should be consistent with the portfolio’s documented mandate. Ask about differences in asset classes, credit exposure, currency exposure and concentration.

For fixed-income holdings, distinguish maturity from duration. Maturity concerns when an obligation is due; duration is a measure of sensitivity to interest-rate changes. Neither, by itself, tells you how quickly proceeds will reach the practice’s operating account.

A fund classified as “fixed income” should not be assumed to function like immediately available operating cash. Have the adviser explain the holdings and access terms rather than relying on a broad category label.

2. Does it reflect the withdrawal schedule?

A benchmark generally does not share your practice’s exact payment calendar. A favorable long-term comparison may overlook a decline just before an equipment deposit or lease-related payment.

Request a separate view of the amounts expected to be available by each material spending date. Ask what happens if a payment moves forward or payer receipts arrive late. Performance reporting and liquidity planning are related, but one does not substitute for the other.

3. Is the calculation comparable?

Check that portfolio and benchmark returns cover matching dates and use a consistent basis. Ask whether figures include income, whether returns are cumulative or annualized, and which fees are reflected.

Deposits and withdrawals also matter. A rising account balance may reflect new contributions rather than investment gains. Time-weighted returns help separate investment performance from external cash-flow timing; money-weighted returns reflect the timing and size of those flows. Ask the adviser which method is shown and why it is appropriate.

Benchmark-review worksheet

Complete this worksheet with the practice manager, finance team and licensed investment adviser. Use “not provided” where information is missing rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

Review item What to record Question for the adviser
Mandate Purpose, owning entity, approved risks and spending commitments What makes this benchmark consistent with the written mandate?
Access to funds Withdrawal process, restrictions, settlement timing and transfer steps When would cleared funds reach the operating account, including under stressed conditions?
Duration and maturity Reported duration, maturity information and material differences from the benchmark Could rate sensitivity conflict with our expected spending dates?
Concentration Material issuer, sector, fund, custodian or other exposures, as applicable Which concentrations does the benchmark comparison fail to reveal?
Fees Adviser, account, custody, underlying investment and applicable transaction costs Which costs are included in reported returns, and which are excluded?
Periods compared Exact start and end dates, inception dates and annualized or cumulative presentation Are both sides measured over the same period and on the same basis?
Cash-flow treatment Contributions, withdrawals, income and return methodology How have practice deposits and spending affected the figures?
Benchmark construction Index name, components, weights and rebalancing method if blended How was it selected, and has it changed during the reporting history?
Downside and liquidity Declines during the period and funds available at key dates Could we meet the withdrawal plan without disrupting operations?
Follow-up Missing documents, responsible person and next review point What must be clarified before the owner approves any change?

If a benchmark has changed, ask for the reason and effective date. Preserve that explanation with the report so comparisons across reporting periods remain understandable.

Use AI to prepare questions—not approve investments

AI may help organize a dense adviser report into a consistent review format. Useful tasks include extracting stated benchmarks, listing reporting dates, summarizing disclosed fees and highlighting fields absent from the worksheet.

A bounded request might be:

Summarize the benchmark, reporting period, return methodology, stated fees and access restrictions explicitly described in this report. Identify the source page for each item. Mark missing information as “not provided.” Compare the report with the supplied practice withdrawal schedule and list questions for the adviser. Do not infer guarantees or recommend investments.

Even with that instruction, check every material statement against the original document.

Errors that require human review

AI can misread a table, combine figures from different periods or confuse a benchmark return with the account’s return. It may classify all bond holdings as cash-like, treat an account value as fully accessible or interpret yield as total return.

It can also ignore withdrawal needs unless those needs are supplied explicitly. A summary saying that a portfolio “performed well” is incomplete if it never addresses the practice’s upcoming payments.

Keep the adviser’s report as the source of record. Label the AI summary as a draft and ask the adviser to resolve unclear classifications, calculations or access terms. An AI summary is not a transaction authorization.

Limit the information used

Reserve benchmarking ordinarily calls for account-level financial information and aggregate cash requirements—not patient-identifiable billing records. Remove unnecessary account identifiers and other sensitive information before using any approved analysis workflow.

Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool. Where financial work involves protected health information, obtain appropriate privacy and contractual guidance. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; it does not establish that a particular tool is suitable for handling practice data.

Make the review part of the finance workflow

Start each review by reconciling account balances and updating the cash forecast. Then compare the report with the current mandate and withdrawal schedule. Revisit the review when a capital project changes, a new location opens or operating needs materially shift—not just when markets move.

Keep responsibilities clear:

  • Owner: Approves the business purpose, acceptable constraints and authorized decisions.
  • Practice manager: Updates payment timing and operational commitments.
  • CPA or finance team: Maintains reconciled books, entity-level visibility and the business cash plan.
  • Licensed investment adviser: Explains investment risks, benchmark selection, fees and suitability within the agreed mandate.

Conclusion

The right benchmark helps explain whether reserve assets are being managed consistently with their purpose. It does not override payroll needs, capital commitments or entity-level constraints.

Separate practice funds from personal assets, document withdrawal needs and require comparable reporting. Use AI to organize evidence and questions, then have the appropriate human advisers resolve uncertainty before decisions are made.

FAQs

Should operating reserves use the same benchmark as the owner’s personal portfolio?

Not by default. Operating reserves support business obligations, while personal assets may have different goals and time horizons. Keep the mandates and reporting separate, and ask the licensed adviser to explain the benchmark selected for each.

What if practice reserves outperform their benchmark but cannot fund an upcoming payment?

The performance comparison has not answered the liquidity question. Confirm the withdrawal process, timing and potential costs with the adviser, and update the cash plan. Reassess whether the mandate and reporting adequately reflect the practice’s spending commitments.

Can AI determine whether a reserve benchmark is suitable?

AI can summarize disclosed information and flag apparent mismatches, but it can miss restrictions, misclassify assets or overlook withdrawals. Suitability requires review of the actual mandate, holdings and business needs with the licensed investment adviser. Verify AI-generated figures against the original reports.
