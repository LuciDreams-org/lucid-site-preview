Equity Dilution vs. Debt Costs for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice considering equipment purchases, a location expansion or working capital needs more than a comparison of an interest rate with an ownership percentage. Debt creates payment obligations and contractual exposure. Equity changes who participates in the practice’s economics and decisions. Those costs operate differently and cannot be reduced to a single percentage without substantial assumptions.

For a staffed private practice, the decision also affects resources available for provider and administrative payroll, owner distributions and future investment. Before comparing proposals, establish whether the proposed equity participant and arrangement are permissible. Healthcare counsel must review eligibility and the proposed structure; this article does not assume outside investment is allowed.

The purpose here is to compare financing costs and control rights—not to build a month-by-month debt-versus-owner-contribution cash forecast. Start with the proposed terms, identify what each party receives, and bring the unresolved questions to your CPA, lender and healthcare counsel.

Start With the Same Funding Need and Entity

Two proposals are not comparable if they fund different needs or involve different entities.

Write a short financing brief that identifies:

  • Use of funds: Equipment, build-out, working capital or another defined purpose.
  • Recipient: The specific entity receiving the loan or equity contribution.
  • Net usable proceeds: The amount available after fees and closing costs.
  • Affected operations: Which locations benefit and which entities carry the obligations.
  • Owner objectives: Retaining voting authority, limiting personal exposure, preserving distributions or admitting an eligible long-term participant.

Distinguish money paid into the practice from money paid to an existing owner. A purchase of an owner’s existing interest may provide that owner with liquidity but provide no new capital to the practice. A newly issued interest can fund the practice while diluting existing owners. A transaction can contain both components, which should be shown separately.

For a multi-entity group, do not assume cash held by one entity is freely available to another. Identify the borrower, equity issuer, guarantors and any proposed intercompany transfers for adviser review.

What Debt Actually Costs

Separate repayment from financing expense

A loan’s principal repayment uses cash, but it is not the same as interest expense. Comparing all principal and interest payments with an equity participant’s distributions would mix repayment of borrowed capital with the cost of obtaining that capital.

Review these components separately:

Debt component Economic significance What to verify
Principal Capital that must be returned Amortization schedule, maturity and balloon balance
Interest Charge for using borrowed money Fixed or variable rate, calculation method and reset terms
Fees Additional financing costs Origination, closing, ongoing and early-repayment charges
Collateral Assets exposed under the agreement Scope of liens and release conditions
Guarantees Potential exposure beyond the borrower Who guarantees, what is covered and when release is possible
Covenants Restrictions that can limit flexibility Reporting, liquidity, additional borrowing and distribution conditions

For a variable-rate proposal, ask the lender to show how rate changes affect payments or maturity obligations. Do not treat the initial payment as the maximum possible commitment.

Ownership can remain unchanged while flexibility decreases

A conventional loan without equity-linked features does not itself dilute ownership. That does not mean the practice retains unrestricted financial discretion.

A loan agreement may limit distributions, require financial reporting or condition additional borrowing on lender approval. Ask counsel to explain defaults, remedies, notice requirements and any cure opportunities. Examine whether the lender can reach assets or guarantees associated with another location or entity.

For a practice with uneven payer receipts, payment timing also matters. A profitable month on the income statement does not necessarily produce cash before payroll and the loan payment are due. Keep that liquidity assessment separate from the financing-cost calculation.

What Equity Actually Costs

Dilution is an allocation of future economics

When a practice issues a new ownership interest, existing owners generally hold a smaller percentage afterward. They may then receive a smaller share of distributions and future transaction proceeds, depending on the governing agreements.

But a smaller percentage does not, by itself, establish a dollar loss. The practice receives new capital, and its future value remains uncertain. Avoid describing equity as automatically more expensive than debt—or as free money because it lacks a conventional loan payment.

The actual bargain depends on valuation and the rights attached to the interest.

Review more than the headline percentage

Before treating a proposed interest as a simple share of profits, identify:

  • Whether distributions are discretionary or subject to agreed requirements.
  • Whether any participant receives priority distributions or proceeds.
  • Whether voting rights match economic ownership.
  • Which decisions require the participant’s consent.
  • Whether future issuances create additional dilution or participation rights.
  • How transfers, departures, disability, death and buyouts are addressed.
  • Whether any redemption or purchase obligation could create future cash demands.

Separate compensation for work from returns on ownership. A new physician-owner’s compensation arrangement is not interchangeable with an ownership distribution. The CPA and counsel should review those components separately rather than treating every payment to the participant as a financing cost.

Illustrative Financing Comparison

The following is a hypothetical example, not a lender quote, valuation opinion, permissible ownership recommendation or return prediction. It ignores taxes and assumes counsel has confirmed that the proposed equity participant and arrangement are permissible.

A practice needs $300,000 of usable project funding. It compares these simplified alternatives:

Item Hypothetical debt proposal Hypothetical equity proposal
Capital received by practice $300,000 $300,000
Main terms Five-year loan at a fixed 8% annual rate, with monthly amortization New interest issued at a $1,200,000 pre-contribution valuation
Upfront financing fee $6,000, paid separately from existing cash None assumed for this illustration
Monthly loan payment Approximately $6,083 No scheduled loan payment assumed
Total scheduled principal and interest Approximately $364,975 Not applicable
Interest over the loan term Approximately $64,975 Not applicable
Interest plus stated fee Approximately $70,975 No fixed equivalent; economic participation depends on future results and agreed rights
New participant’s ownership None 20% after the contribution

The loan calculation assumes standard monthly amortization, no missed payments and no early repayment. The $6,000 fee is paid outside the loan, so both alternatives deliver $300,000 for the project, but debt also requires that upfront cash. Actual transaction and advisory costs would need to be added to both proposals.

The $364,975 debt repayment total is not directly comparable to a 20% equity interest. It includes returning the $300,000 borrowed. The approximately $70,975 represents interest and the stated fee, before taxes and other costs; it does not price collateral, guarantee or covenant exposure.

The equity alternative has no fixed financing-cost total under these assumptions. Its economics depend on future distributions, transaction proceeds, negotiated rights and any later buyout.

For another explicitly hypothetical illustration, if $100,000 were available for a strictly pro rata distribution after closing, a 20% participant would receive $20,000. If $1,000,000 were available for pro rata allocation to owners in a future transaction—after liabilities and transaction costs—the participant’s share would be $200,000. These are allocation examples, not forecasts or a cumulative cost estimate.

Build an Ownership-Percentage Bridge

The simplified equity calculation is:

New participant’s percentage = contribution ÷ post-contribution value

In the hypothetical example, $300,000 ÷ ($1,200,000 + $300,000) = 20%.

Assume the practice initially has 1,000 identical units, held 80% by Owner A and 20% by Owner B. At the assumed valuation, issuing 250 new units for $300,000 produces this bridge:

Owner Units before Ownership before Units after Ownership after
Owner A 800 80% 800 64%
Owner B 200 20% 200 16%
New eligible participant 0 0% 250 20%
Total 1,000 100% 1,250 100%

Owner A’s interest falls by 16 percentage points, from 80% to 64%. That is a 20% relative reduction in Owner A’s ownership percentage—not a 20-percentage-point reduction.

This bridge assumes identical economic rights, no other issuances and no special preferences. Actual agreements may produce different voting, distribution or sale-proceeds allocations. Have counsel reconcile the bridge to the documents before relying on it.

Compare Control and Exposure Alongside Cost

Use a decision sheet that keeps financial obligations and governance rights visible together.

Decision area Debt questions Equity questions
Payroll and operating liquidity Can collections support payments without relying on unusually strong receipt months? Can the practice retain working capital under the distribution terms?
Owner distributions Are distributions restricted by the loan? Who approves distributions, and how are they allocated?
Location decisions Do borrowing limits or collateral terms affect expansion or closure? Does the participant have approval rights over locations or budgets?
Future financing Is lender consent required for additional obligations? Can new interests be issued, and who may participate?
Owner departure or sale What payoff and guarantee-release terms apply? What transfer, valuation, buyout and proceeds-allocation provisions apply?

Neither option wins every category. Debt may preserve ownership percentages while introducing repayment pressure. Equity may reduce scheduled debt service while sharing future economics and decision authority.

Prepare an Adviser-Ready Review Package

Clean records make proposed terms easier to evaluate. The IRS guidance on starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals also help distinguish practice funds, owner transactions and financing proceeds.

Prepare:

  • Reconciled entity-level balance sheets and income statements.
  • Per-location results, with shared-cost allocation methods explained.
  • Aggregate payer receipts, patient-balance collections and receivables aging, without unnecessary patient identifiers.
  • Provider and administrative payroll totals, existing debt schedules and owner distributions.
  • Current ownership records, governing agreements and proposed financing documents.
  • A schedule of fees, collateral, guarantees, consent rights and unresolved terms.

Ask healthcare counsel whether the proposed participant and arrangement are permissible and what ownership, governance and transaction restrictions require attention.

Ask the lender for net proceeds, the full repayment schedule, variable-rate mechanics where applicable, collateral requirements, covenant definitions and default terms.

Ask the CPA to reconcile the financing comparison to the books, separate principal from expense, evaluate tax treatment and identify how owner compensation and distributions affect the analysis. Do not assume a universal interest deduction or apply a standard tax adjustment to every practice.

Make the Decision From the Terms, Not the Label

Before signing, compare net usable capital, scheduled obligations, downside exposure, ownership changes and approval rights on one page. Resolve legal eligibility before treating equity as an available alternative.

The practical question is not simply whether debt or equity is cheaper. It is whether the practice can accept the proposed payments, risks and sharing of future economics while protecting operating needs and the owners’ priorities.

FAQs

Is equity cheaper because there is no monthly loan payment?

Not necessarily. Equity can reduce scheduled repayment pressure, but the participant may share in distributions and future transaction proceeds. Some agreements also create buyout or redemption obligations. Evaluate the actual rights and obligations rather than assuming equity has no cash cost.

Does borrowing preserve complete owner control?

A conventional loan without equity-linked features generally leaves ownership percentages unchanged. However, covenants, liens and guarantees can restrict financial choices and create exposure. Review the agreement’s limits on distributions, borrowing and other major decisions.

Can a medical practice accept any outside equity investor?

Do not assume so. Healthcare counsel should review the proposed participant, entity, jurisdiction and arrangement before the owners negotiate on the premise that the investment is permissible. Financial attractiveness does not establish legal eligibility.

Can I calculate one break-even point between debt and dilution?

Only after defining assumptions about distributions, transaction proceeds, timing, taxes, buyout terms and risk. A comparison of stated loan interest with an ownership percentage is incomplete. Keep the contractual debt schedule separate from hypothetical equity allocations, and ask the CPA to explain which conclusions depend on uncertain future outcomes.
