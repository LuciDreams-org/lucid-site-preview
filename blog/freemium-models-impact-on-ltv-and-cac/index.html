Free Offers and Acquisition Costs in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A free educational session or nonclinical resource can introduce people to a private medical practice. But “free” describes the attendee’s price—not the practice’s cost. Planning, promotion, administrative follow-up and staff delivery all consume resources before any related payer or patient receipts arrive.

For an owner or practice manager, the decision is not whether free content can attract attention. It is whether a specific offer produces enough observable financial value to justify its cost, without disrupting operations or relying on speculative lifetime patient spending.

That requires a different approach from software freemium models. Measure a defined campaign, count attributable costs, examine subsequent collected contribution and acknowledge what the results cannot prove.

Why SaaS freemium economics do not transfer cleanly

Software businesses may offer a free tier and charge for additional features or usage. Their analysis often connects free-to-paid conversion, customer acquisition cost (CAC) and subscription lifetime value (LTV).

A staffed medical practice has different revenue mechanics and capacity constraints.

Software freemium assumption Medical-practice reality Financial implication
A free account can upgrade to a paid subscription An educational attendee may never seek care, may already be a patient or may use another practice Attendance is not acquisition
Subscription prices define recurring revenue Collections depend on services, payer arrangements, patient responsibility and receipt timing Use actual collections, not a subscription-style revenue assumption
More usage can lead to a larger subscription Additional care must reflect clinical need and appropriate access Do not make increased utilization the campaign’s objective
Serving another free user may have limited delivery cost Events and follow-up use staff time and may compete with operational work Include labor and capacity constraints
Retention supports an estimated lifetime value Future care, payer mix and patient relationships are uncertain Do not justify current losses with projected lifetime patient spending

A free practice resource should stand on its educational purpose. It should not create a ladder in which necessary care is withheld behind paid tiers.

The useful concept to retain from freemium analysis is narrower: a no-charge offer has an acquisition cost, and that cost needs an evidence-based financial test.

Define the offer before approving a budget

Start with a specific nonclinical offering, such as a general orientation to practice operations or a resource explaining common insurance and payment terminology. Define its scope so staff do not turn an educational activity into individualized clinical advice.

Document the intended audience, responsible manager, participating location, budget and observation window. Separate existing patients from people new to the practice; an offer may be useful to both groups, but their financial effects differ.

Before launch, have qualified counsel assess any proposed discounts, inducements or promotional arrangements, including relevant referral or sponsor arrangements. A positive financial worksheet is not legal clearance. This article does not establish that any particular offer is permissible.

Also decide what success means. A community-education initiative can be approved as a budgeted expense without claiming that it pays for itself through acquisition. If acquisition is the purpose, however, the owner needs a collection-based test rather than downloads or registrations alone.

Count costs that disappear inside ordinary payroll

Advertising invoices are visible. Staff time is easier to overlook because it may already be included in payroll.

For campaign analysis, record attributable work even when it does not create an additional paycheck. Administrative staff may spend time on registration, reminders and follow-up instead of their usual responsibilities. A provider or manager may prepare and present the material during time that could support other work.

Use a consistent loaded hourly cost for assigned staff time. Keep that resource cost separate from incremental cash spending so the owner can see both the cash requirement and the operational burden.

Cost category What to include Main caution
Content and preparation Writing, review, design and presentation preparation Separate reusable setup work from each repeat event
Promotion Campaign-specific advertising, printing and vendor charges Do not count the same invoice in several campaigns
Administration Registration, reminders, coordination and permitted follow-up Include attributable salaried staff time
Delivery Presenter time, room rental and event-specific supplies Distinguish existing payroll from extra cash spending
Subsequent service delivery Costs attributable to completed services, including applicable labor, supplies and processing costs Deduct these when calculating contribution, not again as campaign cost

If the event causes overtime or requires temporary coverage, include that additional spending. If it displaces appointments, document the capacity concern separately; do not automatically assume every blocked appointment would have produced collectible revenue.

Measure collected contribution, not charges

Gross charges do not show what a campaign returned. Booked appointments do not either: appointments may be canceled, and completed services may remain unpaid at the review date.

Use the following management calculation:

Observed collected contribution = receipts associated with the defined completed-service cohort − attributable service-delivery costs.

Then calculate:

Campaign result = observed collected contribution − attributable campaign resource cost.

Define the service scope and collection cutoff in advance. Do not mix charges for recent visits with cash collected from older, unrelated services. Include applicable refunds and payment reversals in the receipt calculation, and flag unresolved balances rather than treating them as cash.

This is a campaign contribution analysis, not a complete practice profit calculation. Rent, baseline administrative overhead and other fixed costs still need funding. If expansion would require additional staffing or space, incorporate those step-up costs before scaling.

For acquisition cost, use a clearly labeled denominator:

Observed campaign acquisition cost = campaign resource cost ÷ qualifying new patients with a completed visit.

Exclude registrations, inquiries, duplicate records, existing patients and no-shows from that denominator. A completed visit can count toward this observed acquisition measure even if payment is pending, but the collection analysis must show that payment limitation.

Hypothetical campaign worksheet

Illustrative example only: A private practice runs a free, nonclinical educational session. The figures below are invented to demonstrate the worksheet, not to provide benchmarks or expected results.

The practice sets a 120-day observation window from the event and reviews both completed visits and collections at that cutoff. This is a management choice for the example, not a recommended universal period.

Campaign input Illustrative amount Treatment
Advertising $900 Incremental cash spending
External design $450 Incremental cash spending
Event-specific tools and materials $150 Incremental cash spending
Administrative time $600 Attributable existing-payroll resource cost
Preparation and presentation time $400 Attributable existing-payroll resource cost
Total campaign resource cost $2,500 Includes $1,500 of incremental cash spending and $1,000 of assigned payroll time

The practice then measures the cohort rather than applying a software conversion benchmark.

Observed result Illustrative amount Interpretation
Attendees 80 Engagement, not acquired patients
New patients with a completed visit linked to the campaign 20 Observed acquisition denominator; causation is not established
Receipts for the defined completed services through cutoff $6,000 Collected amount, not billed charges
Attributable service-delivery costs $2,600 Separate from event preparation and administration
Observed collected contribution $3,400 $6,000 − $2,600
Campaign result after resource cost $900 $3,400 − $2,500
Observed acquisition cost per completed new patient $125 $2,500 ÷ 20

The result is encouraging but not conclusive. It does not prove that all related visits were incremental, that future events will perform similarly or that the practice earned $900 of net profit after all overhead.

The distinction between cash and resource cost also matters. Existing payroll time may not create an additional campaign cash payment, but excluding it would make the activity appear less costly than it is to operate repeatedly.

Test attribution before repeating the offer

A person can attend an event and later visit the practice without the event causing that visit. They may already have planned to schedule, received a referral or encountered several marketing channels.

Choose an attribution rule before launch. For example, record a campaign identifier through an approved administrative workflow and distinguish new patients who report the event as their source from those with uncertain or competing sources. Count each person once under the chosen rule and keep existing-patient activity separate.

Maintain an “uncertain” category rather than forcing every visit into the campaign’s success column.

Illustrative sensitivity test using the worksheet above: Suppose eight of the twenty completed new-patient relationships have uncertain attribution. For this simplified test only, assume contribution is evenly distributed at $170 per patient. Counting only the twelve clearer links yields $2,040 of contribution against $2,500 of campaign resource cost—a $460 shortfall.

Actual contribution may differ substantially by service and payer, so use cohort-specific amounts when available. The point is that uncertain attribution can change the decision even when the headline result looks positive. A reported campaign source is evidence of association, not proof of incremental demand.

State the observation limits

At each review, identify:

  • Claims or patient balances not yet collected, without assuming they will be paid in full.
  • Late refunds or reversals that could change the result.
  • Existing marketing or referral activity that overlaps with the campaign.
  • Appointment constraints, seasonality or staffing changes affecting the comparison.
  • Whether linked visits filled available capacity or displaced other demand.

A later collection review may improve the evidence. It should not become an open-ended search for enough future spending to make the campaign appear profitable.

Use a stop-or-redesign framework

Agree on spending authority, a review date and a tolerable downside before launching. There is no universal CAC target or LTV:CAC ratio that makes an educational offer worthwhile for every practice.

Finding at review Decision Next step
Collected contribution covers resource cost, attribution is reasonably supported and capacity remains available Consider a limited repeat Keep the budget controlled and test whether results repeat
Results depend heavily on uncertain attribution Redesign measurement Improve source capture before increasing spending
Collections remain immature Hold expansion Set a later cutoff without assuming outstanding balances will convert to cash
Engagement is high but completed visits or contribution are weak Redesign the offer or audience Assess whether the activity serves a separate education objective
Staff burden interferes with normal operations Pause or simplify Reduce delivery work and reassess capacity
Results remain negative after a defined review Stop acquisition spending or explicitly fund another purpose Do not justify losses with speculative lifetime value
Legal review identifies unresolved concerns Do not proceed as proposed Obtain qualified counsel’s guidance before reconsidering the arrangement

A community resource can remain worthwhile as an explicitly budgeted educational activity. That is a different decision from declaring it a financially successful acquisition campaign.

Keep the analysis connected to the books

Assign a campaign code or supporting worksheet, reconcile vendor spending and retain support for staff-time allocations. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records and reconciling bank records; campaign analysis should tie back to those records rather than exist as an unsupported marketing spreadsheet.

For multiple locations or entities, document which location incurred costs and which entity received collections. Allocate shared preparation costs using a consistent, documented basis, and avoid counting the same receipts in several campaign reports.

Use aggregate or appropriately de-identified financial reporting where possible. Patient-linked source tracking should stay within approved workflows. The HHS minimum necessary guidance is relevant to limiting information use where that standard applies; its exceptions and circumstances matter. Do not place patient-identifiable campaign or billing data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

The owner’s decision

This analysis is not a broad acquisition-improvement checklist or a recurring-revenue dashboard. It answers a narrower question: Should this particular free offer receive another allocation of practice money and staff time?

Decide using attributable costs, observed collections, service-delivery contribution and an honest account of uncertainty. A small, measurable test is more useful than a confident forecast of lifetime patient spending.

FAQs

Can a medical practice use a freemium model?

A practice can evaluate a no-charge nonclinical resource, but software-style free and paid tiers are not a suitable template for access to necessary care. Have qualified counsel assess proposed discounts, inducements and promotional arrangements before launch. This financial framework provides no legal clearance.

Should acquisition cost include staff who are already on payroll?

Yes, include attributable staff time in the resource-cost view. Also show incremental cash spending separately. This lets the owner distinguish the immediate cash requirement from the workload needed to repeat the offer. Do not count the same labor again in service-delivery costs.

How long should a practice wait before evaluating the campaign?

Choose a window based on the practice’s scheduling and collection patterns, then disclose what remains unresolved. An early review can show workload and completed visits; a later review can show more mature collections. Neither should treat outstanding balances or future care as guaranteed revenue.

Does a positive campaign result justify expansion?

Not by itself. Check attribution, repeatability, available appointment capacity and any added staffing or space costs. A positive contribution result also does not mean all practice overhead has been covered. Expand cautiously only after those limitations are understood.
