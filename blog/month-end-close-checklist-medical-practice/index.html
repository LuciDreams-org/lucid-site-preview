What should my books look like at the end of every month?

December 4, 2026 · 8 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 4, 2026

At the end of each month, your books should agree with every bank and card statement, agree with what your practice software says you collected, and show each cost under the right location and provider. A month‑end close is how you get there: 12 steps, each with a named person and a business day to finish by.

Key points

  • You'll reconcile every account each month: checking, savings, payroll, credit cards and loans.
  • You'll match insurance deposits to the payer's remittance advice, not to the day you posted the payment.
  • In Example 1, a practice traces a $1,430 gap in one week to $250 of card fees and $1,180 of insurance timing.

What's on a month‑end close checklist for a medical practice?

A month‑end close checklist for a practice has about 12 steps, from closing out the last day's payments to locking the month. Your office manager handles the first two on business day 1, your bookkeeper does the reconciling and coding by business day 6, and you review and sign off by business day 8.

Step Who Business day
1. Post the last day's payments and close the final card batch Office manager 1
2. Pull the practice software's month‑end reports: payments, adjustments, refunds, aging Office manager or biller 1
3. Reconcile every bank account to its statement Bookkeeper 3
4. Reconcile every credit card and loan account to its statement Bookkeeper 3
5. Match deposits to collections: card batches, insurance payments, patient checks Bookkeeper 3
6. Record card processing fees and bank fees Bookkeeper 3
7. Code every expense and send the owner a list of unknown charges Bookkeeper 4
8. Record payroll from the payroll company's reports Bookkeeper 4
9. Split loan payments into principal and interest; list new equipment Bookkeeper 5
10. Split shared costs across locations and providers Bookkeeper 5
11. Tie receivables in the books to the aging report; review refunds and credit balances Bookkeeper with the biller 6
12. Review the reports against last month, sign off and lock the month Owner, then bookkeeper 8

Business day 1 is the first working day of the next month. You'll answer the step 7 questions by business day 5. If your bookkeeper finishes a step late, move the sign‑off day back rather than skip a step. For step 11 in detail, see the receivables part of the close. What is a month‑end close?

A month‑end close is the set of steps you take after a month ends to check, correct and lock that month's books, so the reports you read are right. Every practice needs one, whether you keep the books in QuickBooks Online, Xero or another system.

These are the terms you'll see in this post:

  • A bank reconciliation is the check that every transaction on a bank statement is in your books, and every bank transaction in your books is on the statement.
  • A card batch is the group of card payments your front desk settles at the end of a day, which your card processor deposits as one amount, minus fees.
  • A remittance advice is the payer's statement listing which claims one insurance payment covers. The electronic version is called an ERA.
  • An allocation is a written rule for splitting one shared cost between locations or providers.
  • An open‑items list is the list of amounts nobody has explained yet, with the person checking each one and the day it's due.
  • Locking the month is setting a closing date in your accounting software, so nobody can change that month without a password.

How do I match my bank deposits to my practice software's collections report?

Match by payment type, not by day. Your card processor deposits each batch minus fees, insurance payers deposit when they pay, often days after you post, and patient checks reach the bank when someone takes them. Once you've matched each type, any gap left should be fees or timing you can name.

Medicare pays claims by electronic funds transfer, and payers send the electronic remittance advice in a national format called the 835 (CMS, Electronic Funds Transfer). Your bookkeeper matches each insurance deposit to its remittance advice by payer and amount, then lists what's left: deposits with no posting, and postings with no deposit.

Example 1: matching one week of deposits. A two‑provider practice posts $24,530 of payments in its practice software for Monday, November 2 to Friday, November 6, 2026. The bank shows $23,100 of deposits for the same week.

Payment type Practice software Bank deposits Difference and why
Card batches $8,420 $8,170 $250 of processing fees, taken out before the deposit
Insurance payments $14,960 $13,780 $1,180 from one payer, posted Friday and deposited the next Monday
Patient checks $1,150 $1,150 $0
Total $24,530 $23,100 $1,430, all of it named

Your bookkeeper records the $250 as a card fee expense and carries the $1,180 into the next week's match. Anything still unnamed goes on the open‑items list for your office manager.

How should a practice with more than one location split shared costs?

Pick one written rule for each shared cost, such as each office's share of visits, revenue or square feet, and use it every month. Code direct costs, like each office's rent, straight to that office. Then you can read a profit and loss for each location and see which one is losing money.

Example 2: splitting shared costs between two offices. A primary care practice has two offices, north and south, with one provider in each and one office manager for both. The practice keeps one set of books in QuickBooks Online, with a location tag for each office. In November, the north office had 1,100 visits and the south office 900, so the bookkeeper splits two shared costs 55% and 45%.

Shared cost November total North office (55%) South office (45%)
Office manager's pay $5,200 $2,860 $2,340
Practice software $900 $495 $405
Total $6,100 $3,355 $2,745

If the bookkeeper coded all $6,100 to the north office, the north office's profit and loss would carry $2,745 of the south office's costs, and the south office would look $2,745 more profitable than it is.

Change the allocation only at the start of a year, so you can compare one month with the next. For more on groups, see accounting for practices with more than one location. If each office is its own company with its own bank account, read how to keep books for several practices.

How do I know when my books are done each month?

Your books are done when your bookkeeper has finished all 12 steps, you've signed off, and someone has locked the month in the accounting software. Pick a fixed sign‑off day, such as business day 8, and ask for a short note listing anything still open.

On sign‑off day, ask for a reconciliation for every account, the open‑items list and the reports. Then read the results with how to read a P&L for your medical practice.

Save each month's statements, reconciliations, reports and open‑items list. The IRS says "You must keep your records as long as needed to prove the income or deductions on a tax return" (IRS, Recordkeeping).

What do you add to the close in December?

The December close is a normal close plus a short year‑end list: payroll and contractor records for Forms W‑2 and 1099, a count of any product on the shelf, an updated list of equipment, and a clean‑up of old open items. Your CPA will use all of it to prepare the year's tax returns.

  • Payroll and Form W‑2: check each employee's name, address and year‑to‑date pay. For year‑end bonuses, see how bonuses are taxed for practice owners.
  • Contractors and Form 1099‑NEC: collect a Form W‑9 from each contractor you paid, and total what you paid each one. For who gets one, see who gets a 1099 from a medical practice in 2027.
  • Product count: count injectables, retail product and supplies on the shelf on December 31.
  • Equipment list: list each piece of equipment you bought during the year, with its date and cost, so your CPA can figure depreciation (IRS, Publication 946).
  • Old open items: clear or explain every uncleared check and old deposit before you lock December.

Tax rules depend on your state and your numbers, so ask your CPA before you act on this.

Questions practice owners ask about the month‑end close

What is a month‑end close?

A month‑end close is the work you do after each month ends to make that month's books complete and correct, then lock them. For a practice, that means reconciling accounts, matching deposits, coding expenses and splitting shared costs. Once you've closed a month, you can compare its profit and loss with the month before.

How long should a month‑end close take for a practice?

Plan on a fixed sign‑off day in the first two weeks of the next month; the checklist above signs off on business day 8. The hours depend on how many accounts, locations and transactions you have. If your close often runs past the 15th, ask your bookkeeper what's holding it up.

How do I match insurance deposits to the payments in my practice software?

Match each insurance deposit to that payer's remittance advice by amount, then check that the payments posted in your practice software add up to the same total. One deposit can cover dozens of claims. If you can't find the remittance for a deposit, ask the payer or your billing company for it before anyone posts the payment.

What's the difference between a bank reconciliation and a close?

A bank reconciliation is one step of the close: you check one account against one statement. The close covers every account, plus steps no bank statement shows, like coding expenses, recording payroll and splitting shared costs. You can have a reconciled bank account and still have a profit and loss that's wrong.

What extra steps happen at year end?

At year end, you add a short list to the December close: payroll records for Forms W‑2, contractor details for Forms 1099, a product count, the equipment list and old open items. Start in early December, since staff and contractors can take weeks to send you what you need.

Who should do the close: the office manager, a bookkeeper or a CPA?

Usually all three, each on different steps. Your office manager closes out payments and pulls reports. A bookkeeper does the reconciling, coding and cost splitting. A CPA reviews the year and prepares the tax returns. Split the work so the person who records payments isn't the only one who checks them.
