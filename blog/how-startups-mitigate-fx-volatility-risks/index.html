Managing Foreign Exchange Risk in Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Foreign exchange risk matters to a US medical practice when a documented obligation or receipt changes in dollar value as exchange rates move. An overseas vendor alone does not establish that exposure: the key questions are which currency the contract requires and when the dollar cost becomes fixed.

For a staffed private practice collecting payer receipts and patient balances in dollars, a foreign-currency equipment invoice can create a mismatch. Payroll, rent, and the vendor payment draw on the same dollar cash balance, but the vendor payment may become more expensive before it is due.

The owner's decision is not whether to predict the next currency move. It is how to protect operating cash while meeting an actual contractual obligation. Start by documenting the exposure, estimating its cash sensitivity, and comparing payment terms—not by taking a currency position.

Establish Whether Your Practice Has Actual Currency Exposure

Review signed contracts, open purchase orders, unpaid invoices, and any foreign-currency bank balances. Possible exposures include equipment purchases, software subscriptions, or other vendor services explicitly priced in a foreign currency. Include foreign-currency receipts only if your practice actually has them.

Separate three concepts:

Type What it means Practice-owner response
Transaction exposure The dollar amount required to settle a foreign-currency invoice or collect a receivable can change before conversion or payment Track the currency, amount, settlement date, and cash sensitivity
Accounting translation Converting foreign-currency financial information into dollars for reporting can change reported balances Ask the CPA which accounting treatment applies; do not assume every reporting change requires a cash payment
Speculative currency position Holding or trading currency beyond documented business needs to profit from exchange-rate changes Keep it outside the practice's invoice-payment process; this article does not recommend currency trading

A foreign vendor issuing a fixed dollar invoice may leave the practice with no direct currency conversion requirement for that invoice. However, a contract that permits currency-related repricing can affect future costs. Record that provision separately rather than treating a fixed current invoice and a possible future price change as the same exposure.

Build an Exposure Register From Source Documents

Use a shared financial worksheet or your existing accounting workflow to maintain an exposure register. Each entry should lead back to an invoice, contract, or other supporting document.

Field What to record Why it matters
Responsible entity Legal entity that owes or receives the payment Keeps obligations and cash forecasts assigned correctly
Location or shared cost Benefiting location, or the approved shared-cost allocation Supports useful location-level profitability reporting
Vendor and document reference Vendor name, invoice reference, and contract reference Helps prevent duplicate or unsupported payments
Currency and amount Contractual currency and outstanding foreign-currency amount Defines the exposure before estimating dollars
Payment schedule Deposit, installments, final due date, and processing lead time Identifies when cash must be available
Conversion basis Rate convention, quote time, and whether a rate is indicative or executable Makes comparisons consistent
Payment costs Conversion spread, transfer charges, and any intermediary or recipient-bank deductions Captures costs beyond the headline rate
Contract terms Currency choice, quote expiry, repricing rights, cancellation terms, and early-payment provisions Establishes which alternatives are actually available
Decision and approval Selected approach, approver, and supporting quote Connects the payment to an authorized business decision

Confirm what the vendor must receive. If intermediary fees reduce the amount delivered, an apparently complete payment may leave an unpaid balance. Ask the bank or payment provider how fees are charged rather than assuming the quoted transfer fee covers every deduction.

A finance register generally does not need patient-identifiable information. Keep it focused on vendor, entity, payment, and accounting details. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools for this analysis.

Put Exposure Dates Beside Payroll and Expected Collections

An invoice due date is not necessarily the day to begin arranging payment. Bank processing, vendor receipt requirements, and approval steps can require earlier action. Confirm those details with the parties involved.

Build an exposure calendar alongside the practice's cash forecast. Include provider and administrative payroll, rent, and other committed payments. Treat expected payer receipts as forecast inflows—not cash already available.

Illustrative exposure calendar

The following is an invented planning example, not a payment timetable recommendation. Assume one practice entity has a €20,000 equipment invoice due on Day 30.

Timing Exposure or operating event Manager action
Day 1 €20,000 invoice received Verify amount, currency, due date, and contractual payment options
Day 3 Vendor's alternative dollar quote expires Compare the written dollar quote with the all-in euro payment estimate
Day 12 Provider and administrative payroll Check that any early vendor payment leaves enough operating cash
Day 18 Forecast payer receipts Update the forecast using actual collections; flag any shortfall
Day 23 Payment preparation checkpoint Confirm approval, current quote, payment instructions, and required processing time
Day 30 Vendor payment due Confirm receipt or settlement status and resolve any balance difference
Month-end Accounting close Reconcile bank activity, fees, invoice settlement, and any currency differences

For a multi-entity practice, show which entity holds the cash and which owes the invoice. Cash elsewhere in the ownership group should not be treated as automatically available. Ask the CPA how any necessary intercompany funding should be recorded, and obtain legal input where the arrangement requires it.

Estimate the Dollar Cash Sensitivity

Choose one exchange-rate convention and label it clearly. For example, use US dollars per euro, so a higher quoted rate means the same euro invoice costs more dollars.

For a known foreign-currency payable:

Estimated dollar payment = foreign-currency amount × dollars per currency unit + separately charged payment costs.

If an executable conversion rate already includes a provider's spread, do not add that spread again. When comparing providers, compare the total dollars debited and the foreign-currency amount delivered on equivalent terms.

Illustrative sensitivity calculation

These invented assumptions are for planning only, not current rates, typical fees, or exchange-rate forecasts:

  • Equipment invoice: €20,000.
  • Baseline conversion rate: $1.10 per euro, assumed to include the provider's conversion spread.
  • Separate transfer charge: $40.
  • Sensitivity cases: the dollar cost per euro rises or falls by 5%.
  • No other charges are assumed for this example.
Scenario Assumed dollars per euro Dollar value of invoice Separate charge Total cash required
Baseline $1.100 $22,000 $40 $22,040
Euro costs 5% more dollars $1.155 $23,100 $40 $23,140
Euro costs 5% fewer dollars $1.045 $20,900 $40 $20,940

Under these assumptions, the adverse scenario requires an additional $1,100 compared with the baseline. That is the useful management number: could the practice absorb that amount without disrupting payroll or another committed payment?

The percentage is a hypothetical stress test, not a forecast or a universal threshold. Choose scenarios that help the owner evaluate the actual obligation, payment window, and available cash. A small recurring invoice may need only routine monitoring; a large equipment installment may warrant a specific cash contingency.

Compare Payment Timing With Dollar-Denominated Terms

Using the same illustrative invoice, assume the vendor also offers a binding alternative price of $22,400, payable on Day 30, with no additional payment charges. Assume paying the euro invoice on Day 1 would cost the baseline $22,040 in total.

These are invented comparison terms. Actual options depend on the contract and executable quotes.

Approach Illustrative cash outcome What becomes more certain Main trade-off
Pay the euro invoice on receipt $22,040 paid on Day 1 This invoice's dollar settlement cost is fixed once conversion and payment occur Cash leaves earlier; vendor-performance, cancellation, and refund risks still matter
Pay the euro invoice near its due date $20,940, $22,040, or $23,140 under the modeled rate cases Cash stays available longer, but the dollar amount remains uncertain until conversion An adverse currency move can coincide with other cash demands
Accept fixed dollar-denominated terms $22,400 paid on Day 30 under the assumed binding quote Dollar payment amount is fixed while payment remains due later The vendor may charge more for taking currency risk; future orders may have different terms

In this example, the fixed dollar quote costs $360 more than the baseline euro payment. That difference is a visible price for the offered terms, not proof that either choice is better.

Before paying early, check the practice's cash position after payment, supplier reliability, and contractual protections. Before accepting dollar terms, confirm that the price is genuinely fixed and cannot be reset before payment. Before waiting, reserve enough cash for an adverse outcome rather than assuming the baseline estimate will hold.

Changing invoice currency may move immediate conversion risk to the vendor, but it does not prevent the vendor from changing future prices where the contract permits it.

Set a Simple Approval and Review Process

The practice manager can maintain the register and obtain quotes; the owner or designated financial approver should authorize material departures from normal payment terms. Avoid allowing one person to change vendor bank instructions and release the payment without an independent check.

Document a short operating policy covering:

  • Scope: Only documented business obligations, receipts, and necessary currency balances belong in the exposure register.
  • Decision authority: Identify who can approve early payment, revised currency terms, and transfers between entities.
  • Liquidity test: Review projected cash after payment, including payroll and other committed expenses.
  • Escalation: Set a dollar-impact level tied to the practice's available cash and operating needs, rather than borrowing a generic industry percentage.
  • Review cadence: Revisit exposure when invoices, quotes, payment dates, or collection forecasts change, and reconcile completed payments during the close.

Keep the invoice, accepted terms, approval, conversion confirmation, fee detail, and bank settlement support together. IRS Publication 583 explains the importance of complete business records, separate business accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those recordkeeping principles support this workflow; they do not prescribe a particular currency strategy.

Know When to Bring in Specialists

A bank or payment specialist can explain executable conversion quotes, transfer timing, fee allocation, supported payment currencies, and the amount the recipient should receive. Ask for a comparable all-in quote, not just an attractive headline rate. This article does not recommend securities, derivatives, or hedging products.

A CPA should address how foreign-currency invoices, settlement differences, fees, and any foreign-currency balances belong in the practice's books under its accounting method. Accounting translation and cash planning are related but different tasks. Ask the CPA before assuming a currency difference has a particular tax treatment.

Legal counsel can review ambiguous currency clauses, repricing rights, early-payment protections, cancellation provisions, and disputes over what constitutes full payment. Seek advice on the contract in question rather than treating a general article as a cross-border legal checklist.

Conclusion

Managing foreign exchange risk starts with a documented obligation—not a market prediction. Identify the invoice currency, map the payment dates, calculate the all-in dollar sensitivity, and compare available terms against the practice's operating cash needs.

The best decision is one the owner can explain from the contract, the cash forecast, and the approved payment record. Protecting payroll and meeting vendor obligations matter more than trying to achieve the lowest possible exchange rate.

FAQs

Does every medical practice buying from an overseas vendor have FX risk?

No. A fixed dollar invoice generally does not require the practice to convert currency for that payment. Review the contract for currency-related repricing provisions and distinguish current invoice exposure from possible future price changes.

Is paying a foreign-currency invoice immediately always safer?

It ends the invoice's exchange-rate uncertainty once conversion and payment occur, but it also uses cash earlier. It does not eliminate vendor-performance or refund risk. Compare the confirmed payment cost with projected payroll, other obligations, and the contract's protections before deciding.

Should the practice buy extra foreign currency because the rate looks favorable?

Do not confuse funding a documented obligation with buying currency in anticipation of a profit. Extra currency creates a position that can gain or lose value independently of the invoice. Keep the operating workflow focused on actual obligations and seek specialist advice before considering any separate currency strategy.

How should a multi-location practice report foreign-currency costs?

Record the obligation in the responsible entity and assign the underlying cost to the benefiting location or an approved shared-cost allocation. Have the CPA determine the treatment of fees and currency differences so location reports remain consistent and reconcile to entity-level books.
