AI Credit Scoring Challenges for Medical Practice Borrowers

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

An automated credit assessment can appear inconsistent with a medical practice’s financial position. The lender may have reviewed an older reporting period, received records for the wrong entity, or interpreted a temporary cash-flow disruption without its context. The assessment may also reflect a genuine weakness that the owner has underestimated.

The practical response is to investigate the inputs and document specific discrepancies—not try to reverse-engineer the score. A useful review connects each concern to a source record, explains what the record actually shows, and asks the lender what correction or human-review process is available.

This article addresses exception resolution after an assessment raises questions. It is not a guide to the benefits of automated scoring or routine lender preparation. The goal is a focused clarification packet for a staffed US private medical practice, not a promise of approval or better terms.

Start With the Assessment, Not an Assumption About AI

A financing decision may involve an automated score, lender policy, financial-statement analysis, collateral, owner information or several factors together. Do not assume that an unexplained result came entirely from AI.

Save the assessment, lender messages and the application version submitted. Then ask the lender:

  • Which borrower entity and reporting period were evaluated?
  • What financial records or external reports were used, to the extent the lender can share?
  • What specific financial concerns can the practice address?
  • Can corrected records or explanations be submitted, and in what format?
  • Is a human review or reconsideration process available?
  • Does the lender have a submission window or other process requirements?

Record the answers without interpreting them as a commitment to reconsider. Available procedures can differ by lender and financing product. Questions about legal rights or notice requirements belong with qualified counsel; the workflow here is financial and operational.

Assign one practice contact to coordinate the response. The owner should confirm the borrowing purpose and entity, the finance team should reconcile the records, and the practice manager can explain operational timing. Avoid sending separate, inconsistent explanations from multiple people.

Check the Financial Inputs Behind the Concern

Stale data or mismatched reporting periods

Compare the assessment date with the dates covered by the submitted statements, bank records and debt schedule. A recent upload does not necessarily contain recent information.

For example, a prior-year P&L and a current bank balance describe different periods. Likewise, a cash-basis report should not be compared directly with an accrual-basis report without explaining the difference.

Prepare a consistent reporting set with a stated period, accounting basis and reconciliation status. If the latest month is not closed, label it preliminary and identify what remains unresolved. Do not present incomplete figures as finalized results.

Entity and location mismatches

A practice’s trading name, operating entity, property entity and owner are not interchangeable borrowers. Confirm which entity owns the revenue, holds the cash, employs staff and owes each debt.

For a multi-location practice, a location P&L can explain operating performance, but it does not replace entity-level statements. A location may carry allocated administrative costs without having a separate bank account or legal identity.

Do not imply that another entity’s assets are available for repayment merely because the same owner controls both entities.

Owner and practice commingling

Personal expenses paid through practice accounts, owner contributions recorded as receipts, and distributions mixed with payroll can make the financial picture difficult to interpret.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports keeping complete records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. For an assessment investigation, those controls help establish which transactions belong to the practice.

Have the CPA review questionable classifications. A transfer from the owner is not automatically operating revenue, and a payment to the owner is not automatically a payroll expense. Preserve the original records and document any supported accounting corrections. Do not simply remove transactions to make cash flow look stronger.

Unusual payer receipts or payment timing

Bank activity can show a weak period even when the explanation involves timing. Payer receipts may arrive after the reporting cutoff, patient-balance collections may fluctuate, or a payroll calendar may concentrate payments in one month.

Timing explanations still require evidence. Compare deposits with aggregate collection reports, reconcile receipts to the ledger, and show subsequent receipts separately from the original period. Distinguish collections already received from receivables that remain outstanding.

An unresolved payer delay may represent real liquidity pressure. Explain the cause where known, but also show how provider and administrative payroll, rent and debt payments will be funded if receipts remain delayed.

Unexplained one-time expenses

Equipment repairs, relocation costs or professional fees connected with a transaction may warrant explanation. Calling an expense “one-time,” however, does not establish that it should be excluded from a lender’s analysis.

Provide the invoice, payment evidence and ledger classification. Explain whether the expense is complete, whether similar spending is expected, and whether any related obligation remains. Keep the recorded financial results intact and present any management adjustment separately. Ask the lender how it treats the item rather than assuming an add-back is acceptable.

Build a Discrepancy Log Before Sending More Documents

The log should distinguish a confirmed error from an explanation or an unresolved question. It also prevents repeated submissions of different versions of the same financial statement.

Add the date identified, document version, lender response and next action to the working log. Close an item only when its outcome is recorded. “Submitted” is not the same as “accepted” or “corrected.”

If the evidence does not support the owner’s initial explanation, revise the log. A clear finding that cash flow weakened is more useful than an unsupported claim that the assessment is wrong.

Assemble a Focused Lender Clarification Packet

Every issue should link to both a source record and a plain-language explanation. A large document upload without that connection leaves the reviewer to reconstruct the practice’s reasoning.

Use a short cover memo

Identify the applicant entity, application reference, assessment date and purpose of the submission. List the issues being clarified and the action requested for each: correction of an input, clarification of a financial concern, or human review if available.

Keep the tone factual. For example: “The submitted statement covered an earlier period than the period referenced in our discussion. Attachment A contains the updated closed statements and a comparison.” Avoid asserting that a correction must change the decision.

Index the supporting records

Packet section Contents Connection to the review
Issue summary Discrepancy log and requested actions Gives each concern a stable reference
Borrower identification Relevant entity details and account ownership information Establishes whose finances are being assessed
Reconciled financials Dated P&L, balance sheet, cash-flow information and reconciliation status Establishes the reporting period and basis
Issue-specific evidence Selected statements, invoices, debt records or aggregate collection reports Supports each factual explanation
Separate management analysis Timing reconciliation or proposed expense adjustment Distinguishes recorded results from interpretation
Open questions Missing evidence and items awaiting lender clarification Prevents uncertain matters from appearing resolved

Use descriptive filenames that identify the entity, period and issue reference. Keep a copy of exactly what was sent. If a financial statement changes, explain the revision rather than silently replacing it.

Keep patient information out of the financial narrative

A lender clarification generally should focus on financial totals, not patient-level detail. Use aggregate payer receipts and patient-balance summaries where sufficient. Review exports for names, account identifiers and clinical details before sharing.

HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains its scope and exceptions. Have the appropriate privacy adviser assess disclosures and handling arrangements when protected health information may be involved. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Work Within Limited Model Transparency

The lender may not disclose its scoring formula, feature weights or proprietary model details. That limits what an owner can conclude about why a result occurred.

Ask questions the records can help resolve:

  • Was the intended entity evaluated?
  • Were the correct dates and accounting basis used?
  • Were owner contributions or intercompany transfers interpreted as operating activity?
  • Can the lender explain the financial concern more specifically?
  • Can supporting records be considered through an available review process?

Do not alter transaction timing, relabel expenses without support or submit multiple inconsistent versions to test the score. A lower assessment alone does not prove a data error, model bias or an unreasonable lending decision.

Close the Review and Make the Financing Decision

After submission, record what the lender received, which issues it addressed and what remains open. Ask whether a revised assessment will occur or whether the packet will be considered only as supplemental information.

Then return to the practice’s actual borrowing decision. Can existing cash flow support the proposed payments? Would delayed payer receipts leave enough liquidity for payroll? Is the proposed borrower the entity that will receive the financing benefit? If the explanation reveals recurring losses or persistent collection delays, those issues need attention regardless of the score.

FAQs

Does an unexpected assessment mean the AI made a mistake?

No. It may reflect incorrect inputs, missing context, lender policy or a genuine financial concern. Start by checking the borrower entity, reporting period and source records. Keep confirmed errors separate from disagreements about how the lender interprets accurate information.

Can a practice request human review?

Ask whether the lender offers a correction, clarification or human-review process, what documents it accepts and how to submit them. Do not assume that review is available or that it will change the result. Record the lender’s instructions and response.

Should the practice exclude unusual expenses from its statements?

Not simply because they weaken the borrowing picture. Keep the accounting records accurate. Explain an unusual expense with supporting documents and present any proposed adjustment separately. The lender decides how it will treat that explanation in its analysis.

What if the lender will not explain the model?

Focus on verifiable inputs and the financial concerns the lender can share. Ask about reporting dates, entity identification and available correction procedures. You do not need the scoring formula to document a stale statement or an entity mismatch, but you cannot promise that resolving either will change the decision.
