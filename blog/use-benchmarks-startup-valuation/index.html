Using Valuation Benchmarks for a Medical Practice

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A transaction benchmark can help a medical practice owner evaluate a proposed buy-in, sale or ownership transfer—but only when the underlying transactions are comparable. A headline purchase price or earnings multiple is not enough.

Before using another practice’s sale as a reference, establish what changed hands, how earnings were calculated, whether the owner’s clinical work was properly compensated, and how debt and working capital affected the price. Otherwise, apparently similar multiples can describe very different economics.

The practical goal is not to find a number that validates your preferred price. It is to organize reliable evidence for an independent valuation professional, your CPA and your transaction attorney.

1. Define the ownership decision before collecting benchmarks

Start with a written description of the proposed transaction. A sale of an entire operating practice is not directly comparable to a physician buying a minority interest or an existing partner redeeming another owner’s shares.

Record:

  • The interest being valued: operating assets, an entire entity or a specified ownership interest.
  • The purpose and valuation date: a contemplated sale, internal buy-in, redemption or other ownership transfer.
  • The entities included: the clinical practice, related administrative entities and any separately owned real estate.
  • What transfers: equipment, receivables, cash, liabilities and other specified assets or obligations.
  • The owner’s future role: departing, remaining as a clinician, continuing management work or retaining an ownership stake.

Ask the valuation professional to identify the appropriate standard and premise of value for the assignment. A negotiated strategic acquisition price, an independent appraisal and a value determined under an ownership agreement may answer different questions.

For a buy-in, also distinguish money paid to a selling owner from new capital contributed to the practice. Those payments have different effects on practice cash and ownership economics.

2. Separate transaction benchmarks from operating benchmarks and AI models

These tools have different purposes:

Tool What it helps answer What it cannot establish by itself
Transaction valuation benchmarks What did buyers pay for sufficiently comparable interests or assets? The appropriate value of your particular practice
Operating-performance benchmarks How do collections, staffing costs, overhead or other measures compare with peers? A supported transaction multiple or purchase price
AI valuation models What output follows from the model’s data, assumptions and calculations? Whether its source transactions, earnings adjustments or legal assumptions are appropriate

For example, a provider-payroll ratio may reveal an expense issue worth investigating. It does not directly establish the value of an ownership interest.

An AI-generated valuation is also not independent market evidence merely because it produces a precise result. Ask for the underlying transaction sources, valuation dates, price definitions, earnings definitions and treatment of missing information. If those cannot be reviewed, do not treat the output as a verified comparable transaction.

3. Screen transactions for genuine comparability

Ask your valuation professional to document why each proposed comparable belongs in the analysis. Fewer well-documented transactions are more useful than a large collection of unexplained multiples.

Important screening factors include:

  • Specialty and service mix: practices with different staffing, equipment and service requirements may have different economics.
  • Payer and patient-payment mix: payer concentration, patient balances and collection patterns affect the interpretation of revenue and receivables.
  • Provider dependence: assess how much activity depends on a departing owner and what replacement capacity would cost.
  • Scale and locations: a single-location practice may not have the same management infrastructure or shared-cost structure as a multi-location group.
  • Geography and staffing conditions: recruiting requirements, occupancy costs and local competition may differ.
  • Transaction timing and buyer type: older deals and acquisitions involving buyer-specific advantages require particular scrutiny.
  • Control and contractual rights: a minority interest and a controlling interest are not interchangeable.

Distinguish completed transactions from asking prices, preliminary offers and marketing summaries. Record the source and any limitations. Do not assume an undisclosed deal term was favorable, standard or immaterial.

4. Normalize earnings before comparing multiples

A multiple is meaningful only when its numerator and denominator are consistently defined. Enterprise value divided by normalized EBITDA is not equivalent to an equity purchase price divided by owner cash flow.

EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It is not cash available to distribute: equipment spending, debt payments and working-capital needs still matter.

Account for the owner’s actual work

Owner compensation is especially important in a staffed private practice. An owner may perform clinical services, supervise staff and manage the business. Adding back all owner pay while ignoring the cost of replacing that work overstates transferable earnings.

Separate wages, benefits and other compensation from ownership distributions. Then identify which duties will continue and estimate supportable replacement compensation with your advisers. The employment agreement offered after a sale should also be reviewed alongside the purchase price.

Illustrative example only—not a market compensation benchmark: A practice reports EBITDA of $420,000 after recording $360,000 of owner compensation. Advisers estimate $240,000 for the continuing clinical and management work and identify a documented $60,000 nonrecurring expense.

Earnings bridge Illustrative amount
Reported EBITDA $420,000
Add back recorded owner compensation $360,000
Deduct estimated replacement compensation ($240,000)
Add back documented nonrecurring expense $60,000
Proposed normalized EBITDA $600,000

Every adjustment remains subject to review. A recurring recruitment expense should not become an add-back simply because the practice hired a different clinician this year. A proposed future saving is also different from a historical expense adjustment.

Reconcile the accounting basis and business scope

Cash-basis receipts, accrual revenue and gross billed charges are not interchangeable. Payer receipts can arrive after the service period, while patient balances may have uncertain collectibility. Ask the CPA to reconcile the reported financials to the earnings measure used in the valuation.

For multiple entities or locations, document shared administrative payroll, rent and other allocations. A location P&L that excludes central support costs can overstate that location’s standalone earnings. Likewise, related-party rent may need analysis rather than automatic removal.

In particular, multiplying Transaction B’s interest price by four does not establish the value of full control. The rights attached to the interest, transfer restrictions and agreement terms require professional analysis.

For your own worksheet, add columns for the source document, valuation date, responsible reviewer and unresolved questions. Mark unavailable facts as Not disclosed or Pending adviser verification, rather than entering zero.

6. Reconcile enterprise value, equity value and closing cash

Enterprise value generally describes the operating business before the agreed treatment of financing and cash. Equity value reflects the value attributable to owners after relevant adjustments. The signed transaction definitions govern the actual calculation.

Illustrative example only: Assume an enterprise value of $2,400,000, $100,000 of cash included for the seller’s benefit and $500,000 of debt to be repaid. With no working-capital adjustment or other deductions, the simplified equity-value bridge is:

$2,400,000 + $100,000 − $500,000 = $2,000,000.

That is not necessarily the seller’s closing cash or after-tax proceeds. Transaction expenses, escrow, deferred payments, retained equity and tax consequences require separate analysis.

Agree on working capital rather than assuming it

A practice needs resources to meet provider and administrative payroll, rent and other obligations while collections arrive. The agreement should specify which working-capital accounts transfer and how any closing adjustment is calculated.

Questions include:

  • Are payer receivables and patient balances included, and how is collectibility assessed?
  • Which payables, accrued payroll, refunds or other obligations transfer?
  • Is cash excluded from the working-capital definition?
  • Does the target reflect ordinary operations, seasonality or unusual collection timing?
  • Could the same balance be counted both as debt-like and as working capital?

Keep this calculation separate from the headline multiple so adjustments remain visible.

7. Compare deal terms, not just purchase prices

An all-cash price at closing is not economically identical to the same stated amount partly payable through an earnout or seller note. Retained equity also differs from cash proceeds.

Have advisers separate closing cash, contingent payments, financing terms and continuing employment compensation. Ask who controls earnout measurements, what happens if employment ends, and what obligations remain with the seller.

For a buy-in, review governance rights, future capital requirements, distribution provisions and exit terms alongside price. A transaction attorney should assess the proposed structure and applicable ownership and transfer requirements; a benchmark cannot resolve those questions.

8. Bring a documented financial package to your advisers

Prepare records that let reviewers trace earnings and balance-sheet items back to supporting information:

  • Monthly P&Ls, balance sheets and general-ledger detail for the periods requested.
  • Reconciled bank accounts and entity-level account listings.
  • Aggregate collections, receivables aging, payer concentration and patient-balance summaries.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, owner compensation and benefit details.
  • Location P&Ls and documented shared-cost allocations.
  • Debt schedules, equipment obligations, leases and related-party arrangements.
  • A proposed earnings-adjustment schedule with support for each item.
  • Ownership agreements, prior valuation provisions and proposed transaction terms.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices also make financial diligence easier to substantiate.

Use aggregate or de-identified information where practical. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Coordinate any necessary access with appropriate advisers and safeguards; HHS guidance on minimum necessary information explains the standard and its exceptions.

Conclusion: Use benchmarks to test assumptions

A useful benchmark connects a documented price to consistently defined earnings and comparable transaction terms. Before discussing a multiple, resolve the ownership interest, owner replacement compensation, debt, working capital and payment structure.

The strongest next step is a reviewable worksheet: reliable inputs, explicit adjustments and visible missing-data flags. Bring that worksheet and its supporting records to your valuation professional, CPA and attorney before relying on a proposed price.

FAQs

Can I value my medical practice using a percentage of revenue?

Revenue alone does not show staffing costs, owner replacement compensation, collection quality or capital needs. If a transaction source uses a revenue multiple, confirm the revenue definition and included assets. Ask a valuation professional whether that comparison is appropriate rather than treating it as a standalone valuation.

Should all owner compensation be added back to earnings?

No. Identify the clinical and management work that must continue, then account for supportable replacement compensation. Separate compensation from ownership distributions and document each adjustment with your CPA and valuation professional.

Does a minority buy-in price establish the value of the whole practice?

Not automatically. Control, contractual rights, transfer restrictions and whether the payment goes to the practice or a selling owner can affect the analysis. Do not simply scale an interest price into a whole-practice value without professional review.

Can an AI valuation replace an independent valuation professional?

An AI output does not establish that its transaction data or assumptions are appropriate. Require reviewable sources and calculations. For a buy-in, sale or ownership transfer, use qualified advisers to assess the evidence, financial adjustments and agreement terms.
