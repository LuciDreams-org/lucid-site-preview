7 Current Financial KPIs for Medical Practice Capital Partners

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A private medical practice discussing financing, expansion or an existing capital relationship needs more than a current bank balance. Owners and capital partners need to understand whether collections support staffing, how much cash operations generate and whether the practice can meet its debt obligations.

Seven measures provide a focused starting point: unrestricted cash, operating cash flow, net collections, receivables aging, operating margin, payroll cost relative to net revenue and debt-service coverage.

“Current” does not mean every number should update continuously. Cash may need daily attention, while operating margin usually needs a completed monthly close. A useful report distinguishes fresh operational information from reconciled financial results.

This is a capital-partner reporting set—not a broad metric catalog, dashboard-building guide or AI tracking tool comparison. Its purpose is to help owners explain financial capacity, identify unresolved questions and support specific decisions without promising investment returns.

Agree on the reporting basis before comparing results

Before sharing the seven KPIs, document:

  • Reporting scope: Which practice entities and locations are included? Are related management or property entities excluded?
  • Accounting basis: Are financial statements prepared on a cash, accrual or other agreed basis?
  • Period: Which measures are point-in-time balances, monthly activity or trailing-period calculations?
  • Definitions: How are refunds, owner-provider compensation, shared costs and unusual items treated?
  • Status: Is each figure preliminary, reconciled or based on closed books?

Use financing and partnership documents to confirm required definitions. A lender's debt-service calculation may differ from the practice's internal management calculation. Present both with a reconciliation when necessary rather than substituting one for the other.

There are no universal targets for these seven measures. Specialty, payer mix, staffing model, location maturity, capital needs and contractual requirements all affect interpretation.

1. Unrestricted cash

Decision supported: Can the practice meet near-term obligations without relying on delayed payer receipts or new financing?

Calculation: Add cash and cash equivalents within the reporting scope, then subtract amounts subject to restrictions that prevent their use for general practice operations. Support those restrictions with account documentation or relevant agreements.

Use reconciled ledger balances for the formal report. For an interim liquidity update, identify the bank balance date and adjust for known outstanding payments and deposits in transit without counting them twice.

Period and refresh: Report an as-of date. Review daily or weekly when liquidity is tight, with a reconciled month-end figure for capital partners.

Limitations: Unrestricted cash is not the same as cash available for expansion or owner distributions. Upcoming provider and administrative payroll, vendor payments and debt service may consume it quickly. Show these commitments alongside the balance without calling them legally restricted unless they actually are.

Keep cash visible by entity. Do not assume that funds in one entity can be used by another simply because both have the same owner. Undrawn borrowing capacity should be disclosed separately, not included as cash.

2. Operating cash flow

Decision supported: Is the practice generating cash from its ordinary activities, or is its bank balance being sustained by financing and owner contributions?

For an owner-facing direct-method schedule, calculate:

Operating cash flow = operating cash receipts − operating cash payments.

Inputs include payer and patient receipts, payroll, benefits, supplies, rent and other operating payments. Exclude loan proceeds, capital contributions, owner distributions, debt principal payments and equipment purchases from this operating calculation. Have the finance team document the treatment of interest, taxes and unusual transactions under the reporting basis used.

Reconcile the result to the overall change in cash through a schedule of operating, investing and financing activity. Transfers between included accounts are not new operating receipts.

Period and refresh: Report monthly and year-to-date results, with a trailing-period view where helpful. Review a preliminary weekly cash summary when managing liquidity, but label it separately from closed results.

Limitations: A strong month can reflect collection of older receivables or delayed supplier payments rather than stronger current operations. Conversely, a payroll timing difference can weaken one month's cash flow without indicating a lasting deterioration. Explain material timing effects.

3. Net collections

Decision supported: How much cash is the practice actually collecting from its services, and what explains changes?

For this reporting set, define net collections as payer and patient payments posted during the period, less refunds and payment reversals, with recoupments treated consistently and without double counting. Exclude financing proceeds, owner contributions and unrelated receipts.

Use the billing system's payment and adjustment detail, then bridge the result to deposits and the general ledger. Separate differences caused by posting dates, settlement timing, processing fees and unapplied receipts.

Period and refresh: Review preliminary weekly totals for collection changes. Present reconciled monthly totals with year-to-date and comparable prior-period results.

Limitations: These are collection dollars, not a net collection rate. If a partner requests a rate, agree separately on the collectible-charge denominator, adjustments and claim cohort. Dividing this month's receipts by this month's charges can mismatch different service periods.

Net collections also are not necessarily accounting revenue. On accrual books, revenue and cash receipts occur at different times. Explain whether changes reflect provider availability, payer timing, patient-balance collections or other identifiable causes rather than assuming every increase represents growth.

4. Receivables aging

Decision supported: Where is expected cash becoming delayed or less certain?

Calculation: Group open payer and patient receivables into consistent age bands, such as current, 31–60, 61–90 and over 90 days. Document whether age begins at the service date, claim submission date or another event. Do not silently change that basis between reports.

Show the dollar balance in each band and, where useful:

Older-receivable share = receivables above the selected age ÷ total receivables on the same basis.

Separate payer balances from patient balances. Identify credit balances, unapplied payments and disputed items rather than allowing them to obscure the collectible balance.

Period and refresh: Review weekly for operational follow-up. Preserve a month-end snapshot for capital-partner reporting and reconcile it to the ledger where receivables are recorded.

Limitations: Older balances are not automatically uncollectible, and newer balances are not guaranteed cash. Gross billed charges can also overstate expected receipts before contractual adjustments. State whether the report uses gross or adjusted receivables and explain any difference from the financial statements. Cash-basis books may not carry a receivables balance to reconcile directly.

5. Operating margin

Decision supported: Does the practice's revenue support its ongoing operating cost structure?

Calculation:

Operating margin = operating income ÷ net operating revenue × 100.

Define operating income as net operating revenue less the operating expenses included under the agreed accounting policy. State the treatment of depreciation and amortization, and keep interest, taxes and nonoperating items consistent with that policy. Do not label an EBITDA-based calculation as operating margin.

Use the closed profit and loss statement. Explain how contractual adjustments, refunds and revenue estimates affect net revenue. For cash-basis statements, label the basis clearly because receipt and payment timing can affect the result.

Period and refresh: Calculate after the monthly close, with year-to-date and trailing-period comparisons.

Limitations: Owner-provider compensation can materially affect comparisons. Show reported results first; if partners request normalized compensation or other adjustments, provide a separate bridge with support for each item.

For multiple locations, show shared administrative costs and their allocation method. A location P&L excluding central costs is not directly comparable with one that includes them. Disclose material allocation changes before interpreting margin movement.

6. Payroll cost relative to net revenue

Decision supported: Is the practice's staffing cost aligned with its revenue base?

Calculation:

Payroll cost ratio = defined payroll cost ÷ net operating revenue × 100.

Define the numerator before reporting it. A fully burdened employee-cost measure can include provider and administrative wages, employer payroll taxes, employer-paid benefits, bonuses and related payroll accruals. Show provider and administrative components separately when that helps explain changes.

If contract clinicians are excluded from payroll, disclose their costs separately so a shift between employees and contractors does not appear to improve staffing economics by itself.

Period and refresh: Report monthly after payroll and benefit reconciliations. Use a rolling period to reduce distortion from payroll-calendar timing, and review staffing commitments between closes when hiring decisions are pending.

Limitations: Match numerator and denominator periods and use a consistent accounting basis. Bonuses, vacancies, temporary coverage, paid leave and a new provider's ramp-up can change the ratio. It does not independently measure productivity or establish an appropriate staffing level. For owner-providers, explain compensation treatment consistently with the operating-margin report.

7. Debt-service coverage

Decision supported: Does the practice have sufficient capacity to meet the debt payments being evaluated?

Calculation:

Debt-service coverage ratio = defined cash flow available for debt service ÷ required debt service for the same period.

For covenant reporting, use the financing document's numerator, permitted adjustments, debt scope and payment definition. Do not assume operating cash flow, EBITDA and lender-defined cash flow are interchangeable.

For internal analysis without a contractual definition, document how cash available for debt service is derived and which principal and interest obligations are included. Reconcile the calculation to financial statements, debt schedules and payment records. Avoid counting interest twice if it is already deducted in the starting cash-flow figure.

Period and refresh: Review monthly using a trailing period and refresh when debt terms or balances change. Test contractual compliance on the schedule required by the agreement.

Limitations: Historical coverage does not establish future payment capacity. Balloon payments, variable rates, new borrowing and guarantees may require separate analysis. A positive ratio is not a financing approval or an investor-return forecast. If no debt service exists for the period, report the ratio as not applicable rather than implying unlimited capacity.

Why bank-feed activity is not a closed financial result

A bank feed shows account activity, not necessarily the correct accounting treatment. A deposit might be payer receipts, a loan, an owner contribution or an interaccount transfer. A withdrawal might settle an earlier bill rather than represent the current month's expense.

Bank feeds also do not establish unpaid expenses, payroll accruals, receivable adjustments, depreciation or appropriate shared-cost allocations. Even matched transactions need review for classification, completeness and reporting scope.

The IRS guidance on business records and bank reconciliation supports maintaining complete records and reconciling bank records. For capital reporting, complete the relevant reconciliations and closing entries before calling results closed. Use faster interim information when needed, but retain its preliminary label.

Turn the report into a decision

Send the seven measures with a short commentary: what changed, why it changed, what remains uncertain and what action or decision is requested. A delayed payer deposit may call for collection follow-up; a sustained payroll-ratio increase may require reviewing staffing commitments and revenue assumptions. Those are different problems.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. Capital-partner reports generally do not need patient-identifiable detail. Where financial work involves protected health information, evaluate the workflow and applicable responsibilities with appropriate advisers; the HHS minimum necessary guidance provides relevant context and exceptions.

FAQs

Do capital partners need all seven KPIs in real time?

No. Match frequency to the decision. Cash and collection exceptions may need frequent review, while margin and payroll ratios usually require reconciled monthly results. Show both the reporting period and preparation date so a recent update is not mistaken for a completed close.

What if our calculation differs from the lender's definition?

Keep the internal calculation for management use, but calculate contractual reporting exactly as the governing documents require. Provide a bridge between the two definitions and have the finance team review uncertain adjustments before submission.

Should a multi-location practice report consolidated or location-level KPIs?

Use the scope relevant to the capital relationship, then add detail where it explains risk or performance. Consolidated results can conceal a weak location, while location results can mislead if shared costs are omitted. Preserve entity-level cash and debt visibility, and eliminate internal transfers from consolidated activity.
