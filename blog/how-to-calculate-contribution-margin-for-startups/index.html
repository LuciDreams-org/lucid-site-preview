How to Calculate Contribution Margin for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Contribution margin tells a medical practice owner how much earned revenue remains after paying costs that vary with a defined activity. That remainder helps cover salaried staff, occupancy, administrative overhead and other fixed costs before producing operating profit.

The calculation is straightforward. The judgment behind it is not: payer receipts arrive after services are delivered, patient balances may remain outstanding, and payroll does not necessarily change when visit volume changes.

A useful contribution margin report therefore needs three things: a defined activity, a consistent revenue basis and a documented explanation of which costs vary over the period being analyzed.

What contribution margin measures

Use these formulas:

Contribution margin dollars = Net earned revenue − Costs that vary with the selected activity

Contribution margin percentage = Contribution margin dollars ÷ Net earned revenue × 100

For a practice, the selected activity might be a service category, a location's completed visits or the total activity of one operating entity. Choose the scope before extracting revenue and expenses.

Contribution margin is a management measure, not a substitute for the practice's complete profit and loss statement. It answers: How much does this activity contribute toward costs that do not vary with it?

It does not, by itself, answer whether a location is profitable after all overhead or whether the practice has enough cash for payroll.

Step 1: Define the activity, period and decision

Write a short scope statement at the top of the calculation. Include:

  • Activity: The services, location or entity included.
  • Period: The service month or other consistent reporting period.
  • Revenue basis: Net earned revenue attributable to that activity and period.
  • Cost behavior: Which expenses change with activity within the relevant volume range.
  • Decision horizon: Whether the analysis concerns the current schedule, a staffing commitment or a longer-term operating structure.

For a monthly location report, ordinary salaries may remain fixed within the existing schedule. For a longer-term review involving an additional staffed session, a new payroll commitment may become relevant. Those are different analyses and should not silently share the same cost assumptions.

Use consistent definitions when comparing locations. A site that classifies all provider compensation as variable cannot be compared directly with one that treats salaried providers as fixed without explaining the difference.

Step 2: Use net earned revenue—not billed charges or deposits

Billed charges, earned revenue and cash receipts describe different things.

Measure What it represents Why it matters to contribution margin
Billed charges The practice's gross charges before applicable adjustments Usually overstates the revenue available to cover costs if used without adjustments
Net earned revenue Revenue attributable to services performed, using the practice's accounting policies and supported adjustments Provides the revenue basis for this calculation
Cash receipts Payer and patient collections received during the period May relate to services performed in earlier periods
Bank deposits Amounts deposited, potentially net of fees or mixed with nonrevenue transfers Require reconciliation before they can support revenue analysis

Work with the accountant to establish a consistent treatment of contractual adjustments, refunds and estimated uncollectible amounts. Do not subtract an adjustment twice because it appears in both a billing report and the general ledger.

Outstanding patient balances are not automatically equivalent to collectible revenue. Likewise, a payer deposit received this month does not necessarily belong to this month's services.

If the books are maintained on a cash basis, label a cash-based calculation accordingly. To calculate earned-revenue contribution margin, prepare a supported reconciliation rather than renaming collections as earned revenue.

Step 3: Classify costs by behavior

An expense is not variable merely because it is assigned directly to a provider or location. A location-specific lease is direct to that location but generally fixed within its current agreement. A shared expense can have a variable component if the actual charge changes with activity.

Cost-classification worksheet

Cost item Question to resolve Typical treatment within an existing schedule Supporting record
Clinical supplies consumed Does consumption increase with the selected services? Variable to the extent used by that activity Usage records and supply costs
Salaried provider compensation Does pay change when activity changes? Fixed if the salary remains unchanged Employment terms and payroll detail
Productivity-based compensation What compensation actually changes under the agreement? Variable component, with any base separated Compensation agreement and calculation
Administrative payroll Do paid hours or wages change with volume? Often fixed within current staffing; may become a step cost Schedules, time records and payroll
Incremental hourly coverage Are extra paid hours required because of the activity? Variable or incremental within the defined scope Approved extra hours and associated payroll costs
Payment processing Which charges depend on transactions or receipts? Variable portion only; separate monthly minimums Processor statement and fee agreement
Outsourced billing expense Is the fee fixed, activity-based or mixed? Split according to contract terms Vendor agreement and invoice
Rent, software and insurance Does the expense change within the selected volume range? Generally fixed; additional commitments may create steps Lease, subscription and policy records
Shared management costs Does the underlying cost change, or only its allocation? Fixed unless evidence supports a variable component Entity accounts and allocation policy

For mixed expenses, separate the fixed and variable components where records support doing so. If the split is uncertain and material, disclose the assumption rather than presenting a precise margin without qualification.

Salaried staffing versus incremental staffing

A salaried provider's compensation does not become variable simply because more visits occur. The same applies to administrative staff whose paid hours remain unchanged.

By contrast, additional paid coverage caused by the selected activity can be incremental. Include associated employer payroll costs that also change. Avoid applying an average payroll cost per visit and calling it variable when payroll would remain the same without those visits.

Owner compensation needs the same care. Distinguish compensation recorded for work performed from owner distributions, which are not operating expenses.

Supplies purchased versus supplies consumed

A large supply order can distort a monthly calculation if the purchased items will be used over several periods. Where material and supported by records, use the cost of supplies consumed for the activity rather than treating every purchase as a current variable cost.

Step 4: Calculate contribution margin in dollars and percent

The following is an illustrative example, not a benchmark or a case study. Assume one location is reviewing a service month within its existing staffed schedule.

Establish the revenue basis

Illustrative revenue calculation Amount
Gross billed charges for the service month $300,000
Contractual adjustments ($90,000)
Other supported revenue adjustments under the practice's accounting policies ($10,000)
Net earned revenue $200,000

Assume separately that bank deposits during the month were $185,000. That amount is not substituted into this calculation: deposits can include collections from earlier services and exclude amounts still outstanding for the current service month.

Subtract the variable costs

All figures below are part of the same illustrative example. Assume the practice has documented that each listed expense varies with the selected activity.

Illustrative variable cost Amount
Supplies consumed $18,000
Variable component of provider compensation, including associated variable employer payroll costs $12,000
Activity-related payment processing fees $3,000
Variable portion of outsourced billing expense $7,000
Total variable costs $40,000

Contribution margin = $200,000 − $40,000 = $160,000

Contribution margin percentage = $160,000 ÷ $200,000 × 100 = 80%

In this illustrative example, each dollar of net earned revenue leaves $0.80 to cover fixed operating costs and profit. It does not mean the location earns an 80% operating profit margin.

For several locations, calculate the combined percentage by dividing total contribution margin dollars by total net earned revenue. Do not take a simple average of location percentages when their revenue differs.

If net earned revenue is zero, the percentage is undefined. Investigate the underlying activity and adjustments rather than reporting a misleading ratio.

Step 5: Reconcile contribution margin to operating profit

A contribution report should connect to the books. Continuing the illustrative example:

Reconciliation Amount
Net earned revenue $200,000
Less variable costs ($40,000)
Contribution margin $160,000
Fixed provider and administrative payroll, including associated employer costs ($100,000)
Occupancy costs ($20,000)
Other fixed operating expenses ($15,000)
Allocated shared operating costs ($10,000)
Operating profit after shared-cost allocation $15,000

The illustrative operating profit margin is 7.5%, despite the 80% contribution margin. Both figures can be correct because they subtract different categories of expense.

For location reporting, showing operating profit before and after shared-cost allocations can help owners distinguish local results from the effect of the allocation policy. Document whether shared costs are allocated using revenue, staffing, space or another reasonable basis.

Across multiple entities, eliminate duplicated intercompany charges when preparing a combined view. An allocation changes where an expense appears; it does not necessarily change the total expense the practice group incurs.

If the reconciliation does not match the relevant P&L, investigate missing expenses, timing differences, revenue adjustments and inconsistent account mappings before using the report.

How idle capacity, step costs and time horizon change interpretation

Idle capacity does not make existing payroll disappear. Additional activity within already-paid staffing may have relatively few incremental costs. That can produce a strong contribution margin while the location still struggles to cover its fixed expense base.

Step costs are not smoothly variable. An additional staffed session, larger space commitment or new management role may add a block of cost once a threshold is crossed. Do not spread that block across visits and assume it changes with every visit. Show it separately when the activity requires the commitment.

Longer horizons can change cost behavior. A lease may be fixed during its current term but change under a future relocation decision. Staffing may be fixed this month yet adjustable through later scheduling or hiring choices. State the horizon rather than labeling an expense permanently fixed or variable.

Contribution margin is therefore one input to a decision—not a complete capacity, expansion or closure analysis. A positive contribution does not establish that an activity covers all its costs. A higher percentage also does not automatically mean a better overall result if the activity produces fewer contribution dollars.

A practical review routine for practice owners

Before accepting a contribution margin report, ask:

  1. Does revenue belong to the same activity and service period as the costs?
  2. Are payer and patient adjustments supported and applied consistently?
  3. Which payroll costs actually change, and which are merely allocated?
  4. Are supplies measured on a reasonable consumption basis?
  5. Have fixed fees and step costs been separated from variable expenses?
  6. Does contribution margin reconcile to operating profit without missing or duplicated costs?
  7. Are changes caused by economics, timing, service mix or revised classifications?

Maintain the supporting schedules with the financial records. The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation; those practices help establish reliable inputs, though they do not define contribution margin.

Use aggregated financial information wherever possible. Contribution analysis generally does not need patient names or other patient-identifiable details in working summaries.
