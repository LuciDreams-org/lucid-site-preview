Multi-Currency Bookkeeping for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A US private medical practice can collect payer receipts and patient balances in dollars while still receiving vendor invoices in another currency. An overseas equipment supplier, for example, might invoice in euros even though the practice pays from a US-dollar bank account.

The bookkeeping challenge is connecting the original invoice, its dollar value, each payment, conversion charges and any unpaid balance. Without that connection, the practice can overstate vendor costs, leave paid invoices open or mistake a bank fee for an exchange-rate loss.

Use one documented workflow from invoice entry through settlement and month-end review. The practice manager gathers the records, the bookkeeper maintains the currency schedule, and the CPA confirms the accounting treatment. This article focuses on that recording and reconciliation process—not currency hedging or cross-border legal requirements.

Establish the Currency Rules Before Posting Bills

Three terms help distinguish what the vendor charges from what appears in the practice’s financial statements.

Term Meaning Practice application
Transaction currency The currency in which the invoice or payment obligation is denominated A supplier bills the practice in euros
Functional currency The currency of the entity’s primary economic environment, determined under the applicable accounting framework A US practice operating primarily in dollars may have USD as its functional currency; the CPA confirms this
Reporting currency The currency used to present the financial statements The owner receives financial statements in USD

A foreign-currency invoice does not, by itself, mean the practice needs financial-statement translation for a foreign operation. For a practice with USD functional and reporting currencies, the immediate task is generally to record and reconcile a foreign-currency transaction in dollar books.

Before starting, ask the CPA to confirm:

  • The practice’s accounting basis and applicable financial-reporting framework.
  • When a vendor purchase should be recognized.
  • Whether the purchase is an expense, prepaid item, inventory or capital asset.
  • Which open balances require period-end currency adjustments.
  • How exchange differences and conversion fees should appear in the ledger.

The worked example below assumes accrual-basis books and a USD functional and reporting currency. A cash-basis workflow will differ, particularly before payment. Do not copy the example’s entries into the practice’s books without confirming that they fit its accounting policies.

1. Capture the Invoice in Both Currencies

Record the foreign-currency amount as well as its dollar equivalent. A dollar-only bank entry cannot explain how much of the original invoice remains unpaid after a partial payment.

For each bill, retain:

Record field Purpose
Vendor name and invoice number Links the bill, payments and vendor statement
Responsible legal entity Identifies which practice owes the obligation
Location or shared-cost designation Supports location reporting without duplicating the bill
Currency code and original amount Preserves the vendor’s actual claim
Recognition date and due date Separates accounting timing from payment timing
Exchange rate, direction and source Explains the initial dollar measurement
Dollar amount and expense or asset account Connects the invoice to the general ledger
Payment references and remaining foreign balance Supports partial-payment tracking

Confirm the responsible entity before posting. If one practice entity buys equipment used by another, the currency schedule does not replace the CPA-approved process for shared costs or intercompany balances.

For a multi-location practice within one entity, allocate the underlying expense using a consistent method. Keep the vendor payable tied to the actual invoice rather than creating a separate obligation for every location.

2. Document the Exchange-Rate Policy

A useful rate policy states which source to use, which date applies and how the rate is expressed. It should also explain how the team handles nonbusiness days, rounding and missing rate information.

Always label the rate direction. “1.10 USD per EUR” means that €1,000 converts to $1,100. A rate quoted as EUR per USD requires a different calculation.

Keep evidence of the rate used with the transaction or close workpapers. For settlement, retain the bank or payment provider’s confirmation showing the foreign amount delivered, dollar principal charged and any separately disclosed fees.

A provider’s conversion rate may include a spread rather than a separately stated charge. Do not estimate a fee from a market quote and present it as a documented bank charge. Record disclosed fees separately and have the CPA determine how to treat differences involving the actual conversion rate.

For period-end review, use the approved closing-rate source consistently. Do not choose different sources simply to make a reconciliation difference disappear.

3. Record Payments Against the Original Invoice

When paying a bill, match the payment to the existing payable. Posting the bank withdrawal directly to expense can duplicate a cost already recognized when the invoice was entered.

For every payment, identify:

  1. The amount of foreign currency applied to the invoice.
  2. The dollar principal used to settle that amount.
  3. Any separately disclosed bank or conversion fee.
  4. The carrying value of the payable portion being settled.
  5. The resulting exchange gain or loss.
  6. The remaining invoice balance in the original currency.

A payment confirmation also helps distinguish the instruction date from the completed settlement date. Use the date required by the practice’s accounting policy, supported by the transaction records.

4. Work Through a Partial-Payment Example

Illustrative example only: All amounts, exchange rates and dates below are invented to demonstrate the bookkeeping mechanics. They are not current market rates.

Assume a practice receives a €1,000 invoice for an administrative service that its CPA has confirmed should be expensed when recognized. The invoice is recognized in Month 1 at 1.10 USD per EUR. The practice pays €400 during that month, leaves €600 unpaid at month-end and settles the remainder in Month 2.

Invoice and settlement schedule

Event Foreign amount involved Illustrative rate: USD per EUR USD amount Accounting effect
Invoice recognized €1,000 1.10 $1,100 Record service expense and accounts payable
First partial payment €400 1.12 $448 principal Remove $440 of payable; record $8 exchange loss
First payment fee — — $12 Record separately disclosed bank fee
Month 1 closing review €600 still unpaid 1.15 $690 closing payable Increase the remaining payable from $660 to $690; record $30 unrealized exchange loss
Final settlement in Month 2 €600 1.13 $678 principal Remove the $690 carrying balance; record $12 exchange gain relative to that balance
Final payment fee — — $10 Record separately disclosed bank fee

How the amounts move through the books

At invoice recognition, the practice debits the service expense and credits accounts payable for $1,100.

The first payment settles €400. That portion originally carried a dollar value of $440: €400 × 1.10. The practice now spends $448 to settle it, creating an $8 realized exchange loss. With the separately disclosed $12 fee, the bank withdrawal is $460.

After that payment, the practice still owes €600. Its dollar carrying value before the closing adjustment is $660. At the illustrative closing rate of 1.15, the remaining payable becomes $690. The $30 increase is an unrealized exchange loss because that portion has not yet settled.

In Month 2, paying the final €600 costs $678 in principal. Compared with the adjusted payable of $690, this produces a $12 exchange gain. Including the $10 fee, the second bank withdrawal is $688.

Reconciliation checks

Check Calculation Result
Foreign invoice balance €1,000 − €400 − €600 €0 remaining
Dollar payable rollforward $1,100 − $440 + $30 − $690 $0 remaining
Total dollar principal paid $448 + $678 $1,126
Cumulative net exchange loss $8 + $30 − $12 $26
Separately disclosed fees $12 + $10 $22
Total bank withdrawals $460 + $688 $1,148
Overall cost reconciliation $1,100 original expense + $26 net exchange loss + $22 fees $1,148

The final $26 net exchange loss equals the difference between the original $1,100 liability and the $1,126 principal ultimately paid. Fees remain separate.

This schedule carries the month-end adjustment forward rather than reversing it automatically. If the practice uses reversing entries, the next period’s calculations must account for those reversals. The CPA should confirm any reclassification between realized and unrealized accounts so the earlier $30 adjustment is not counted twice.

5. Distinguish Realized and Unrealized Differences

A realized exchange difference arises when a foreign-currency obligation is settled. An unrealized exchange difference reflects a change in the dollar carrying value of an open balance at period-end.

Separate ledger accounts or a supporting schedule can help the owner distinguish vendor spending from currency movement. For example, the administrative service in the illustration initially costs $1,100 in the books; the later exchange losses and bank fees explain why total cash outflow differs.

Do not revalue every foreign purchase simply because exchange rates changed. An unpaid monetary liability and an asset already recorded at historical cost can have different treatment. Have the CPA identify which balances require adjustment and which retain their historical measurement.

6. Reconcile the Bank, Vendor Balance and Ledger

A complete reconciliation connects three records:

  • The vendor record: What amount remains due in the transaction currency?
  • The payment record: What foreign amount was delivered, what dollar principal was charged and what fees were disclosed?
  • The ledger: What payable, cash, fee and exchange-difference entries were posted?

If the practice pays from a USD account, reconcile the dollar withdrawal to the statement while separately tracking the foreign invoice balance. If it holds a foreign-currency bank account, reconcile that account’s original-currency units as well as its dollar carrying value under the approved policy.

Investigate differences before posting an exchange adjustment. Common causes include a payment applied to the wrong invoice, an omitted fee, a vendor credit, a duplicate bank-feed entry or a timing difference between payment initiation and settlement. None should automatically be treated as an exchange loss.

The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records and reconciling bank records. It does not establish the currency-accounting treatment illustrated here; that treatment should be confirmed with the practice’s CPA.

7. Make Currency Review Part of the Monthly Close

For occasional foreign invoices, a controlled supporting spreadsheet may be sufficient alongside the ledger. More frequent activity may justify accounting tools with currency functionality, but verify actual capabilities before relying on them.

FAQs

Do we need a foreign-currency bank account to pay an overseas vendor?

Not necessarily. A practice may pay a foreign-currency invoice from a USD account if its payment provider supports that transaction. The bookkeeping still needs the foreign amount settled, dollar principal, disclosed fees and remaining invoice balance. Opening another account is a separate operational decision, not a prerequisite for recording the bill correctly.

What if the vendor invoices us in US dollars?

An overseas vendor’s location alone does not make the bill a foreign-currency transaction. If the invoice and settlement obligation are fixed in USD, record that obligation in dollars. Review any separately disclosed international payment fees on their own merits.

How should we correct an exchange-rate error after closing?

First identify whether the error concerns the original invoice, a payment, a fee or a closing adjustment. Reconstruct the correct foreign and dollar balances, then have the CPA approve the correction and its posting period. A missing fee or duplicate expense should not be corrected by forcing the difference into an exchange account.

Can we use the same currency schedule for tax reporting?

The schedule can provide supporting records, but book entries do not automatically determine tax treatment. Give the CPA the original invoices, rates, payment records, fees and period-end adjustments so tax treatment can be assessed separately.

Keep Every Currency Difference Explainable

The goal is a clear chain from the foreign invoice to the dollar financial statements. Preserve original amounts, document rates, separate disclosed fees, reconcile partial payments and review open balances at each close. With those controls, the owner can understand both what the practice purchased and why the eventual cash payment differed from the initial recorded cost.
