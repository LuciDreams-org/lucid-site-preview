Payment Provider Compliance Reviews for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A payment provider review should answer more than whether a vendor displays a compliance badge. For a staffed medical practice, the practical questions are: Who handles each payment step? Where do funds settle? What can delay access to cash? Who responds when a transaction is disputed or a service fails?

Before selecting or renewing a provider, assemble a review file that connects the contract to your practice’s actual payment flows. Include patient payments, refunds, recurring payment arrangements and any payer receipts handled through that provider. Do not assume all receipts pass through the same system.

The goal is an informed contracting decision—not a declaration that a provider is compliant. Provider assurances do not, by themselves, resolve the practice’s responsibilities. Legal counsel, privacy and security advisers, and qualified payment-security specialists should address questions within their expertise. Your finance team should assess settlement visibility, reconciliation and cash exposure.

1. Map the Payment Flows Before Reviewing the Sales Proposal

Start with a walkthrough involving the practice manager, billing lead and finance lead. Trace a payment from initiation through posting, settlement and any later refund or dispute.

For each flow, record the practice entity, location, payment channel, systems involved, merchant account identifier and destination bank account. Use masked identifiers in working summaries.

Payment flow What to document Key provider question
Front-desk patient payments Terminal or application, merchant account, location and deposit account Which legal entity supplies each service, and who supports failures?
Online patient balances Payment page, portal operator, processor and posting process Who operates the payment page, and how do payments reach the practice’s records?
Recurring patient payments Authorization process, cancellation workflow and responsible staff Who maintains authorization evidence and handles failed or canceled payments?
Payer receipts ACH, checks or virtual cards, where applicable Which receipts actually use this provider, and which follow separate banking arrangements?
Refunds and disputes Initiation, approval, funding source and ledger treatment Who may debit the practice’s account, under what terms, and with what notice?

For multi-location practices, distinguish location reporting from legal ownership. A location label on a dashboard does not establish which practice entity contracted for the account or received the cash.

Ask your CPA and counsel to review unclear entity arrangements before changing settlement instructions. Do not assume a shared provider account is appropriate merely because locations share administrative staff.

2. Build a Review File With Evidence, Not Just Answers

Keep one organized file for each provider relationship, with entity- and location-specific schedules where needed. Assign a practice employee to maintain it.

Request these materials before approval or renewal:

  • The proposed or executed agreement, incorporated terms, amendments and applicable fee schedules.
  • The contracting entity’s legal name and a description of the roles of the gateway, processor, acquiring bank, platform and other relevant parties.
  • Settlement, reserve, withholding and account-debit terms.
  • Refund and dispute procedures, including response deadlines and escalation contacts.
  • Relevant compliance evidence and a written explanation of its service coverage.
  • Subcontractor disclosures and the process for notifying the practice of material changes.
  • Incident, outage, termination and data-export procedures.
  • Sample settlement reports, statements and reconciliation exports.

For every document, record its version or date, the service it covers, the reviewer and any limitations. Preserve the terms that informed the decision rather than relying only on a web page that may later change.

A verbal assurance such as “we handle everything” should become a written, task-specific answer. If the provider will not supply a requested document, record that limitation and ask the appropriate adviser whether alternative evidence is sufficient.

3. Establish a Provider Responsibility Matrix

The following matrix is a review template, not a statement of any provider’s obligations. Replace the questions with confirmed responsibilities and contract references. Identify one accountable practice owner for each task, even when several parties contribute.

Area Provider responsibility to confirm Practice responsibility to assign Evidence and escalation owner
Payment acceptance Services, channels and entities included in the agreement Approve channels and maintain the account inventory Contract and service schedule; practice manager
Card-security requirements Provider’s covered services and support for identifying remaining merchant requirements Obtain qualified guidance and complete applicable practice tasks Relevant PCI DSS evidence and responsibility documentation; payment-security adviser
Settlement Timing, deductions, reserve triggers and destination accounts Verify deposits and investigate differences Settlement terms and sample reports; finance lead
Refunds Processing steps, funding and permissions Approve refunds and reconcile them to patient balances Refund procedure; billing manager
Disputes Notification channel, submission process, deadlines and fees Monitor notices and submit appropriate evidence Dispute guide and contract; billing manager, with counsel for legal questions

This exercise often exposes gaps between vendors. For example, a portal operator may direct the practice to a processor for disputes, while the processor sends notices to an inbox nobody monitors. The review is incomplete until a named employee owns that handoff.

4. Examine Settlement Terms as a Cash-Flow Risk

A payment approval is not the same as available cash. Review the contract and reporting together to understand when receipts become usable and what could change that timing.

Ask the provider:

  • What starts the settlement clock, and what cutoffs or exceptions apply?
  • Are deposits gross or net of fees, refunds and disputes?
  • When can funds be withheld or a reserve established, increased or released?
  • Can one account’s obligations affect another practice entity’s funds?
  • What rights allow debits from the practice’s bank account?
  • Who approves bank-account changes, and how are those changes verified?
  • What happens to pending settlements after suspension or termination?

Have counsel interpret the contractual rights. Have finance assess the operational consequences, including whether a delay could affect provider and administrative payroll or other committed expenses.

Test Whether the Reports Explain the Deposit

Illustrative example: A practice’s payment report shows $48,000 in processed receipts. The provider deducts $1,200 in refunds, $800 in fees and $2,000 in reserve withholding, producing a $44,000 bank deposit. Finance must be able to identify each component rather than treating the deposit alone as collections or revenue.

Ask for report fields that connect transactions, batches, deductions and deposits. Confirm that the reports support the practice’s entity and location reporting without relying on patient names in general financial summaries.

The IRS guidance on business records and bank reconciliation supports maintaining complete records and reconciling bank activity. A provider statement is one part of that record set, not a substitute for reconciliation.

5. Review Compliance Evidence Within Its Actual Scope

Separate three questions: What evidence does the provider supply? Which services does it cover? What remains for the practice to do?

For card payments, ask for relevant, current PCI DSS evidence and a description of the covered services. Use the PCI Security Standards Council as a reference point, and ask a qualified payment-security adviser to assess whether the evidence and responsibility allocation fit your acceptance channels. This is not a technical implementation checklist.

If a provider supplies a SOC report, have a qualified reviewer examine its scope, reporting period, exceptions and any customer responsibilities. Do not treat a report name, badge or marketing statement as proof that every contracted service is covered.

Follow the Data Through Subcontractors

Ask which third parties process transactions, host relevant information, provide support or handle disputes. Request an explanation of what information each receives, what function it performs and how material changes are communicated.

Health information requires a separate, fact-specific assessment. HHS guidance on covered entities and business associates provides the framework; counsel should evaluate the actual services and data involved rather than assuming every payment relationship has the same status.

If a separate cloud service maintains electronic protected health information on the practice’s behalf, the HHS cloud-computing guidance explains that business-associate obligations can apply even when the service lacks the decryption key. Encryption alone does not settle the question.

For routine financial analysis, use aggregate totals and transaction references where sufficient. Apply the HHS minimum necessary guidance with appropriate advice and attention to its exceptions. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

6. Walk Through Disputes, Outages and Exit Procedures

Before signing, test how the arrangement works when something goes wrong.

For a dispute, identify who receives the notice, who monitors that channel during absences, how the response deadline is determined and who submits evidence. Confirm any fees, account debits and appeal options in the applicable terms. Staff should use an approved process for deciding what information to disclose rather than sending complete patient records by default.

For an outage or delayed settlement, request operational and escalation contacts. Ask how the provider communicates status, which alternative payment workflows are supported and how delayed transactions appear in subsequent reports. Do not assume an alternative method is available until it has been confirmed.

For termination, establish how the practice obtains needed records, handles outstanding refunds and disputes, and verifies final settlements and reserves. If stored payment credentials or recurring arrangements are involved, ask what transition options exist and what approvals or limitations apply. Do not assume they can be transferred.

7. Use a Renewal Checklist and an Open-Issue Register

Review before the contractual notice window, not simply before the anniversary date. Counsel should confirm the actual renewal and termination provisions.

Renewal checkpoint Completion evidence Unresolved issue to record Escalation owner
Confirm service and entity scope Updated account and payment-flow inventory Missing location, entity mismatch or unclear service Practice manager and counsel
Review current terms Dated agreement, amendments and fee schedule Unexplained change or conflicting terms Owner and counsel
Recheck settlement exposure Reconciliation samples and reserve terms Unexplained deposits, hold rights or debit exposure Finance lead and counsel
Refresh compliance evidence Reviewed documents and scope notes Expired evidence, exclusions or unanswered responsibilities Security/privacy adviser
Review subcontractor changes Updated disclosures and data-flow notes Undisclosed party or unclear data access Privacy lead and counsel
Test dispute and incident contacts Confirmed contacts and internal coverage Unmonitored inbox or unclear response ownership Billing manager and practice manager
Confirm exit readiness Export sample and transition plan Inaccessible records or unclear final settlement Finance lead and counsel

Maintain an open-issue register alongside this checklist. Each issue should include the provider’s written response, missing evidence, potential financial or operational effect, responsible person, target date and decision consequence.

Use explicit statuses such as resolved, pending evidence, accepted with documented conditions or approval blocked. The practice owner should make the final commercial decision with adviser input. Do not mark an unanswered item complete merely because renewal is approaching.

Put the Financial Review in the Right Hands

A useful review ends with a documented decision: select, renew, negotiate conditions or pursue an alternative. Retain the evidence, remaining obligations and assigned follow-up work.

FAQs

Does using a payment provider remove the practice’s compliance responsibilities?

No. A provider’s assurances do not establish that all practice responsibilities have been addressed. Document the division of work, review the agreement and ask qualified advisers to identify obligations that remain with the practice.

What should a practice request before renewing a payment provider?

Request current contract terms, fee and settlement schedules, reserve and debit provisions, relevant compliance evidence, subcontractor disclosures, dispute procedures and exit terms. Compare those documents with actual statements, unresolved reconciliation differences and the practice’s current payment flows.

What if the provider will not supply requested evidence?

Record the refusal or limitation and ask whether alternative evidence is available. Escalate the gap to the adviser responsible for that subject. The owner should decide whether to delay approval, negotiate conditions or consider another provider; missing evidence should not be recorded as a completed review.

Who should lead the review in a staffed medical practice?

The practice manager can coordinate the file, with finance reviewing settlements and cash exposure, billing reviewing refunds and disputes, and qualified advisers reviewing legal, privacy and security questions. The owner should approve the final decision and any accepted unresolved risks.
