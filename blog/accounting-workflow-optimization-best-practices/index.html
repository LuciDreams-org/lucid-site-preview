Accounting Workflow Best Practices for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice’s monthly accounting workflow should answer three questions: Who owns each task? What information do they need? What must happen before the next person can proceed?

When those answers are unclear, practice managers chase receipts, bookkeepers wait for payroll reports, and owners receive financial statements with unresolved questions. Buying automation does not resolve an unclear handoff or an approval that nobody owns.

The goal is a repeatable path from source documents to reviewed financial statements. For a staffed private medical practice, that path should connect payer and patient receipts, vendor expenses, provider and administrative payroll, reconciliations, and owner decisions—without turning the accounting team into a second billing department.

This guide explains how to organize that workflow before choosing additional tools.

1. Define What a Completed Monthly Close Includes

Start with the reporting package the owner needs, then work backward to the tasks that produce it.

A practical monthly package may include:

  • A profit and loss statement and balance sheet for each legal entity.
  • A per-location P&L when location tracking is maintained.
  • A cash summary showing available balances and known upcoming obligations.
  • Explanations of significant changes in collections, payroll and operating expenses.
  • A list of unresolved items and how they affect the reports.

Agree with the reviewing CPA on the accounting basis and reporting policies. Billing-system charges, outstanding patient balances and cash received are different measures; they should not be treated as interchangeable revenue figures.

Define completion explicitly: required accounts reconciled, supporting schedules prepared, review questions resolved or documented, and the reporting package approved for release. Label preliminary reports clearly rather than letting an unfinished version circulate as final.

2. Map the Current Workflow Before Redesigning It

Trace one recent month from the first source document through report approval. Ask the people doing the work to show actual handoffs, including spreadsheets, emails and repeated requests.

For each step, record:

  • The input and where it comes from.
  • The person responsible and their backup.
  • The action performed and evidence of completion.
  • The next recipient.
  • The reason work sometimes stops.

Look for three problems before selecting automation:

Problem What it can look like in a practice Workflow correction
Duplicate entry The manager and bookkeeper both enter the same vendor bill Assign one entry owner; make the other person an approver or reviewer
Missing source documents A card charge appears without an invoice or business purpose Assign collection responsibility and track the missing evidence
Approval bottleneck Routine expenses and unusual owner transactions all wait in the same inbox Define approval categories, required context and backup approvers
Unclear reporting source Billing and accounting teams use different collections reports Designate the report, period and owner for each handoff
Repeated reclassification Shared expenses move between locations each month Document the allocation policy and who may change it

A bank-feed transaction is not a substitute for every supporting document. The IRS guide to business recordkeeping explains the importance of complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciling bank records.

3. Assign Responsibilities Across the Entire Workflow

A responsibility matrix should distinguish preparation, operational confirmation, accounting review and final approval. One person may fill several roles in a smaller practice, but those responsibilities should remain visible.

Use this as a starting point, not a universal staffing model:

Workstream Preparer or coordinator Operational confirmation Accounting review or approval
Monthly document collection Practice manager or designated administrator Department or location contact supplies missing items Bookkeeper checks completeness
Payer and patient receipt summaries Billing lead provides agreed reports Practice manager explains operational discrepancies Bookkeeper records activity; CPA reviews accounting questions
Vendor transaction coding Bookkeeper Manager confirms purpose, entity and location CPA reviews unusual classifications
Payroll handoff Payroll administrator supplies final reports Authorized manager confirms provider/admin and location assignments Bookkeeper records payroll; CPA reviews accounting treatment
Account reconciliations Bookkeeper Manager supplies missing explanations CPA or designated accounting reviewer reviews work
Shared-cost allocations Bookkeeper applies documented policy Manager validates operational inputs CPA reviews method; owner approves policy changes
Exception resolution Assigned owner for each issue Relevant staff member confirms facts CPA resolves accounting judgments; owner decides business matters
Monthly report release Bookkeeper assembles package Manager confirms operational explanations CPA completes financial review; owner accepts the management package

Financial review is not the same as authorization to release payments. Keep payment permissions separate from bookkeeping responsibilities where staffing permits. If a person performs multiple roles, document an independent review of sensitive activity rather than assuming software replaces oversight.

4. Standardize Document Collection and Payroll Handoffs

Create one monthly checklist organized by entity, reporting period and document type. Include bank and card statements, vendor invoices, payroll reports, financing statements and agreed billing-system summaries.

Use consistent file names and a designated storage location. The checklist should distinguish “received” from “reviewed”: uploading a file does not establish that it covers the correct period or contains everything needed.

Define the receipts handoff

The billing lead should supply the agreed summary reports and explanations needed for accounting. The bookkeeper should not need to reconstruct billing operations from scattered screenshots.

Specify how the handoff identifies payer receipts, patient payments, refunds, processor fees and timing differences. Questions about individual account balances should remain with authorized billing personnel; accounting can usually work from summaries and exception references without patient names.

Define the payroll handoff

The payroll package should identify the final payroll version, applicable pay periods, funding activity and relevant liability or benefit reports. Include approved provider-versus-administrative and location assignments when the financial reports require them.

Set a clear correction procedure. If payroll changes after the initial handoff, the payroll administrator should flag the replacement report and explain what changed. Otherwise, the bookkeeper may record an outdated version or duplicate activity.

Payroll authorization and monthly accounting review are separate processes. The accounting close should not become the mechanism for approving employee pay after the fact.

Limit sensitive information

Use only the information needed for each accounting task. HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions. Design routine financial reporting to avoid unnecessary patient information.

Do not place patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. If accounting work requires access to protected health information, evaluate the applicable arrangements and responsibilities; HHS identifies accounting among services that can involve business-associate responsibilities.

5. Use a Close Calendar Built Around Dependencies

A useful close calendar shows what unlocks the next stage. Assign actual target dates based on statement availability, staffing and reporting needs—not an assumed universal turnaround time.

The following is an illustrative monthly close sequence, not a promised schedule:

Calendar stage Work to complete Dependency Completion evidence
Throughout the month Collect invoices, confirm expense purposes and log unusual transactions Staff submit information as activity occurs Updated document checklist and exception list
Before month-end Confirm report owners, backup approvers and expected changes Staff availability and known operational changes Assigned close checklist
After period-end reports become available Collect statements, final payroll reports and billing summaries Source systems finish the relevant reporting period Complete, period-matched source package
After source validation Record transactions and prepare supporting schedules Correct entity, period and coding information Updated ledger and documented coding questions
As each account becomes ready Complete reconciliations and identify differences Relevant entries and statements are available Prepared reconciliation with unresolved items identified
After preparation Review classifications, allocations and significant changes Reconciliations and schedules are ready CPA review notes and proposed corrections
After review questions are addressed Approve and release the reporting package Corrections posted or remaining limitations documented Dated release approval and clearly labeled reports
After release Review workflow problems and assign improvements Feedback from the completed close Updated process and next-cycle actions

Not every task must wait for every other task. A complete card account can move forward while a separate loan statement is pending. However, reports should not be presented as final while a significant unresolved issue could change the owner’s conclusions.

6. Give Exceptions and Approvals Their Own Process

A missing receipt, unexplained deposit and disputed allocation need different owners. Putting them all into a general “accounting questions” thread makes resolution harder.

Maintain an exception register with:

  • The account, entity, location and period affected.
  • A concise description and reference to supporting material.
  • The person responsible for the next action.
  • The decision or document needed.
  • The next follow-up point and escalation route.
  • The resolution and any resulting accounting adjustment.

Illustrative example: A deposit contains both payer receipts and a patient refund adjustment, but the summary does not explain the components. The bookkeeper logs the difference; the billing lead supplies the breakdown; the CPA reviews any accounting judgment. The owner need not become the first stop for routine clarification.

Reserve owner attention for decisions that require it, such as uncertain business purposes, owner-related transactions or changes to allocation policy. For ordinary approvals, give the designated manager enough context to decide without searching through multiple systems.

Agree with the CPA which unresolved items prevent report release and which can remain documented with appropriate treatment. A missing document should not disappear from the checklist merely because the transaction has been categorized.

7. Add Entity and Location Controls Where Needed

Multi-location and multi-entity practices need more than a location label on the final P&L.

At transaction entry, identify the legal entity that incurred the expense and the location benefiting from it. Document shared-cost methods for central administration, payroll or other common expenses. Validate the allocation inputs before applying the method each month.

If one entity pays another entity’s expense, flag the transaction for appropriate accounting rather than simply assigning it to a location. Location reporting does not replace entity-level books or separate business accounts.

The close checklist should also identify missing location information before review. Otherwise, a consolidated result may look complete while individual location margins remain unreliable.

8. Choose Automation Only After Ownership Is Clear

Once the workflow is stable, identify repetitive work with defined inputs and review criteria. Document reminders, recurring task lists and draft coding suggestions may be useful candidates. Availability and suitability depend on the tools selected.

Before changing a process, ask:

  • Does this remove a duplicate step or merely move it elsewhere?
  • Who checks incomplete or incorrect output?
  • How are exceptions returned to a responsible person?
  • What happens if the tool or assigned employee is unavailable?
  • Does the information involved require additional privacy review?

Pilot one change through a complete close cycle. Compare rework, missing documents, unresolved questions and approval waiting time—not just the number of tasks marked complete.

Make the Workflow Repeatable

Start with a shared checklist, a named owner for each handoff and a clear definition of report approval. Then use the next close to identify where work stalls. Improve the process before adding more software.

FAQs

Who should own the monthly accounting workflow in a medical practice?

Assign one coordinator, often the practice manager, to track handoffs and missing inputs. That does not make the manager responsible for every accounting judgment. The bookkeeper prepares the books, designated staff confirm operational facts, the CPA reviews accounting matters, and the owner makes business decisions and accepts the reporting package.

What should we fix before buying accounting automation?

Identify duplicate entry, missing source documents, unclear coding policies and approvals without an assigned decision-maker. Define the authoritative source for each report and the evidence needed to complete each task. Automation should support that process rather than compensate for undefined responsibilities.

Can we close the books with unresolved questions?

It depends on the nature and significance of the questions. The reviewing CPA should determine the appropriate accounting treatment and whether reports can be released with documented limitations. Clearly distinguish preliminary from approved reports, and keep unresolved items assigned until they are addressed.

How should a practice measure whether the workflow is improving?

Track missing documents, repeated requests, corrections after review, approval waiting time and unresolved items carried forward. Compare successive closes while accounting for staffing or operational changes. A useful workflow produces understandable, reviewed reports—not merely a faster checklist completion date.
