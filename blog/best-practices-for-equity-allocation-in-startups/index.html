Preparing Equity Allocation Decisions for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An equity discussion in a staffed medical practice should start with records, not a percentage. Whether you are considering a physician buy-in, adding an eligible co-owner or revisiting an existing arrangement, the first task is to establish what is owned today, what each person has contributed and what the proposed change is intended to accomplish.

A proposed ownership percentage does not, by itself, explain compensation, voting authority, distributions or responsibility for future funding. Those issues need separate discussion and documentation.

This guide explains how to prepare the financial inputs for that discussion. It does not recommend an equity-splitting formula, establish a practice valuation or prescribe a vesting arrangement. Ownership eligibility, entity structure, enforceability and tax treatment require practice-specific advice from licensed legal and tax advisers.

1. Define the Decision Before Negotiating Percentages

Write a short statement of the proposed change. Identify the people and entities involved, the business objective and the questions that remain open.

For example, an existing owner may want to explore whether an employed physician could become an owner while preserving sufficient working capital for provider and administrative payroll. A multi-location group may instead be considering an interest in one entity rather than in the entire organization. These are different decisions, even if both discussions begin with the same proposed percentage.

Your initial decision statement should answer:

  • Who is being considered for ownership, subject to legal eligibility review?
  • Which legal entity would be involved?
  • Would money go into the practice, to an existing owner or somewhere else?
  • Is the objective succession, new capital, leadership continuity or another documented need?
  • What would change about work responsibilities, compensation and decision-making?
  • What must be resolved before anyone makes a commitment?

Ask counsel to assess eligibility and structural constraints early. Do not assume that a physician, administrator, employee, outside investor or adviser can receive an ownership interest merely because that arrangement is common in another industry.

2. Establish the Current Ownership Baseline

Prepare an ownership schedule by legal entity. Use signed governing documents and transaction records as the starting point, rather than relying only on a spreadsheet or the equity accounts in the bookkeeping system.

Record What to capture Question to resolve
Governing documents and amendments Entity name, owners, interests and documented rights Do the documents match everyone's understanding?
Ownership issuances and transfers Parties, dates, interests and supporting agreements Were prior changes documented and approved?
Contribution and owner-loan records Amounts, dates, classification and balances Was funding recorded as capital, debt or something unresolved?
Buy-sell and related agreements Existing transfer, departure and purchase provisions Could existing provisions affect the proposal?
Ownership summary Current interests by person and entity Does the summary reconcile with the underlying records?

Keep an exceptions list. If a prior payment was called a loan in correspondence but posted as equity in the books, flag the conflict for review. Do not change its classification simply to make a proposed allocation easier to explain.

Separate entities from locations

A location-level profit and loss statement is not an ownership record. One entity may operate several locations, while a practice group may also have separate operating, property or other related entities.

Map the entities, bank accounts, locations and intercompany balances before modeling a change. Someone discussing an interest in an operating practice should not have to guess whether the proposal also includes a building or another entity.

3. Reconcile the Financial Records Behind the Discussion

An allocation discussion can become misleading when the underlying financial picture is incomplete. Start with reconciled accounts and clearly identify the period and accounting basis used in each report.

The IRS business recordkeeping guidance in Publication 583 supports maintaining complete business records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. These practices help establish a reliable starting point; they do not determine what an ownership interest is worth.

Prepare a financial package that includes:

  • Balance sheets and profit and loss statements for agreed historical periods, plus current year-to-date results.
  • Bank and credit account reconciliations, with unresolved items identified.
  • Debt schedules, owner loans and amounts due between related entities.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, owner compensation and distributions shown separately.
  • Aggregate payer and patient receivable information, with collection assumptions and limitations explained.
  • Location-level results where relevant, including the method used to allocate shared expenses.
  • Expected cash requirements for payroll, occupancy, debt payments and planned expenditures.

For practices using cash-basis reports, explain that receipts during a period may relate to services delivered earlier. Ask the CPA what supplemental receivable, payable or other information is needed to interpret those results.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. Patient-identifiable details generally are not needed to compare ownership scenarios and should not be placed in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

4. Document Contributions Without Treating Them as an Equity Formula

Create a contribution register that separates historical facts from proposed future commitments. Each entry should identify the contributor, date or period, supporting evidence, accounting treatment and unresolved questions.

Contribution or commitment Evidence to assemble Important distinction
Cash funding Bank records, agreements and ledger entries Capital and owner loans are not interchangeable
Equipment or other property Purchase records, transfer documents and related debt Original cost does not establish current value
Prior operating or management work Role descriptions, payroll records and agreements Work may already have been compensated
Personal guarantees Signed loan or lease documents A guarantee is not cash contributed
Future leadership responsibilities Proposed duties, authority and time commitment A future promise is not a completed contribution
Future funding Proposed amount, timing and conditions Identify what remains contingent or unfunded

Avoid assigning an ownership percentage to each contribution category before advisers have reviewed the facts. A cash contribution ratio is useful evidence about funding history, but it is not automatically a fair, lawful or tax-appropriate allocation of ownership.

Also identify whether prior contributions have already been repaid or otherwise addressed. Counting the same contribution both as an outstanding loan and as a justification for additional ownership can obscure the actual proposal.

5. Separate Compensation, Ownership and Economic Rights

Prepare a compensation schedule alongside the ownership records. Include clinical compensation, management pay, bonuses, benefits, reimbursements and owner distributions, with their current treatment confirmed by the CPA.

This prevents an equity discussion from becoming an unclear substitute for a compensation discussion. A provider's additional clinical work, an owner's administrative responsibilities and an ownership interest are related business considerations, but they are not the same thing.

Document the proposed rights separately:

Topic Question for the discussion packet
Ownership interest What interest is being proposed, in which entity?
Distributions What economic participation is contemplated, subject to documents and available cash?
Voting and management Which decisions would the person influence or approve?
Compensation What payments would continue for clinical or management work?
Future funding What additional funding obligations are being considered?
Departure or reduced work What questions arise if duties, eligibility or employment change?
Transfer or sale What existing restrictions or approvals need review?

Treat this as a list of questions for advisers, not a draft legal agreement. Do not assume that a stated ownership percentage carries identical voting, distribution and exit rights.

6. Compare Alternatives Using Explicit Assumptions

A useful scenario model shows what changes, what stays unchanged and what has not yet been decided. Include the current arrangement as a baseline rather than assuming that a transaction must happen.

Illustrative example: cash contributions versus a future ownership proposal

The following figures are hypothetical and are not valuation guidance or a recommended allocation.

Assume an existing owner previously contributed $120,000 in documented capital to a practice. An employed physician proposes contributing $30,000 to the practice in exchange for a future 25% ownership interest, subject to eligibility, documentation and adviser review.

The proposed contribution represents 20% of the combined $150,000 in those two contributions. That arithmetic does not establish that the physician should receive 20%, 25% or any other ownership interest. Historical funding is not the same as current practice value, and the proposal may involve rights and obligations that have not yet been defined.

Scenario Cash entering the practice Ownership assumption for discussion Key unresolved question
Current arrangement continues No new contribution Existing owner retains the current interest Does the practice need additional funding or a succession plan?
Proposed future allocation $30,000 Existing owner 75%; incoming owner 25% after a contemplated closing What valuation, rights and transaction terms would support this proposal?
Alternative allocation for comparison $30,000 Existing owner 80%; incoming owner 20% after a contemplated closing Why would this allocation be appropriate beyond matching contribution arithmetic?

Neither allocation is endorsed. Both are discussion cases requiring legal, tax and potentially valuation review.

For an additional illustrative cash-flow comparison, suppose the practice sets aside $80,000 as a hypothetical amount available for distribution after its operating and reserve assumptions. If advisers confirm that distributions would follow the modeled percentages, a 25% share would be $20,000 and a 20% share would be $16,000. These figures are not compensation, guaranteed payments or tax calculations.

The example also highlights transaction direction: cash paid to an existing owner would not create the same practice cash balance as cash contributed to the entity. Model those alternatives separately.

Stress-test cash needs, not just ownership percentages

Ask whether each scenario leaves enough liquidity if payer receipts arrive later than expected, patient balances collect more slowly or staffing expenses rise. For a multi-location practice, show whether shared-cost allocation changes the apparent profitability of the location involved.

State assumptions visibly. Do not describe accounting profit as automatically available for distribution, and do not treat an illustrative ownership model as evidence of market value.

7. Assemble an Adviser Briefing Packet

Give legal and tax advisers a consistent, dated packet so they are reviewing the same facts.

Packet section Contents
Decision summary Objective, people, entities, proposed timing and open questions
Ownership baseline Governing documents, amendments, ownership schedule and discrepancies
Contribution history Capital, loans, property, guarantees and supporting evidence
Compensation arrangements Employment agreements, compensation schedules and distribution history
Financial baseline Reconciled statements, debt, receivables, cash needs and shared-cost methods
Proposed rights Economic participation, voting, responsibilities, funding and departure questions
Scenario comparison Current arrangement and alternatives with assumptions clearly labeled
Issue log Missing records, conflicting classifications and decisions requiring advice

Ask counsel about ownership eligibility, permitted structure, required approvals, enforceability and any healthcare regulatory or contractual implications. Ask the CPA about transaction classification, entity and individual tax consequences, reporting obligations and any time-sensitive steps. Ask whether an independent valuation specialist is needed.

Assign someone to record decisions and follow-up items. Keep proposed terms visibly separate from approved and executed terms, and update the financial records only after the appropriate documentation and accounting review.

Turn the Discussion Into a Documented Decision

The goal of preparation is not to prove that one percentage is inherently fair. It is to make the facts, trade-offs and unanswered questions visible before owners commit.

FAQs

Does contributing more cash automatically entitle someone to more medical practice equity?

No. Contribution records document funding history, but they do not independently establish ownership rights or current value. Review the existing agreements, whether the money was capital or a loan, and the proposed transaction with legal and tax advisers.

Can a medical practice offer ownership to an employee or administrator?

Do not assume eligibility. The answer requires review of the person's qualifications, applicable state requirements, the entity and the proposed arrangement. Obtain practice-specific legal advice before promising or documenting an ownership award.

Should owners use a standard equity split or vesting schedule?

A standard percentage or schedule from another industry is not a medical-practice rule. First document the objective, contributions, compensation, proposed rights and departure questions. Advisers can then assess which arrangements, if any, are appropriate and enforceable.

Do clean financial statements establish the value of a practice?

No. They provide inputs for analysis. A valuation may require additional work on earnings, assets, liabilities, collection expectations and other practice-specific factors. Ask advisers whether a valuation specialist is needed before treating a proposed buy-in amount as support for an ownership percentage.
