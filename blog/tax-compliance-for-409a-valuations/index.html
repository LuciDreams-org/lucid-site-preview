409A Valuation Records for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Offering a physician, administrator or other employee a stake in future practice value can raise questions that ordinary payroll records cannot answer. Before promising equity-linked compensation or a deferred payment, owners need to understand what they are offering, which entity is responsible and what records their advisers need to review.

Do not assume that every private medical practice needs a startup-style stock valuation. Whether Section 409A is relevant depends on the actual entity, instrument and compensation arrangement. A description such as “ownership bonus” or “equity incentive” is not enough to establish the treatment.

This guide focuses on preparing records and questions for licensed tax and legal advisers. It does not determine whether an arrangement is subject to Section 409A, prescribe valuation safe harbors or provide a compensation-plan design. The practical goal is a complete review file and a clear decision process before the practice makes commitments or changes an existing arrangement.

Start With the Arrangement, Not the Valuation Order

A practice owner’s first decision should be how to scope the professional review—not which valuation package to purchase.

Ask counsel and your CPA to review the proposed arrangement together. Counsel can evaluate the legal documents and compensation terms; the CPA can address tax questions and the supporting financial records. If a valuation is needed, a qualified valuation professional should receive a scope consistent with that review.

Prepare a short arrangement summary covering:

  • The legal entity making the promise or issuing the interest.
  • The intended recipient and their relationship to the practice.
  • What the recipient would receive: an ownership interest, an option, a cash payment tied to value or another benefit.
  • The service, performance or other conditions attached to the benefit.
  • When amounts would be earned, vested and paid under the proposal.
  • What would happen upon departure, a sale, disability, death or another specified event.
  • Whether anyone has already signed, approved or communicated terms.

Distinguish a proposal from an executed agreement. If an offer letter already contains a promise, include it even if the formal plan is unfinished. Do not rewrite or backdate historical documents to make the file appear consistent; ask advisers how to address discrepancies.

Keep Different Valuation Questions Separate

A medical practice may have several reasons to discuss value. They are not interchangeable.

Owner’s question Records to bring Issue to clarify with advisers
Does this compensation arrangement raise Section 409A questions? Proposed and executed terms, entity documents, payment conditions and prior advice Whether and how Section 409A is relevant to this specific arrangement
What is the practice worth for a sale or owner buy-in? Transaction terms, financial statements, ownership rights and forecasts The valuation purpose, subject interest and appropriate scope
Can an existing report support a proposed award? Full report, stated purpose, valuation date, underlying data and award terms Whether that report is suitable for this use or additional work is needed
How should a cash incentive linked to practice value be structured? Draft formula, payment provisions, employment terms and financial inputs The legal and tax review needed before adoption

A sale valuation or buy-sell agreement may be relevant background, but it should not automatically be treated as sufficient support for compensation decisions.

Likewise, this is not an incentive stock option versus nonqualified stock option comparison or a phantom-equity tax guide. Those labels do not replace a review of the actual documents, entity and payment terms.

Build an Adviser-Ready Document Request Checklist

Create an index with a document owner, version date, status and storage location for each item. Ask advisers which historical periods and supporting detail they need rather than assuming a universal lookback period.

Identify the Correct Entity and Economic Interest

For a multi-entity practice, include a simple entity map. Identify the employer, the entity promising the benefit, the entity whose value determines the benefit and the entity expected to fund any payment. These may not be the same.

For example, a practice may have separate operating entities and a shared administrative company. Advisers need to know whether a proposed incentive refers to one location, one entity or the broader organization. A combined financial report does not answer that question by itself.

Do not force every practice into a corporate stock template. Provide the actual ownership and governing records, and let advisers determine which additional schedules are appropriate.

Make Financial Inputs Traceable

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. Those are useful foundations for preparing a reliable financial package; they do not establish a Section 409A conclusion.

Before sending the package, have the finance team:

  • Reconcile bank and other relevant balance-sheet accounts.
  • Identify whether statements use cash, accrual or another reporting basis.
  • Explain how payer receipts and patient balances appear in the reports.
  • Separate provider compensation, administrative payroll and owner distributions in the supporting schedules.
  • Document shared-cost allocations across locations and entities.
  • Flag incomplete periods, unusual entries and unresolved bookkeeping issues.

Do not present an unclosed month as final. If catch-up work remains, label the statements accordingly and tell advisers what may change.

Explain Practice-Specific Changes Without Drawing Tax Conclusions

A list of financial statements is not enough if it omits changes that affect how the practice operates. Maintain a business-change log and send relevant developments to advisers for assessment.

Useful entries may include a provider joining or leaving, an acquisition, a location opening or closure, a significant payer-contract change, new financing or a revised compensation arrangement. These are review prompts, not automatic legal triggers or automatic requirements for a new valuation.

For each entry, record the event date, affected entity, available documents and expected financial effect. Separate confirmed facts from management estimates.

Illustrative Example: A Two-Location Practice

An owner proposes a future cash bonus for an administrator based on growth in one location’s value. The practice has also changed how it allocates centralized payroll and billing costs between its two locations.

The review file should include the proposed bonus terms, the relevant entity documents, location-level P&Ls and the old and new allocation methods. Otherwise, advisers could receive a growth forecast without understanding that part of the apparent improvement comes from a reporting change.

The owner should not assume that calling the payment a “bonus” resolves its treatment, or that a valuation of the entire practice answers a question about one location. Advisers should establish the review scope before the owner finalizes the promise.

Use a Responsibility Calendar With Adviser-Established Dates

There is no single calendar suitable for every arrangement. Have the tax and legal advisers establish the applicable dates, review intervals and event-based checkpoints. Then assign an internal owner to track each item.

Track unresolved questions alongside the calendar. A scheduled grant or payment should not quietly proceed because the calendar reminder arrived while required advice remained outstanding. Escalate the decision to the owner and advisers.

Questions to Resolve Before Making a Commitment

Use the review meeting to obtain clear next steps rather than a general assurance that the paperwork looks reasonable.

  1. What exactly are we offering? Confirm the relevant entity, instrument, recipient and economic rights.
  2. What is the Section 409A analysis for these terms? Ask advisers to identify assumptions, missing facts and any additional specialist review needed.
  3. Is a valuation required for this purpose? If so, clarify the subject interest, purpose, relevant date and qualifications needed for the engagement.
  4. Can any existing report be used? Ask whether its scope, assumptions and supporting information fit the proposed use.
  5. Which actions need review first? Identify proposed communications, approvals, amendments, grants or payments that should await further advice.
  6. Who handles administration? Establish responsibility for maintaining schedules, obtaining instructions and coordinating with payroll.
  7. What changes should we report? Have advisers define the information they need when business conditions or compensation terms change.
  8. How should the file be retained? Obtain instructions suited to the documents and circumstances rather than applying a generic destruction date.

Keep a written action list showing who owns each answer. Preserve relevant written adviser guidance with the version of the arrangement it addresses.

Keep the Review File Useful and Appropriately Limited

Use a controlled document repository agreed with your advisers. Keep executed documents distinct from drafts, preserve prior versions and use consistent file names. A simple status register—requested, received, under review, final or superseded—can prevent confusion.

Financial analysis generally should not require sending patient-level detail by default. Start with aggregate financial reports and ask whether additional information is actually needed. The HHS minimum necessary guidance provides context for limiting protected health information where that standard applies; the appropriate handling process depends on the circumstances. Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

The Owner’s Next Step

Before commissioning a valuation, assemble the arrangement summary, governing documents, financial package and change log. Ask licensed tax and legal advisers to determine the review scope, identify missing records and establish the calendar.

The goal is not to reproduce a startup compliance checklist. It is to make a specific compensation decision using the correct entity records, reliable financial information and advice tied to the actual arrangement.

FAQs

Does every private medical practice need a 409A valuation?

No blanket assumption is appropriate. Advisers need to review the entity, instrument and compensation terms before determining whether Section 409A is relevant and whether a valuation is needed. Having employees, offering an incentive or discussing ownership does not by itself establish the answer.

Can we use the valuation prepared for an owner buy-in?

Provide the complete report to your advisers, including its purpose, valuation date and assumptions. Do not assume it supports a different compensation decision. Advisers and the valuation professional should assess whether it is suitable for the proposed use or whether additional work is necessary.

What if we already promised equity-linked compensation?

Collect the original communications, signed agreements, approvals and records of any payments or amendments. Tell counsel and your CPA what has happened and what actions are planned next. Preserve the history rather than changing documents to make them appear consistent, and obtain advice before taking further action.

How often should we update our records or obtain another valuation?

Keep records current as documents and business facts change. Have advisers establish the review cadence and determine when a valuation or other analysis is needed for the specific arrangement. Do not rely on a generic annual schedule or an unsupported percentage-change trigger.
