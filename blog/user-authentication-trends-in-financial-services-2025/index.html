Modern Authentication for Medical Practice Finance Access

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A practice manager loses the phone used to approve payroll logins. An outside accountant needs bank statements before month-end close. A departing administrator’s personal email is still the recovery address for a financial account.

These situations make authentication a practice-finance issue, not just an IT concern. Owners need dependable access to payer receipts, patient-balance collections, payroll and entity-level financial records without relying on shared credentials or one person’s device.

Modern options—including passkeys and phishing-resistant multifactor authentication—can strengthen sign-in protection. But the owner’s decision is broader than choosing a login method: who will enroll each user, approve recovery and keep essential financial work moving when someone is unavailable?

This guide focuses on human access to financial systems. It is not a technical configuration guide, an API integration plan or a substitute for an IT security review.

Separate Identity Verification From Financial Permissions

Authentication answers: Is this person the account holder they claim to be? Authorization answers: What may this account do?

A strong sign-in method does not determine whether someone should release payroll, change payment instructions or view every entity’s accounts.

Practice role Authentication question Separate permission decision
Owner Can the system verify the owner’s named account? Which entity accounts and payment approvals does the owner need?
Practice manager Can the manager sign in without using the owner’s credentials? May the manager prepare payroll, approve it, or both?
External accountant Does each authorized accountant have identifiable access? Is statement and transaction access sufficient, without payment authority?
Location administrator Can the administrator use an individual account? Should access cover one location or the whole practice?

Keep these decisions separate during a rollout. A successful login should not become a reason to expand financial authority. Likewise, resetting an authentication method should not automatically restore permissions that were previously removed.

Understand the Authentication Developments That Matter

Passkeys reduce reliance on reusable passwords

A passkey uses cryptographic credentials associated with a service rather than asking the user to type a reusable password. Depending on the service and credential type, a passkey may be available through a device, a credential provider or a hardware security key.

A fingerprint, facial scan or device PIN may unlock use of that credential. That local unlock step is not the same as sending a fingerprint to the bank or accounting system.

Passkeys are designed to bind authentication to the legitimate service, helping resist counterfeit sign-in pages. The FIDO Alliance’s explanation of FIDO2 describes the underlying approach, while the WebAuthn standard documents web authentication mechanisms.

For a practice owner, the important questions are operational:

  • Does this specific financial service support passkeys for the account type you use?
  • Who controls the device or credential-provider account holding the passkey?
  • What happens if the user loses that device or leaves the practice?
  • Can the user enroll a separately protected backup method?
  • Does recovery introduce a weaker route into the account?

Do not assume a personal phone is the right place for every practice credential. Have IT review device ownership, credential storage and recovery before enrollment.

Phishing-resistant MFA is different from ordinary MFA

Multifactor authentication uses different categories of evidence, such as something a person knows and something they possess. Not all MFA methods resist phishing in the same way.

Text-message codes, authenticator-app codes and ordinary approval prompts can add protection beyond a password, but users may still be tricked into disclosing a code or approving an attacker’s request. Appropriately implemented FIDO-based authentication is designed to resist that kind of credential phishing.

Method Practical consideration Question for IT or the provider
Password plus text code Depends on access to a phone number; codes can be phished What stronger options are available, and how are number changes verified?
Password plus app code or approval prompt Adds a factor, but codes and approvals can still be manipulated What happens when the phone is replaced or a prompt is approved by mistake?
Passkey Can provide phishing-resistant sign-in; storage and recovery differ Where is it held, how is it unlocked, and what fallback remains?
FIDO-capable hardware security key Can provide phishing-resistant authentication when supported Who issues it, where is the backup kept, and how is loss handled?

“Passwordless” does not automatically mean phishing-resistant, and no method eliminates fraud. An authenticated user can still be deceived into approving a fraudulent payment. Payment verification remains a separate financial control.

Recovery deserves the same attention as sign-in

An account with strong everyday authentication may still be vulnerable through a poorly protected recovery email or an easily manipulated support process.

Review recovery addresses, backup methods, provider identity checks and administrator reset powers. A familiar voice, urgent message or video call should not, by itself, authorize a recovery change. Use a documented verification route independent of the incoming request.

Build a Finance Authentication Inventory

Start with the systems that move money or are necessary to run payroll and close the books. Include the email accounts used for password resets; they can be a dependency for several financial services.

The inventory should describe access arrangements—not contain passwords, recovery codes, patient information or copies of identity documents.

System or account group Named users and business purpose Authentication details to record Recovery and continuity details
Bank accounts, by legal entity Owner, manager and accountant as appropriate Available methods, enrolled users and device ownership Provider recovery route, authorized contact and independently enrolled backup user
Payroll Preparer, approver and external payroll adviser Method for each person and any enrollment gaps Recovery approver and authorized alternate for payroll deadlines
Accounting system Finance team and external accountant Individual accounts, supported methods and reset dependencies Administrator, backup administrator and support route
Payer and payment portals Staff who retrieve receipts or settlement reports Individual access availability and enrolled methods Organization-level recovery process and departing-user procedure
Finance email accounts People receiving statements or recovery messages Sign-in method and recovery dependencies Verified recovery contact and alternate administrator

For multi-entity practices, identify the legal entity and account owner rather than recording only the vendor name. One bank relationship may contain accounts with different authorized users or recovery arrangements.

Assign a business owner and an IT reviewer to each inventory entry. Record the review date and unresolved gaps. If a provider appears to require shared access, ask about named-user or delegated-access options before accepting credential sharing as the default.

Plan an IT-Reviewed Rollout Around Financial Deadlines

1. Rank systems by financial consequence

Prioritize access whose loss could interrupt payroll, payment approvals or visibility into cash receipts. Include any email account that can reset those systems.

Do not switch every critical account immediately before a payroll run. Choose a pilot with a defined test window and a provider-supported recovery path.

2. Confirm support rather than assuming it

Have IT verify available methods for the actual account and user types. A provider may offer different options for an owner, employee and external accountant.

Ask how enrollment, device replacement, fallback and account recovery work. Confirm whether an alternate authorized user can continue essential work without borrowing the locked-out user’s credentials.

3. Give people their own access

Enroll owners, managers and outside accountants individually. Where supported and approved, use access intended for external advisers rather than sharing an employee’s login.

The owner approves the business need. The manager coordinates enrollment. IT reviews the authentication and recovery arrangements. The provider controls which methods its service supports.

4. Test the workflow before expanding

Test ordinary sign-in and an approved recovery scenario. Confirm that users can complete their actual tasks, such as retrieving bank statements or preparing payroll, without receiving unnecessary authority.

Provide a supported alternative for staff who cannot use a particular biometric method or personal device. Strong authentication should not depend on everyone owning the same phone or using the same physical interaction.

5. Document the go-live decision

Expand only after the pilot establishes that named access, recovery contacts and alternate coverage work. Keep exceptions visible, with an owner and review date. Avoid “temporary” shared credentials becoming the permanent solution.

Run a Lost-Device Recovery Tabletop

Illustrative scenario: The practice manager loses the phone used for payroll authentication on payroll preparation day. The owner is away from the office. An external accountant is available, but has no authority to release payroll.

This is a discussion exercise using fictional account details—not an instruction to disable a live account or change real credentials.

Stage Action to rehearse Responsible person Approval or evidence needed
Report and assess Report the loss through a known contact route; determine whether compromise is suspected Manager and IT lead Record affected systems without recording secrets
Verify identity Follow the documented identity-verification process IT or the provider’s authorized recovery team Independent verification, not only a message from a replacement number
Authorize recovery Confirm the request and permitted scope Designated owner or alternate business approver Documented business approval plus provider-required checks
Restore access Use the provider-approved recovery process Provider or authorized administrator Completed verification and approval; no account borrowing
Maintain payroll continuity Use an already authorized alternate if recovery is delayed Alternate payroll preparer and approver Existing authority; the accountant does not inherit payment authority
Close the incident Review affected sessions and credentials; address the lost device IT with the provider as needed Confirm necessary revocation and replacement steps were completed

After the exercise, ask:

  • Could staff find the correct provider support route without the missing phone?
  • Was the alternate approver independently reachable?
  • Did recovery depend on another account that was also inaccessible?
  • Could payroll proceed without bypassing its normal approval process?
  • Did anyone propose sending a password or recovery code through ordinary chat?

Assign each gap to a named person. Where the provider controls the process, seek clarification before treating the plan as workable.

Preserve Access During Staff and Accountant Transitions

A staff departure should remove that person’s access without removing the practice’s ability to operate.

Before a planned transition, confirm that the successor has a separate account and has tested sign-in. Identify recovery addresses or devices tied to the departing person. Transfer appropriate administrative responsibilities through the provider’s approved process—not by transferring the former employee’s identity.

At departure, have IT and authorized administrators address account access, active sessions and enrolled authenticators as applicable. Confirm that the practice retains access to its financial records and that personal recovery details are no longer dependencies.

Use the same approach when changing accounting firms. Approve incoming users individually, confirm their financial scope and remove outgoing access at the agreed transition point. Do not leave a former adviser as the only administrator.

Keep transition documentation free of patient-identifiable details. Bank reconciliation and per-location financial reporting generally do not require copying patient-level records into access tickets or chat. Where financial work involves protected health information, obtain appropriate privacy and security guidance rather than assuming an authentication upgrade resolves those obligations.

Make Authentication Part of Finance Continuity

The practical deliverable is a reviewed inventory, tested recovery plan and clear approval responsibilities—not a claim that the practice now uses the newest technology.

FAQs

Should a medical practice replace every password with a passkey?

Not automatically. Review provider support, device ownership, accessibility, recovery and fallback options first. Pilot an appropriate method, test continuity and expand with IT approval. A passkey does not fix excessive permissions or unsafe payment approvals.

Can an owner and practice manager share one financial login?

Use separate named accounts wherever supported. Sharing makes individual access harder to withdraw and can make recovery depend on one person’s phone. If named access is unavailable, ask the provider about supported alternatives and document the limitation with IT rather than informally passing credentials around.

Who should approve a lost-device recovery request?

The practice should designate a business approver and an alternate before an incident. IT or the provider performs the applicable identity checks and recovery steps. Business approval alone should not bypass provider verification, and the requester should not be the sole approver of their own reset.

Does phishing-resistant MFA prevent payment fraud?

No. It helps protect authentication against credential phishing, but an authorized person can still be manipulated into making a payment or changing banking instructions. Keep independent payment verification and approval procedures separate from sign-in protection.
