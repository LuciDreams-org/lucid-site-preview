Post-Sale Financial Planning for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

The amount shown on a practice sale agreement is not the same as the money available for your personal use. After selling all or part of a private medical practice, an owner may still have funds in escrow, contingent earnouts, retained equity, tax obligations, and responsibilities connected to staff or retained entities.

The first post-close decision is therefore not how to invest the headline proceeds. It is how to distinguish cleared personal liquidity from money that remains restricted, uncertain, or committed to the practice.

A useful post-sale plan has three connected parts: a proceeds-and-obligations map, reconciled records for each affected entity, and a calendar assigning responsibility for the remaining work. Together, they support decisions about personal spending, continuing practice funding, and longer-term capital planning.

Start With What Actually Changed Hands

Build a closing file using the executed agreements, final settlement statement, bank confirmations, debt payoff statements, and supporting schedules. Your accountant should reconcile those documents to actual cash movements. Your attorney should clarify unresolved questions about contractual responsibilities.

Do not rely on a presentation summarizing the deal. Trace each component to its documented recipient and status:

  • Which funds went directly to you, and which went to a practice entity?
  • Which amounts were paid to lenders, advisers, or other parties at closing?
  • What remains in escrow or depends on a later calculation?
  • Which ownership interests did you retain?
  • What obligations continue after the sale?

A full sale can leave an old entity with collection or wind-down work. A partial sale can leave you responsible for funding retained operations. Neither situation should be treated as a clean separation until the records support it.

Build a Proceeds-and-Obligations Map

Use one master schedule, with a separate line for each payment, asset, reserve, or obligation. Record the legal owner, supporting document, expected timing, responsible adviser, and next review date. Keep uncertain amounts visibly separate from cleared cash.

Component How to treat it in the cash plan Records and follow-up
Cleared sale proceeds Confirm the recipient before treating funds as personal liquidity Settlement statement, bank receipt, entity ledger, and distribution records where applicable
Escrow or holdback Exclude from current available cash until released Escrow statement, release conditions, claims notices, and review date
Earnout Track as contingent future proceeds, not dependable spending money Contract calculation, reporting periods, supporting results, and payment status
Retained ownership Show separately from cash; document distribution and funding terms Ownership records, entity financial statements, and applicable agreements
Post-close price adjustment Track as an unresolved inflow or outflow Closing schedules, proposed calculation, supporting detail, and adviser review
Taxes Reserve using the tax adviser’s estimate, with assumptions documented Transaction documents, tax workpapers, payment confirmations, and estimate updates
Guarantees and debt Keep on the obligation register until status is documented Loan and lease records, payoff statements, and written releases where applicable
Continuing practice costs Reserve within the responsible entity’s forecast Payroll schedules, vendor balances, leases, benefit invoices, and transition costs

For guarantees, record more than the current loan balance. Identify who is obligated, what exposure remains, and what evidence would establish a release. Ask counsel to confirm the status rather than assuming the sale or a buyer’s payment removed your responsibility.

An illustrative liquidity calculation

The following is an illustrative example, not a transaction benchmark or tax estimate.

An owner’s closing documents show $2,000,000 of cash paid into a retained practice entity’s account, $250,000 in escrow, and a potential $300,000 earnout. The owner also retains an equity interest.

The entity has $150,000 of unpaid closing-related obligations and a $200,000 operating reserve requirement. Separately, the tax adviser estimates a $500,000 reserve for the owner’s taxes based on the available facts.

Illustrative calculation Amount
Cleared cash received by the entity $2,000,000
Less unpaid closing-related obligations ($150,000)
Less retained operating reserve ($200,000)
Preliminary amount for distribution review $1,650,000
Less owner-level tax reserve, if not already funded elsewhere ($500,000)
Preliminary personal planning amount $1,150,000

The $1,150,000 is not automatically distributable. The CPA and attorney still need to confirm the appropriate treatment, remaining obligations, and authority to transfer funds. Escrow, the potential earnout, and retained equity are excluded from current spending capacity. Reserves should also be checked for double counting between the entity and owner.

Keep Personal Liquidity Separate From Practice Capital

After a partial sale, the owner may have personal cash while a retained practice entity still needs funding. Conversely, a large entity bank balance may be committed to payroll, vendors, or transaction adjustments rather than available for personal use.

Maintain three distinct planning views:

  1. Personal cash flow: household spending, debt payments, adviser-estimated taxes, and expected compensation after closing.
  2. Retained-entity cash flow: provider and administrative payroll, benefits, rent, vendors, debt service, and shared costs.
  3. Contingent proceeds and exposures: escrow, earnouts, possible adjustments, guarantees, and potential funding requests.

The IRS guide to starting and operating a business supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliations. Those fundamentals remain useful during a sale handoff: every transfer should have a documented purpose and accounting treatment.

For a multi-location practice, review cash needs alongside location-level P&Ls. If one location was sold but another remains, determine how the remaining entity will cover costs previously shared across both locations. Administrative payroll, rent, and technology costs may not decline in proportion to revenue. Update the forecast using actual continuing commitments rather than the old allocation alone.

Reconcile the Operational Handoff

Payer receipts and patient balances

Cash received after closing may relate to services provided before closing. The receipt date alone does not establish which party is entitled to the money. Use the executed agreement and supporting records to determine the treatment.

Maintain a reconciliation of receipts collected by one party on behalf of another. Include refunds, payer recoupments, and patient credit balances when relevant. Assign responsibility for investigating differences and approving transfers.

Financial handoff reports should minimize patient information. Use aggregate or appropriately limited detail whenever it supports the reconciliation. If patient-identifiable information is necessary, involve the practice’s privacy lead and advisers in selecting the workflow. HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions; do not assume every financial handoff has identical requirements.

Payroll, vendors, and shared costs

Confirm who pays the final pre-close payroll and the first post-close payroll, along with related benefits, payroll liabilities, and any documented accrued compensation obligations. Reconcile the payroll reports to the ledger and bank activity.

For vendors and shared services, identify the responsible entity, service period, approval owner, and payment account. A bill arriving after closing may still belong to the seller’s entity, while an old automatic payment may cover a service now used by the buyer.

If retained entities continue operating, document intercompany charges and settlements so that neither entity’s cash position is overstated.

Use a Post-Close Calendar With Named Owners

The following is a suggested operating cadence, not a legal or tax deadline schedule. Replace each role with a named person and align dates with the executed agreements and adviser guidance.

At closing and immediately afterward Collect final documents; confirm receipts, payoffs, and account ownership Owner and transaction attorney Closing file and proceeds map
First reconciliation cycle Match settlement amounts to banks and ledgers; investigate differences Bookkeeper or controller, reviewed by CPA Reconciled cash schedule and exception log
First payroll and vendor cycles Confirm payment responsibilities and identify duplicate or missing payments Practice manager and payroll lead Reviewed payroll reconciliation and payable list

Assign one coordinator to maintain the calendar. Specialists can own individual tasks, but the owner needs a consolidated view of unresolved items and upcoming cash decisions.

Establish Tax Reserves Without Guessing the Tax Result

Do not apply a generic percentage to the sale price and assume the tax plan is complete. Ask your tax adviser for a written estimate based on the transaction documents, entity structure, ownership information, and relevant tax records.

The estimate should distinguish owner-level from entity-level amounts, identify assumptions, and flag items awaiting verification. It should also explain when the estimate needs updating as adjustments, escrow releases, or earnout payments become clearer.

Keep transaction payments separate from wages, consulting compensation, or other post-close income in your planning records. Have the adviser determine their treatment. Maintain a list of required returns, supporting documents, responsible preparers, and adviser-confirmed payment dates.

Turn Confirmed Liquidity Into a Personal Capital Plan

Once entity obligations and tax reserves are mapped, build a household forecast based on dependable resources. Include any continuing salary or consulting income, but distinguish it from business distributions and contingent proceeds.

Test what happens if an earnout pays nothing, escrow remains unavailable longer than expected, or retained operations need additional funding. Set the personal cash reserve around those exposures, household commitments, and the reliability of continuing income—not a universal number of months.

Before making a large purchase, gift, or new business commitment, ask whether it would consume money reserved for taxes or operating obligations. Separately, review beneficiaries, estate documents, insurance, and long-term goals with the appropriate advisers. Investment decisions belong in a coordinated personal plan, not in an assumption that every sale dollar is immediately available.

Maintain Records Until the Handoff Is Complete

Preserve access to historical ledgers, bank statements, payroll records, tax workpapers, transaction schedules, and reconciliation support before system access changes. Record who holds each file and who can answer later questions.

Ask the CPA and attorney to establish the applicable retention schedule. Do not treat account closure as permission to delete supporting records. IRS employment-tax recordkeeping guidance addresses specific payroll-related records, but it is not a universal destruction schedule for the entire transaction file.

FAQs

Can I treat all cash received at closing as personal money?

No. First confirm who received it and what obligations remain. Cash paid into a practice entity may still be needed for payroll, vendors, adjustments, or other commitments. Have the CPA and attorney review the appropriate treatment before transferring funds for personal use.

How should I budget for an earnout?

Track it separately from dependable cash. Document the calculation, reporting requirements, review dates, and payment status. Build essential household and retained-practice spending plans that do not require an uncertain earnout to arrive.

What if I sold the practice but kept the real estate or another entity?

Maintain a separate cash forecast and records for each retained entity. Identify its debt, rent receipts, expenses, guarantees, and funding commitments. Do not combine its bank balance with personal sale proceeds simply because you own both.

When is the financial handoff finished?

Not necessarily at closing. Use a documented completion review covering reconciled proceeds, outstanding adjustments, escrow, earnouts, guarantees, final operating liabilities, tax responsibilities, and record access. Some items may remain open long after day-to-day operations transfer.
