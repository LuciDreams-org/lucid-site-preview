Stock Option Planning for Medical Practices With Overseas Teams

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A US private medical practice considering stock options for overseas staff faces a more basic question than grant size: can the proposed entity legally and practically offer this ownership opportunity to these workers? Entity type, professional ownership restrictions, worker status and each relevant country’s laws may make options unsuitable or unavailable.

For a practice with an overseas administrative team, this is not simply an extension of the compensation budget. An option generally gives its holder a right to acquire an ownership interest under specified terms. The proposed issuer, underlying interest and eventual owner therefore matter as much as the retention goal.

Before promising grants, the owner should assemble a decision packet for licensed legal and tax advisers. That packet should explain who employs the workers, where they work, what entity would issue the award, what rights are proposed and what cash obligations could follow. Finance can organize the evidence and model affordability; it cannot substitute for jurisdiction-specific advice.

1. Establish whether options are available before designing them

Start with the practice’s legal structure, not a standard option-plan template. A professional entity, a management company and a separately owned service business are not interchangeable issuers.

Initial question Records to assemble Decision requiring adviser review
What would issue the options? Formation documents, governing agreements, ownership register and entity chart Whether that entity can issue the proposed instrument
Who could become an owner? Proposed recipients’ roles, professional-license status where relevant and current ownership restrictions Whether recipients could lawfully hold the underlying interest
Who engages each worker? Employment agreements, contractor agreements and any intermediary arrangements Whether the proposed issuer and worker relationship support the award
Where does each person work? Current work country, relevant work history and expected moves Which jurisdictions require review
Who must approve the arrangement? Existing owner agreements, financing documents and approval provisions What consents, amendments or other steps are needed

Do not assume that an entity’s tax classification establishes its legal ability to grant options. Likewise, describing an award as “nonvoting” does not establish that professional ownership restrictions no longer matter. Counsel should examine the actual rights and the ownership that could result from exercise.

If your practice also owns a management or service entity, ask advisers to evaluate that entity separately. It is not an automatic workaround. Its contracts, economics, ownership and relationship with the clinical practice all belong in the review.

The first decision is whether to investigate the proposal further—not whether to approve a grant. Keep recruiting discussions clear that any proposed arrangement remains subject to legal, tax and financial review.

2. Assemble an entity-and-worker intake packet

A useful intake packet lets advisers understand the facts without reconstructing the business from scattered emails. Assign one practice manager or finance lead to coordinate it, while keeping legal conclusions with counsel.

Map the entities and their financial relationships

Show each entity’s legal name, jurisdiction of formation, entity form, tax classification as confirmed by the CPA, current owners and relationship to the other businesses. Identify the entity that employs or contracts with each overseas worker and the entity proposed to issue awards.

Attach governing documents, existing ownership commitments, relevant service agreements and any restrictions in lending or owner agreements. Include entity-level financial statements and a description of shared administrative costs.

This distinction matters in a multi-location practice. The location benefiting from an overseas scheduling team may not be the entity paying that team, and neither may be the proposed issuer. An award tied to the wrong economic unit can be difficult for both the owner and recipient to understand.

Document worker facts without assuming classification

For each proposed recipient, record:

  • A worker identifier and role, such as scheduling supervisor or finance administrator.
  • The employing or contracting entity and any employer-of-record or agency arrangement.
  • Current work country and relevant prior or planned cross-border work.
  • Current contractual classification and the agreement supporting it.
  • Whether the person has already received an ownership-related promise.
  • The intended business purpose of the award.

Treat “employee” or “contractor” in your spreadsheet as the current recorded status, not a legal conclusion. Ask advisers whether that status has been properly evaluated and how it affects the proposed award. An intermediary arrangement should also be reviewed rather than treated as permission to grant equity.

Describe the proposal, not just a percentage

Provide the proposed issuer, underlying ownership interest, award size, exercise-price approach and vesting intentions. Explain whether the objective is continued service, a defined operational milestone or participation in long-term business value.

A percentage alone is ambiguous. Advisers need to know its denominator, the rights attached to the underlying interest and how future ownership changes might affect it. Mark unresolved terms as Pending adviser verification rather than presenting them as settled promises.

3. Build a country-by-country adviser intake matrix

Use a separate row for every relevant country, with additional rows where workers have materially different arrangements. Work location is a starting point; advisers should identify whether residence, prior work locations or other facts require additional jurisdictions to be included.

The following is an illustrative intake matrix. Canada and the United Kingdom are example locations only; replace them with the countries relevant to your team. No country-specific legal or tax treatment is assumed.

Jurisdiction Facts to supply Legal questions for local counsel Tax and payroll questions Review status
United States Issuer structure, owners, worker relationships, proposed award and financial records Can this entity issue the instrument, and can the proposed recipients hold the resulting interests? What approvals or restrictions apply? What treatment, valuation support, employer obligations and records apply to this proposal? Pending adviser verification
Canada, if relevant Worker location and history, engagement agreement, proposed issuer and terms Is the proposed offer and award permissible? What employment, securities or other local requirements need review? Which events require analysis, who has obligations and what cash or reporting preparation is needed? Pending adviser verification
United Kingdom, if relevant Worker location and history, engagement agreement, proposed issuer and terms Are local documents, approvals or changes to the proposed terms needed? What employer and recipient consequences require review, including any cross-border overlap? Pending adviser verification
Each additional relevant country, separately named Affected workers, relevant dates, entities, agreements and proposed terms What makes the proposal available, restricted or unsuitable here? What calculations, registrations, withholding, remittances or reporting may be required? Pending adviser verification

Add the responsible adviser, written response date, remaining questions and implementation owner to your working version. Do not mark a country “approved” merely because a document has been sent for review.

Ask advisers to distinguish three outcomes: unavailable under the proposed facts, potentially workable with specified changes, or ready for documented approval. That makes the matrix a decision tool rather than a list of unanswered questions.

4. Compare actual equity with cash-based alternatives

Options should be considered alongside alternatives, not assumed to be the default. Ask advisers to compare the proposed option with direct equity and cash-based arrangements, such as a service-based retention award or a contractually defined long-term cash incentive. This comparison does not establish that any alternative is appropriate or permitted.

Decision area Questions about options or actual equity Questions about a cash-based alternative
Ownership Could the recipient become an owner, and with what rights? Does the agreement avoid ownership rights as intended, and what other restrictions still apply?
Economic value What interest is being valued, and can the recipient realistically realize value? How is payment determined, and can the recipient understand the formula?
Cash demands Could employer taxes, administration, repurchase terms or other obligations require practice cash? When could payment become due, and can the practice fund it?
Departure What happens to unvested rights, vested rights and any acquired interest? What payment, forfeiture or dispute provisions need review?
Cross-border administration What local approvals, documents and employer processes are needed? What local employment, tax and payroll processes are needed?

A cash arrangement is not automatically simpler, and an option is not automatically cash-free. Compare total obligations, recipient understanding and administrative capacity before selecting a structure.

For a closely held practice, ask specifically about liquidity. A recipient should not be led to expect a ready market, a guaranteed buyback or a particular future sale unless the approved documents actually establish the relevant rights and obligations.

5. Prepare valuation inputs and test cash exposure

Give valuation advisers practice-specific financial evidence

Ask the relevant advisers what valuation work the proposal requires, who should perform it and when it must be refreshed. Do not select a method or exercise price merely because another business used it.

Useful inputs may include:

  • Reconciled entity-level financial statements and recent management accounts.
  • Existing ownership interests, outstanding rights and governing agreements.
  • Debt, cash balances, owner transactions and documented shared-cost allocations.
  • Payer receipts, patient-balance collection assumptions and collection trends, where relevant to forecasts.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, staffing changes and expected operating costs.
  • Location-level results where they help explain the issuer’s economics.
  • Forecast assumptions, significant contracts and known business changes.

Separate actual results from projections and explain unusual items. A strong location’s results should not silently become the valuation basis for an award in a different entity.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those are useful foundations for the financial packet; they do not establish an option’s value or tax treatment.

Model obligations without counting on exercise proceeds

Build a cash forecast that separates ordinary operating needs from award-related costs. Include adviser and valuation work, administration, and any employer tax, payroll or contractual payment obligations identified by advisers. Ask who must fund each obligation, in which currency and under what circumstances.

Test the forecast against slower payer collections, higher administrative payroll and a possible award-related payment occurring in the same period. Do not use uncertain employee exercise proceeds to cover routine provider payroll.

For a multi-entity practice, show both where the cost belongs and which entity would pay it. Any proposed intercompany funding or shared-cost allocation should be reviewed rather than used to conceal a shortfall in the issuing entity.

6. Create a documented decision and review process

Before making a binding offer or issuing a grant, obtain the required professional review and approvals. The decision file should identify the approved issuer, recipients, terms, valuation support, country conclusions and responsibility for implementation.

Assign clear owners:

  • Practice owner: business objective, affordability decision and required owner approvals.
  • Licensed counsel: legal availability, ownership restrictions, documents and required approvals.
  • Tax advisers: applicable treatment and employer and recipient tax questions across jurisdictions.
  • Payroll team: ability to execute the adviser-confirmed obligations.
  • Finance lead: reliable financial inputs, cash planning and reconciliation of approved records.

Build a review trigger for relocations, changes in worker status, termination, entity restructuring and material changes to the proposed arrangement. Before acting on an exercise or payment request, confirm whether changed facts require renewed advice.

Keep the financial analysis free of patient-identifiable information wherever possible. Aggregate collections, payroll and entity results are generally the relevant inputs for this decision; patient-level detail is not a substitute for better financial records.

Conclusion: Decide feasibility before promising ownership

For a medical practice with overseas staff, stock option planning begins with eligibility and structure—not a grant formula. Establish what the entity can offer, who could hold the resulting interest and which countries need review. Then compare alternatives, assemble reliable valuation inputs and test potential cash obligations.

The useful outcome is a documented decision: proceed on approved terms, revise the proposal or choose not to offer options. Each can be a sound result when it follows informed review rather than an early promise.

FAQs

Can a medical practice offer stock options to overseas administrative staff?

It depends on the proposed issuer, its governing documents, professional ownership restrictions, the workers’ relationships and the relevant countries’ laws. Overseas staff should not be promised options until licensed advisers have evaluated whether the instrument and resulting ownership are available under the actual facts.

Can we issue options through a separate management company instead?

That is a separate proposal requiring review, not an automatic solution. Provide the management company’s ownership records, contracts, financial statements and relationship with the clinical practice. Advisers must assess that entity and the proposed recipients on their own facts.

What happens if a recipient moves countries?

Notify the legal, tax and payroll advisers before a planned move where possible. Update work-location history and ask whether the move changes the award’s treatment or employer obligations. Do not assume that the original country review covers the new circumstances.

What should we do if options were already mentioned in an offer?

Give counsel the exact offer language, related correspondence and any signed agreement. Avoid making new assurances or informally changing the promise. Ask counsel to determine what has been committed and how to communicate the next steps accurately.
