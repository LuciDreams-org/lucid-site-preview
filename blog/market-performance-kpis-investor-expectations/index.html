Market Risk KPIs for Medical Practice Cash Reserves

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can report a healthy reserve balance and still struggle to fund payroll on time. Some holdings may not mature before the next payroll run. Others may require a sale, settlement period or transfer approval before the money reaches the operating account. Their market value may also fall when the practice needs cash.

For owners of staffed private practices, the central question is not simply, “What did our reserves earn?” It is, “Will the right entity have enough available cash when its obligations come due, without an unplanned sale or unacceptable loss?”

Market-performance reporting remains useful, but it belongs alongside liquidity and risk measures. Your finance team should map obligations and reconcile balances; your licensed investment adviser should help evaluate holdings, market exposure and appropriate comparison criteria. Investment decisions require advice specific to the practice’s circumstances, not a generic return target.

Start With the Purpose of Each Reserve

Before comparing returns, identify what each reserve must fund. Provider and administrative payroll, tax payments, equipment purchases and expansion costs have different timing and flexibility.

Use three planning categories:

  • Operating liquidity: Cash needed for payroll, rent, supplies and other near-term obligations, including a buffer for receipt delays.
  • Scheduled reserves: Amounts designated for known tax payments, equipment deposits or other dated commitments.
  • Longer-horizon reserves: Funds not currently assigned to near-term spending, subject to owner-approved policies and periodic reassessment.

These are planning categories, not recommendations for particular securities or account types. A reserve’s purpose should determine the questions asked of the adviser—not the other way around.

Keep entity ownership visible. Money held by one practice entity should not automatically be treated as available to another. Confirm transfer authority and any accounting, financing or legal constraints before including it in another entity’s liquidity calculation.

Five Reserve Risk KPIs to Review

1. Liquidity coverage by obligation date

A single “months of cash” measure can conceal timing problems. Calculate coverage for specific dates or cumulative planning windows.

Reserve liquidity coverage = eligible reserve funds available by the deadline ÷ planned obligations assigned to those reserves through that deadline.

Define “eligible” carefully. Exclude restricted amounts, money already allocated elsewhere and holdings that cannot reliably become spendable cash by the required date. For holdings requiring sale, use an adviser-reviewed stressed value and realistic settlement and transfer assumptions rather than assuming today’s quoted value will be available immediately.

Keep expected operating receipts separate from this reserve-only ratio. In a companion cash forecast, show beginning operating cash, expected payer receipts and patient collections, obligations, and planned reserve transfers. Do not count the same reserve balance as both beginning cash and a future transfer.

Coverage of 1.0× means the modeled funds exactly match the modeled obligations. It does not establish an adequate buffer for uncertainty.

2. Maturity concentration and funding gaps

A maturity schedule shows when holdings are contractually due to return principal, subject to their terms and risks. Group amounts by relevant funding windows and calculate each window’s share of scheduled maturities.

Maturity concentration = principal scheduled to mature in a selected window ÷ total principal in holdings with contractual maturities.

Report holdings without contractual maturities separately. Do not invent a maturity date for an investment simply because the practice expects to sell it.

Concentration is not automatically undesirable. A large maturity shortly before a planned equipment payment may be intentional. The warning sign is a mismatch: obligations arrive first, while the reserve becomes available later.

Also ask what happens at maturity. Automatic renewal or reinvestment instructions can make an apparently well-timed maturity less useful unless someone is responsible for directing the proceeds.

3. Access restrictions and time to usable cash

Track both the amount subject to restrictions and the elapsed time needed to move proceeds into the paying account.

Restricted reserve share = reserve value subject to identified access restrictions ÷ total reserve value.

Potential restrictions include pledges, withdrawal conditions, account authorizations and contractual limits. Separately record redemption terms, settlement timing, transfer cutoffs and possible early-exit charges.

An account can be accessible in principle but too slow for a particular payroll deadline. Record the date funds must be in the operating account—not only the date a payment leaves it. Confirm who can authorize transactions and who serves as backup when that person is unavailable.

4. Price sensitivity under adviser-defined scenarios

Market value and contractual maturity value are not interchangeable. A holding intended to fund a later obligation may fluctuate before that date. If the practice needs to sell early, that fluctuation becomes operationally important.

Ask the licensed investment adviser to estimate how relevant scenarios could affect reserve values. Depending on the holdings, scenarios may involve interest-rate changes, credit deterioration or broader market declines.

Scenario loss percentage = estimated decline under the defined scenario ÷ current market value.

Document the scenario, valuation date and assumptions. Treat the result as a sensitivity estimate, not a forecast or a maximum possible loss. Where duration is relevant, the adviser can explain its usefulness and limitations.

Then translate the estimate into a practice decision: would the stressed proceeds still cover the assigned obligation, or would the practice need to defer discretionary spending or identify another funding source?

5. Counterparty and issuer exposure

Track exposure by legal issuer, deposit institution and custodian where relevant. These describe different relationships and should not be collapsed into one label.

Exposure share = reserve value associated with the specified institution or issuer ÷ total reserve value.

Several holdings or accounts may still share the same underlying issuer or institution. Conversely, a custodian holding assets is not necessarily the issuer of those assets.

Have the adviser identify the relevant risks and confirm any applicable protections, eligibility conditions, limits and exclusions. Do not assume every account described as “cash” has identical protections, or that protection against one risk prevents market losses or access delays.

Build a Treasury KPI Sheet Around Payment Dates

Use one controlled worksheet that connects the obligation calendar to the reserve inventory. Maintain an as-of date and reconcile holdings to statements before making decisions. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records; it does not establish an investment policy.

Compare Market Returns Without Confusing Them With Practice Performance

Reserve earnings do not demonstrate that the medical practice’s operations improved. A favorable market period can increase account values while payer receipts slow or staffing costs rise.

Keep operating reporting separate from treasury reporting:

  • Operating view: Practice revenue, expenses, operating results and cash collections, with location-level reporting where useful.
  • Treasury view: Interest and distributions, fees, realized gains or losses, unrealized changes and external cash transfers.
  • Combined cash view: How operations, capital spending, owner transactions and reserve movements affect available cash.

Have the CPA determine the appropriate accounting presentation. Do not describe a transfer from reserves as new practice revenue, or a deposit into an investment account as investment performance.

Before comparing results, obtain adviser approval of the benchmark and methodology. Record:

  1. The reserve’s purpose and expected spending horizon.
  2. Comparable liquidity, credit exposure and price sensitivity.
  3. Whether results include income, fees and realized and unrealized changes.
  4. How contributions and withdrawals affect the return calculation.
  5. Whether the comparison reflects applicable costs and tax treatment, with CPA input.

A broad stock-market index may be a poor reference for reserves assigned to next month’s payroll. A higher headline return is not automatically a better outcome if it introduces risks inconsistent with the reserve’s job.

Use a Repeatable Owner Review

Refresh the obligation calendar as payment dates, payroll needs and capital commitments change. Review reserve reporting regularly and again before a material withdrawal, new commitment or change in holdings.

The finance team should reconcile statements, update expected payer and patient receipts, and identify funding gaps. Use aggregate financial information; patient-identifiable detail is not needed for a treasury KPI sheet.

The owner should approve spending priorities and resolve tradeoffs. For shared costs, document which entity owes the payment and how allocations or reimbursements enter the cash forecast. Do not let a consolidated balance conceal an entity-level shortage.

The licensed investment adviser should review market exposures, comparison criteria and any proposed changes to holdings. The CPA should address accounting and tax questions. Document decisions, responsible people and follow-up dates rather than relying on a dashboard alone.

Why Preservation and Availability Can Matter More Than Headline Returns

Owners and capital partners may reasonably prioritize reserves that support continuity over those with the highest recent return. Payroll, tax payments and committed capital spending do not necessarily wait for markets to recover.

A useful reserve report therefore explains what the money must do, when it must be available, what could reduce its value and who is responsible for acting. Evaluate market performance within those constraints—not as a separate contest to maximize returns.

FAQs

How much should a medical practice keep in reserves?

There is no universal amount established by these KPIs. Build the target from planned obligations, receipt variability, payroll commitments, financing constraints and capital plans. Review both total reserves and the portion available to each entity by each funding date.

Can a reserve holding be liquid but still expose the practice to losses?

Yes. A holding may be readily saleable while its price fluctuates. Liquidity measures the ability to obtain cash; price sensitivity measures potential changes in value. Review both, including settlement and transfer timing, before assigning it to a dated obligation.

Should reserve returns be included in location performance comparisons?

Show operating performance separately so reserve earnings do not obscure differences in revenue and expenses. If treasury balances or earnings are allocated across locations, document the allocation method and apply it consistently. Ask the CPA how those items should appear in the financial statements.

What should owners ask their investment adviser first?

Start with the obligation calendar: “Can these holdings provide usable cash by these dates, including under a stressed scenario?” Then review access conditions, maturity mismatches, issuer and institutional exposure, costs and suitable performance comparisons. Ask the adviser to explain assumptions and limitations in writing.
