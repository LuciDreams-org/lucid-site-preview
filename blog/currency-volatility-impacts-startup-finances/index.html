5 Ways Currency Volatility Affects Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Most US private medical practices collect payer receipts and patient balances in dollars, pay staff in dollars, and buy from domestic vendors. For these practices, currency volatility may be a minor planning issue—not something that requires a complex treasury program.

But exchange-rate changes can still reach operating cash through foreign supplier invoices, imported equipment, overseas contractors, foreign-currency balances, and domestic vendor price changes. The owner's task is to identify which exposures actually exist, when they could affect cash, and whether they could disrupt payroll or a planned capital purchase.

An imported product does not automatically create a foreign-currency payable. If a supplier accepts a fixed dollar price with no currency-adjustment clause, the practice generally has a dollar obligation. The supplier may bear exchange-rate risk on that order, while the practice faces possible repricing on future purchases.

Start with the contract and payment terms—not the manufacturer's country or a currency headline.

1. Foreign-Currency Supplier Invoices Can Change the Dollars Needed to Pay

Direct exposure arises when a practice owes a fixed amount in a foreign currency. The invoice amount can stay unchanged while the dollars needed to settle it rise or fall.

For example, a practice might buy specialty supplies directly from an overseas supplier that requires payment in euros. If each euro costs more dollars by the payment date, that invoice consumes more operating cash than originally budgeted. If each euro costs fewer dollars, the dollar cost falls, before considering fees.

Check three documents together:

  • The purchase agreement or accepted quote
  • The invoice, including its payment currency
  • The bank or payment provider's conversion terms

A vendor's address does not establish the exposure. Neither does an invoice displaying an informational dollar equivalent if payment must ultimately be made in another currency.

What the practice should do

Record the original currency amount, due date, budget exchange rate, and expected dollar payment. Ask whether the supplier can offer a firm dollar quote, and compare its total cost with the foreign-currency option.

Include conversion spreads and transfer charges. A quoted exchange rate alone does not show the full payment cost. If a provider's rate already includes a spread, do not add that spread a second time.

For cash planning, place the payment in the same forecast as payer deposits, patient collections, provider and administrative payroll, rent, and other commitments. The useful question is whether an adverse exchange move would consume cash already needed for those obligations.

2. Imported Equipment Quotes Can Put Capital Plans Over Budget

Equipment exposure often starts before there is a payable. A practice may be evaluating an imported device whose quote expires before financing, site preparation, or owner approval is complete.

There are several distinct situations:

Quote structure Currency exposure for the practice Planning action
Fixed dollar price through an agreed payment date No direct foreign-currency payable under those terms Confirm exclusions and conditions that could change the price
Fixed foreign-currency price Dollar cost changes with the exchange rate until the required currency is obtained Test dollar costs for each payment milestone
Dollar estimate subject to currency adjustment Dollar price may change under the contract Identify the reference rate, adjustment date, and calculation
Expired or nonbinding quote Future purchase price remains uncertain Obtain a refreshed quote before committing the capital budget

Separate equipment price changes from exchange movement. A revised quote may reflect a different configuration, freight, installation, supplier pricing, or currency—not just one cause.

Deposits also matter. If part of the price has already been paid, test the unpaid foreign-currency amount rather than applying a new exchange rate to the entire historical purchase cost. Record when the remaining balance becomes due, especially if delivery and installation dates are uncertain.

Illustrative equipment-purchase sensitivity worksheet

The following is a hypothetical example, not a rate forecast or vendor quote. Assume no deposit has been paid. Rates are expressed as US dollars per euro, so a higher rate means the euro purchase costs more dollars.

Worksheet item Original plan Exchange movement only Exchange movement plus supplier repricing
Equipment price in euros €80,000 €80,000 €82,000
Exchange rate: USD per EUR $1.100 $1.155 $1.155
Equipment cost in dollars $88,000 $92,400 $94,710
Separately quoted conversion and transfer charges $400 $400 $600
Total dollar payment $88,400 $92,800 $95,310
Increase from original plan — $4,400 $6,910

The combined increase breaks down as follows:

  • Exchange movement: €80,000 × ($1.155 − $1.100) = $4,400.
  • Supplier price increase: (€82,000 − €80,000) × $1.155 = $2,310.
  • Additional charges: $600 − $400 = $200.
  • Total increase: $4,400 + $2,310 + $200 = $6,910.

This worksheet uses the original equipment amount to isolate the exchange effect and the revised rate to measure supplier repricing. It keeps fees separate so the owner can see what changed.

It is not a complete project budget. Add installation, freight, training, site work, financing costs, and other applicable costs separately. Then decide whether the revised project still fits the practice's capital allocation and cash reserve.

3. Overseas Contractor Payments Can Make Recurring Costs Less Predictable

A practice may engage an overseas contractor for administrative, design, or technical work. The financial exposure depends on what the agreement requires—not simply where the contractor lives.

If the contract fixes payment in dollars, the practice generally does not have a foreign-currency payable. The contractor may later request a higher dollar rate, but that is a contract-pricing decision rather than an automatic exchange adjustment.

If the contract fixes payment in a foreign currency, the dollar cost can change each payment period. The effect may become more noticeable when a recurring commitment continues for months.

Before approving or renewing the agreement, confirm:

  • The contractual payment currency
  • Whether the amount is fixed or adjustable
  • Who bears transfer charges and intermediary-bank deductions
  • Whether the contractor must receive a specified net amount
  • The payment schedule and renewal terms

For a staffed practice, compare the revised contractor forecast with the rest of the administrative budget. Do not assume a favorable exchange move will fund a permanent staffing commitment.

Currency analysis also does not settle worker classification, contracting, privacy, or tax obligations. Raise those questions separately with the appropriate advisers. Financial exposure tracking should not require patient-identifiable information.

4. Foreign-Currency Cash Balances Can Change Available Dollar Liquidity

A practice might hold foreign currency after a canceled order, a supplier refund, or an advance conversion for a planned purchase. The number of foreign-currency units can remain unchanged while their dollar value moves.

That distinction matters when evaluating funds available for US payroll and other dollar expenses. A foreign-currency account balance is not the same as a fixed amount of spendable dollars.

Illustrative example: A €20,000 balance has a dollar equivalent of $22,000 at $1.10 per euro and $21,000 at $1.05 per euro, before conversion charges. The account still contains €20,000, but it would provide fewer dollars at the second rate.

Review why the balance exists and what it is intended to pay. If it is reserved for a euro invoice, compare it with that obligation rather than treating the entire balance as surplus dollar cash. If the purchase has been canceled, ask whether maintaining the balance still serves a business purpose.

For multi-entity practices, identify which entity owns the account and which entity owes the related bill. Do not assume one entity's funds are freely available for another entity's payroll or equipment purchase. Have the finance team document any proposed transfer and obtain appropriate advice.

5. Domestic Vendor Repricing Can Carry Indirect Currency Effects

A domestic distributor may import equipment or supplies and invoice the practice entirely in dollars. Currency changes can affect the distributor's costs and may influence later prices.

This is indirect exposure, not necessarily a foreign-currency liability for the practice.

A vendor price increase may also reflect shipping, labor, product changes, demand, or other costs. Do not attribute the whole increase to currency without supporting information. Exchange-rate changes may reach the practice with a delay, only partly, or not at all under a fixed-price agreement.

When a vendor announces an increase, ask:

  • Does it affect existing orders or only new purchases?
  • Is there a firm dollar price for a defined period?
  • Does the contract allow a currency surcharge?
  • Can the vendor distinguish currency adjustments from other price changes?
  • Are alternative quantities or delivery schedules available without unnecessary inventory purchases?

Update the expense forecast using the price the practice actually expects to pay. For multiple locations, apply the revised unit cost to each site's expected usage. That helps owners distinguish a vendor-price problem from increased purchasing volume or a change in service mix.

Do not assume higher supply costs can immediately be offset through payer receipts or patient charges. Evaluate the expense within the practice's actual reimbursement arrangements and operating plan.

Build a Five-Part Exposure Register

A useful register can be a straightforward finance worksheet. If a category does not apply, record “No current exposure” and the review date rather than creating unnecessary monitoring work.

Exposure category Evidence to review Register fields Suggested review owner
Foreign-currency supplier invoices Accepted orders, invoices, payment terms Currency, unpaid amount, due date, budget rate, fees, paying entity Accounts payable lead
Imported equipment quotes Quote validity, adjustment clauses, deposit schedule Quote currency, committed versus proposed status, unpaid milestones, project cash limit Practice manager and owner
Overseas contractors Agreements, renewal terms, payment instructions Payment currency, recurring amount, adjustment terms, transfer costs Administrative manager
Foreign-currency cash balances Bank statements and related purchase commitments Currency balance, owning entity, intended use, expected conversion date Finance lead
Domestic vendor repricing Renewal notices, price lists, surcharge provisions Dollar price change, effective date, affected locations, documented explanation Purchasing lead

Assign one person to maintain the register and one person with authority to approve material changes. Keep proposed purchases separate from signed obligations to avoid counting both a quote and its eventual invoice as two exposures.

Turn the Register Into Cash Decisions

The register is useful only if it changes the forecast or an approval decision.

Connect exposure to payment dates

Place expected payments in the operating cash forecast by due date. Use documented payment quotes when available and clearly labeled planning assumptions otherwise. Review near-term items before payment and refresh longer-term assumptions during the regular forecast cycle.

Choose scenarios around the actual decision

Use a base case and reasonable alternative rates to test a purchase or recurring commitment. These are planning assumptions, not predictions. Set an escalation point based on the practice's cash position and purchase-approval policy rather than adopting an arbitrary universal percentage.

The resulting decision might be to request a firm dollar quote, refresh financing, revise a purchase schedule, or retain more cash before committing. Paying early is not automatically better: it can reduce rate uncertainty while consuming liquidity sooner and changing supplier risk.

Keep records that explain the variance

Retain the accepted quote, invoice, payment confirmation, conversion details, and fee information. Reconcile payments to bank activity. IRS Publication 583 explains business recordkeeping, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation.

Ask the CPA how foreign-currency transactions should appear in the books and what documentation is needed. Keep accounting and tax conclusions separate from the cash-planning worksheet.

Ask advisers focused questions

Useful questions include:

  • Which obligations are genuinely payable in foreign currency?
  • What dollar amount would leave the bank under the proposed payment method?
  • Which costs are exchange effects, fees, or supplier price changes?
  • Does the revised purchase threaten the operating cash reserve?
  • Which entity should pay, and how should shared costs be documented?
  • What accounting, tax, or contract questions need separate review?

Conclusion

Many medical practices have little direct currency exposure. Confirm that first. Where exposure exists, identify the contractual currency, unpaid amount, payment date, fees, and responsible entity.

Then separate exchange movement from vendor repricing and other costs. A focused exposure register and purchase sensitivity worksheet can show whether volatility is merely a budget variance or a reason to reconsider a commitment before it competes with payroll and other essential payments.

FAQs

Does buying imported equipment create currency risk for a medical practice?

Not necessarily. A fixed dollar contract without a currency-adjustment clause generally creates a dollar obligation. Exposure may instead arise when the quote expires, the vendor reprices a future order, or the contract requires payment in foreign currency.

Should every practice maintain a currency forecast?

No. Review supplier terms, contractor agreements, equipment quotes, and cash accounts first. If everything is fixed in dollars and no relevant adjustment clauses exist, ordinary vendor-price monitoring may be enough. Record that conclusion and revisit it when arrangements change.

How should an owner evaluate a currency-related equipment overrun?

Separate the exchange effect, supplier price changes, and payment charges. Add other project costs, update the payment schedule, and compare the total with available cash and financing. The sensitivity worksheet should support the purchase decision, not predict exchange rates.

Can a practice assume a stronger dollar will reduce domestic vendor prices?

No. Domestic vendors may have fixed costs, existing inventory, contractual pricing, or other reasons not to change prices. Budget from actual quotes and agreed terms rather than assuming exchange movements will pass through immediately or fully.
