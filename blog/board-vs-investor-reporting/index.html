Board vs. Investor Reporting for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice’s governing group and its outside capital providers may review the same financial results, but they use those results for different purposes. Governance reporting supports decisions and oversight. Investor reporting explains performance, capital use, risks, and progress against commitments.

For a staffed private practice, the distinction becomes important when owners consider opening another location, hiring providers, borrowing money, or changing distributions. The governing group needs enough detail to decide what the practice should do. External recipients need information appropriate to their role and reporting rights—not necessarily every internal discussion.

The solution is not two sets of books. It is one reconciled financial foundation, with reporting tailored to each audience’s responsibilities. Different summaries are reasonable; conflicting revenue, cash, or forecast figures are not.

Board and Investor Reporting: Side-by-Side

Here, “board reporting” includes reporting to an equivalent owner-governance group. “Investor reporting” means communication to external equity capital providers. Lenders are a separate audience whose reporting requirements should be checked against their loan agreements.

Reporting dimension Board or owner-governance packet External investor update
Purpose Evaluate alternatives, oversee performance, authorize action, and assign accountability Explain results, capital use, significant developments, and progress against the investment plan
Central question What decision should we make, and under what conditions? How is the practice performing, and what has changed?
Cadence Regular oversight meetings, plus decision meetings when needed The schedule required by agreements and any additional agreed updates
Financial detail Practice-wide statements, relevant location/entity results, variances, liquidity, and supporting analysis Consistent summary financials and required schedules, with explanations of significant changes
Forward-looking assumptions Assumptions, alternatives, downside cases, cash needs, and decision triggers The approved forecast or outlook, its key assumptions, uncertainties, and changes from prior updates
Confidentiality Role-based access to decision-relevant operational and financial detail Information appropriate to the recipient’s rights and purpose; avoid unnecessary patient and personnel detail
Preparation and approval Finance prepares and reconciles; management validates operating assumptions; authorized decision-makers approve actions Finance checks consistency; designated leadership approves release; advisers review disclosure questions as needed
Result A recorded decision, conditions, responsible person, and follow-up date A clear account of performance, risks, milestones, and any requested action

Neither audience has a universal page count or reporting frequency. A practice’s governing documents, financing agreements, operating complexity, and upcoming decisions should shape the schedule.

Practices Without Formal Boards Still Need Governance Reporting

A physician-owned practice may have no formal board. The equivalent forum could be a meeting of the managing owners, an ownership committee, or a sole owner reviewing decisions with the practice administrator and finance adviser.

The important distinction is between operating updates and owner decisions. An administrator may explain payroll trends, but that does not necessarily mean the administrator can approve a new lease or capital commitment. Confirm authority through the practice’s governing documents and advisers rather than inferring it from job titles.

Give the owner-governance meeting a defined agenda:

  • Review financial results and cash commitments.
  • Identify decisions that exceed delegated management authority.
  • Compare alternatives and financial risks.
  • Record what was approved, deferred, or rejected.
  • Assign responsibility for follow-up.

If the practice has no outside investors, it does not need to manufacture an investor update. It may still need separate reporting for a lender or another authorized recipient. Those communications should draw from the same financial records while addressing the recipient’s specific requirements.

Establish One Reconciled Financial Foundation

Audience differences should change emphasis, not underlying facts. Before drafting either report, agree on the reporting period, accounting basis, entities included, and version of the forecast.

Close the books before interpreting the results

Reconcile bank and credit accounts, review payroll postings, and resolve significant classification issues. If the books are incomplete, label the report preliminary and identify the unresolved items rather than presenting estimates as final results.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those practices provide a foundation for reporting; they do not determine which disclosures an investor is entitled to receive.

Keep revenue, receipts, and receivables distinct

A practice can show revenue growth while cash remains tight. Payer receipts may arrive after the activity that generated them, and patient balances may remain outstanding. Reports should distinguish:

  • Revenue: Reported under the practice’s stated accounting basis.
  • Cash receipts: Money actually collected during the period.
  • Accounts receivable: Outstanding balances tracked through the relevant receivables records, with valuation and reconciliation appropriate to the accounting basis.

Do not call billed charges “revenue” in one report and compare them with collections labeled “revenue” in another. Define each measure and explain how operational reports connect to the financial statements.

Preserve location and entity boundaries

A location P&L helps assess operating performance. An entity-level balance sheet helps identify where cash, debt, and obligations sit. These views answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

For shared administrative payroll, software, or occupancy costs, document the allocation method and apply it consistently. If an allocation changes, explain its effect on location comparisons. Where combined results include multiple entities, explain the treatment of intercompany balances and transactions.

A short reporting glossary should state each metric’s definition, source, period, accounting basis, and responsible reviewer. Both reporting audiences should use that glossary.

One Expansion Proposal, Two Reporting Views

Illustrative example: Every amount and timing assumption below is hypothetical, not a benchmark or a description of a Lucid Financials client.

A staffed private practice is considering a second location. Management estimates an initial cash requirement of $300,000: $180,000 for build-out and equipment, plus a $120,000 operating cash reserve. The practice has $500,000 in cash before the proposal, and management is evaluating funding the project from existing cash.

The base case assumes an opening in six months and a subsequent ramp-up in collections. A downside case assumes a later opening and slower payer receipts. Neither case treats scheduled visits or billed charges as immediate cash collections.

The internal decision packet

The owner-governance packet should make the trade-offs visible. Its purpose is not simply to show that the new location could become profitable; it is to determine whether the practice can support the expansion without undermining existing operations.

Internal packet component What decision-makers need to examine
Decision requested Whether to authorize the expansion, the spending limit, and any conditions before commitments are made
Initial cash requirement The $180,000 project outlay, supporting estimates, payment timing, and what is excluded
Operating reserve How the $120,000 reserve relates to provider/admin payroll, rent, supplies, and collection timing
Existing-practice liquidity Obligations already attached to the $500,000 cash balance and the cash needed to sustain current operations
Location economics New-location results before and after shared-cost allocations, with effects on the existing location shown separately
Downside case Cash consequences of delayed opening, staffing costs before receipts, and slower collections
Alternatives Proceed, phase the project, delay, reduce scope, or investigate financing
Decision conditions Required reviews, unresolved assumptions, spending authority, and circumstances requiring another owner decision

A key clarification is that the $120,000 reserve is not necessarily an immediate expense. It is cash designated to support the ramp-up. Likewise, subtracting the entire $300,000 requirement from $500,000 leaves $200,000 outside the proposed project allocation—not automatically $200,000 available for distributions. Existing payroll, payables, debt service, and other commitments still matter.

The packet should end with a decision request. For example: authorize further planning but defer a lease commitment until cash scenarios, staffing assumptions, and adviser reviews are complete. The governing group should record the actual decision and its conditions.

The external investor update

The investor update uses the same proposal, figures, and forecast version, but emphasizes status and capital implications. It should not describe an unapproved expansion as a committed project.

An illustrative summary might read:

Management is evaluating a second location with an estimated $300,000 initial cash requirement, comprising $180,000 in build-out and equipment and a $120,000 operating reserve. The current base case assumes an opening in six months. The proposal remains subject to owner approval. Key uncertainties include opening timing, staffing commitments, and the pace of payer receipts. The next update will distinguish approved commitments from remaining estimates.

Required financial statements and schedules should accompany that narrative as applicable. If investors require detailed forecasts or additional information, brevity does not override those requirements.

Shared fact Internal decision emphasis Investor communication emphasis
$300,000 estimated requirement Test components, alternatives, and liquidity impact Explain expected capital use and approval status
$500,000 current cash Examine obligations and cash availability by entity Report cash with relevant restrictions or commitments explained
Six-month base-case opening assumption Assess dependencies and consequences of delay Identify the assumption as conditional, not guaranteed
Slower receipts in the downside case Determine whether to delay, phase, or seek funding Explain the risk and any resulting change to the outlook

Set Audience Boundaries Without Hiding Important Facts

A shorter investor update is not permission to omit information that must be disclosed. Conversely, access to a financial summary does not automatically justify receiving individual compensation records, patient-level information, or every internal negotiation detail.

Use recipient-specific access and an approved distribution list. Keep financial analysis aggregated where practical. The HHS minimum necessary guidance describes requirements and exceptions for covered situations; it should not be reduced to a universal rule for every report. Have the appropriate advisers assess patient-information handling and any business-associate responsibilities.

Keep patient-identifiable data out of ordinary Slack messages and general-purpose AI tools. Reporting questions can usually be discussed using aggregate balances and de-identified explanations.

Ask legal and financial advisers to resolve questions about ownership eligibility, information rights, financing terms, disclosure duties, and approval authority. A reporting matrix is an operating tool, not a substitute for those determinations.

Use a Controlled Review and Release Process

A practical reporting cycle separates financial verification from decision approval:

  1. Finance prepares the common financial package. Reconcile actual results and identify preliminary items.
  2. Management validates assumptions. Confirm staffing plans, project timing, collection expectations, and operational dependencies.
  3. Authorized owners review decisions. Document approvals, conditions, and outstanding questions.
  4. Leadership approves external communication. Check that statements match the decision record and reporting obligations.
  5. Finance checks consistency before release. Match reporting periods, figures, definitions, and forecast versions across both reports.

When an error is found after distribution, assess who received it and issue a clear correction where appropriate. Do not silently change one audience’s figures while leaving the other audience with an outdated version.

Conclusion

Board and investor reporting should be different views of the same financial reality. The governing group needs alternatives, assumptions, and a clear decision request. External capital providers need consistent performance information, capital-use updates, risks, and disclosures appropriate to their rights.

Start by reconciling the books, defining the reporting audience, and confirming who can approve decisions and communications. Then use the same financial foundation to explain both what the practice is considering and what it has actually authorized.

FAQs

What if the owners, board members, and investors are the same people?

Separate their roles even if the recipients overlap. Use a decision section for approvals and a performance section for results and outlook. Record decisions distinctly so receiving an update is not confused with authorizing a lease, hiring commitment, or distribution. Confirm formal approval requirements with advisers.

Must board reports be quarterly and investor updates monthly?

No universal schedule applies. Set governance meetings around oversight needs and important decisions. Check investor and lender agreements for reporting requirements, then agree on any additional updates. Label preliminary information clearly when a required communication precedes the completed financial close.

Can an investor update omit the downside forecast?

Do not assume that a shorter format permits omitting significant risks or required information. A summary may describe the key downside assumptions and implications without reproducing every internal worksheet, but disclosure requirements and recipient rights should be reviewed with advisers.

What should happen when forecast assumptions change?

Update the shared forecast, explain what changed, and distinguish the new outlook from previously approved spending. Use the same version in both reporting streams. If the change affects liquidity, commitments, or an earlier decision, bring it back to the authorized decision-makers and assess whether an external update is needed.
