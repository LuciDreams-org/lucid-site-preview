How AI Reviews Financial Behavior in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can have accurate books and still repeat financial decisions that create avoidable cash pressure. Purchases get approved after orders are placed. Budget exceptions become routine. Vendor invoices wait for an owner’s signature. Distributions leave the bank before anyone checks the next provider and administrative payroll.

These are questions about financial decision habits, not just expense totals. AI-assisted review can help organize approved financial and approval records, identify recurring sequences and prepare questions for management. It cannot establish why someone acted, whether a decision was justified or what caused a financial outcome.

The useful goal is narrow: identify a recurring decision pattern, verify it with the people responsible and test a practical process change. For staffed private medical practices, that means connecting management decisions to cash obligations, location budgets and entity-level accounts—not assigning psychological labels to employees.

What Financial Behavior Review Means

Ordinary bookkeeping asks where a transaction belongs. Financial behavior review asks how a decision moved from request to commitment, approval and payment.

For example, categorizing a purchase as medical supplies does not show whether it was planned, approved before ordering or part of repeated exceptions to a purchasing budget. Those questions require additional records.

Type of review Main question Boundary
Expense categorization Which account and location should record this purchase? Classification alone does not explain the decision process.
Internal financial behavior review Are purchasing, approval or distribution decisions following a recurring pattern? Patterns require contextual owner review; they do not reveal motives.
Fraud investigation Is there evidence of misconduct requiring investigation? An unusual pattern is not proof of fraud. Use a separate, appropriately supervised process.
Stock-market sentiment analysis What do investors or market participants appear to expect? Market sentiment is not evidence about a practice’s internal management habits.

A useful finding is: “Several purchases were committed before documented approval.” An unsupported conclusion is: “The manager is impulsive.” Keep the review focused on observable events and process design.

Start With a Decision You Can Change

Avoid asking AI to “analyze our financial behavior” without a defined scope. Choose a question tied to an owner decision or a management workflow.

Rushed purchasing

Review whether orders repeatedly occur outside the normal purchasing cycle, before approval or without a documented budget check. Ask whether the pattern reflects supply shortages, unclear reorder responsibilities or genuine urgent needs.

The response might be a revised ordering calendar or clearer backup approval—not simply a blanket spending restriction.

Repeated budget overrides

Compare approved budgets with documented exceptions by spending category and location. Recurring overrides may indicate an unrealistic budget, a new operating requirement or a process that treats every purchase as an exception.

The owner’s decision is whether to revise the budget, change approval authority or require better support for exceptions.

Delayed approvals

Compare complete request dates, approval dates and payment deadlines. A late payment may follow a delayed approval, but it may also reflect a disputed invoice, missing documentation or a deliberate cash-management decision.

Measure the approval step separately from invoice preparation and payment processing. Otherwise, the review may blame the wrong part of the workflow.

Distributions without an obligation check

Review whether owner distributions have documented cash reviews covering upcoming payroll, vendor payments, debt payments and other known obligations. Consider expected payer receipts and patient-balance collections cautiously; forecast receipts are not cash already available.

For multi-entity practices, review the account making the payment. Cash in another entity’s account should not be treated as freely available without appropriate accounting and professional review.

Build an Approved, Limited Dataset

Bank activity alone cannot establish how a decision was made. A payment date is not an approval date, and a credit-card posting date is not necessarily the purchase commitment date.

Use approved records that support the specific question:

Record Useful fields Important limitation
General ledger and reconciled bank records Transaction reference, amount, category, entity, location and payment date These records show financial activity, not necessarily approval timing.
Purchase requests and approval records Request, commitment and approval dates; approval role; exception status Missing documentation does not prove approval never occurred.
Budget versions Category, location, effective period and approved revisions Compare decisions with the budget in effect at the time.
Cash forecast and obligation schedule Opening cash, expected receipts and upcoming payments Forecasts depend on assumptions and may omit commitments.
Decision log Business rationale, evidence reference, review owner and follow-up Record confirmed context rather than AI-generated explanations.

Start with clean books. The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals make a pattern review more interpretable; they do not validate an AI conclusion.

Preserve meaningful distinctions. A shared administrative cost allocated across locations should not appear to be several independent purchasing decisions. Intercompany transfers should not be mistaken for new spending. Aggregate provider and administrative payroll obligations when individual compensation details are unnecessary.

How to Run an AI-Assisted Review

Much of this work can begin in a spreadsheet. AI may help summarize approved notes, group documented exception reasons and draft questions. Calculate dates, totals and rates using reproducible methods, and verify AI summaries against the underlying records.

1. Define observable events

Replace vague labels with explicit definitions. “Rushed purchase” might mean an order committed before required approval, rather than an order someone considered expensive. “Delayed approval” should specify when a complete request became ready for review.

Agree on exclusions, such as documented urgent purchases or disputed invoices. Keep exclusions visible so they cannot silently remove inconvenient results.

2. Establish a fair comparison

Use a period that covers the relevant purchasing or payroll cycles. Compare similar categories and distinguish ordinary operations from a location opening, equipment replacement or other one-time event.

Use denominators as well as counts. More exceptions at a larger location may simply reflect more purchase requests. Compare exceptions with eligible requests and review the associated dollars separately.

3. Ask for evidence-linked questions

A bounded instruction for an approved analysis environment could be:

Using only this approved, patient-free dataset, summarize recurring approval and budget-exception patterns. For each observation, identify the supporting record references, missing information, possible alternative explanations and a management review question. Do not infer motives, personality, misconduct or causation.

Have the reviewer check whether the records actually support each observation. If approval information is missing, report the gap rather than filling it with a plausible story.

4. Validate the context with management

The owner or designated manager should review exceptions with the people who understand the workflow. Was an approval given elsewhere? Did the supplier change delivery terms? Was the budget outdated? Did the request arrive without required documents?

Capture confirmed context in the decision log. Leave unresolved items marked as unresolved. Do not convert an AI-generated explanation into a management fact.

5. Test a process change

Choose a change that addresses the verified issue: a backup approver, an updated purchasing schedule, a revised budget or a documented cash check before discretionary distributions.

AI should not independently authorize payments, restrict purchases or approve distributions. Decision authority remains with the practice’s authorized people.

An Anonymized Decision-Log Example

Illustrative example only: The following anonymized log describes a fictional two-location practice. Its figures are invented to demonstrate the workflow, not actual client results or expected improvements.

During an eight-week review, six of 24 eligible supply orders were committed before documented approval. Four of those six occurred shortly before the regular ordering day. That sequence raises a question; it does not establish that anyone acted carelessly.

Decision-log field Illustrative entry
Observed pattern Six of 24 eligible orders preceded documented approval; four occurred shortly before the regular ordering day.
Evidence reference Purchase-request, order and approval references retained in the approved internal log.
Alternative explanations to check Low-stock alerts, supplier cutoffs, missing approval records or an ordering calendar that does not match demand.
Proposed review step Practice manager and purchasing lead review the affected orders and confirm reasons before changing the process.
Proposed change If the calendar is the issue, adjust order timing and name a backup approver for documented urgent requests.
Measurable follow-up Over the next eight weeks, track pre-approval commitments divided by eligible orders, approval turnaround and urgent-order count.
Balancing measure Track supply interruptions so fewer exceptions do not conceal an operational problem.
Decision owner Practice manager implements the approved change; owner reviews the follow-up.

Suppose the follow-up shows fewer exceptions. That would not, by itself, prove the change caused the difference. Purchasing volume, staffing or supplier conditions may also have changed. Review both the metric and the operating context before keeping or expanding the new process.

Protect Patient Information and Avoid Employee Profiling

This review usually needs financial process data, not patient-level records. Use aggregate payer receipts and patient-balance collections where cash context is relevant. Do not put patient names, account-level patient details, clinical notes or other patient-identifiable information into general-purpose AI tools or ordinary Slack messages.

Removing names alone should not be treated as proof that a dataset is safe to share. Have the appropriate privacy or security reviewer approve the dataset, tool and workflow before use.

HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities when protected health information is involved. Its cloud-computing guidance also explains that applicable business-associate agreements and safeguards matter; encryption alone is not sufficient. No AI analysis or vendor description establishes compliance.

Keep the review focused on processes rather than employee profiles:

  • Use roles and workflow stages when individual names are unnecessary.
  • Do not create personality, trustworthiness or “financial discipline” scores.
  • Do not use sentiment analysis of staff messages to infer motives.
  • Give responsible managers an opportunity to correct incomplete records and explain exceptions.
  • Do not use an AI-generated flag as the sole basis for an employment or misconduct decision.

The purpose is to improve how decisions get made, not build a surveillance system around the people making them.

Turn Findings Into a Management Routine

Add a short decision-pattern review to the practice’s financial meeting. Cover the observation, verified context, proposed action, accountable owner and follow-up measure. Keep open questions separate from confirmed findings.

For multiple locations, review both local decisions and shared-cost policies. For multiple entities, preserve entity-level obligations and account balances. A consolidated total can hide the account-level constraint that matters before a payment or distribution.

FAQs

Can AI explain why a practice owner repeatedly overrides a budget?

No. It can help organize documented overrides and summarize recorded reasons, but it cannot establish motives or causation. The owner must review whether the budget was realistic, circumstances changed or the approval process needs revision.

What if the practice has no approval records?

Start with the records available, but limit the conclusions. Bank and ledger data can identify purchases worth reviewing, not prove when approval occurred. Introduce a simple decision log with request, commitment and approval dates, documented exceptions and evidence references before attempting a detailed timing analysis.

Does an unusual spending pattern indicate fraud?

Not by itself. Unusual activity can reflect timing, miscoding, urgent needs or missing records. Treat it as a question for review. Concerns about misconduct require a separate, appropriately supervised investigation rather than an AI-generated accusation.

How should a practice measure whether the review helped?

Compare a clearly defined baseline with a follow-up period. Useful measures include approval turnaround, exceptions as a share of eligible requests and distributions with documented obligation checks. Include balancing measures, such as overdue invoices or supply interruptions, so a better headline metric does not hide a new problem.
