Multinational Securities Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A person or entity abroad offers to fund your medical practice’s next location, buy an ownership interest or provide financing tied to future profits. Before discussing a closing date, you need to establish exactly what is being proposed—and which advisers must evaluate it.

The starting point is not choosing a securities exemption or copying another practice’s ownership structure. It is preparing a clear transaction record for licensed securities and healthcare counsel, with cross-border tax advice coordinated through your CPA.

This guide helps owners and managers of staffed U.S. private medical practices assemble that record. It focuses on raising capital from abroad, not international vendor payments or selecting investments. It is a preparation framework, not a determination that a transaction is permitted.

Start With the Proposed Transaction, Not the Investor’s Location Alone

“An overseas investor wants to invest” leaves important questions unanswered. Is the investor buying an existing owner’s interest, contributing new money to an entity or lending funds? Which entity receives the money? What rights does the investor expect?

These distinctions also affect the practice’s financial planning. A payment to a selling physician is not the same as cash available to fund provider payroll or a new office buildout.

Prepare a plain-language transaction summary before commissioning detailed projections:

Decision input What to document Why the practice owner needs it
Proposed instrument Equity interest, loan, convertible arrangement, profit-linked payment or other proposed contract Gives counsel the actual economics to assess rather than a label
Recipient of funds Exact legal entity or selling owner receiving each payment Separates practice funding from owner liquidity
Investor rights Proposed voting, information, approval, repayment, conversion and exit rights Makes control and cash commitments visible
Intended use Working capital, acquisition, equipment, location expansion or owner buyout Connects the transaction to a defined financial need
Current status Preliminary discussion, circulated terms, signed document or money already received Helps advisers identify what needs immediate attention

Treat descriptions such as “silent partner,” “family loan” or “passive investment” as starting points for discussion, not legal conclusions. Provide the underlying terms and let counsel evaluate them.

Map the Parties and Jurisdictions

Do not assume that foreign residence alone determines which rules apply. Equally, do not assume that a U.S. practice can evaluate the transaction using only its home-state documents. Ask counsel to identify the relevant jurisdictions from the facts.

Build a party list that includes:

  • The practice entity proposing to issue an interest or borrow funds.
  • Any owner selling an existing interest.
  • Related entities involved in the proposal, including existing management or property entities.
  • The proposed investor’s legal name, entity type, formation location and residence or business location.
  • The people who own, control or act for an investing entity, to the extent known.
  • Anyone making introductions, negotiating terms or expecting compensation for arranging funding.
  • The places where discussions, presentations and proposed commitments have occurred.

Keep unanswered items marked as unknown. An incomplete ownership chain should not be replaced with an assumption that the person attending calls is the ultimate investor.

Ask securities counsel whether foreign counsel is needed and who will coordinate that work. Healthcare counsel should separately evaluate how the proposed parties and rights interact with the practice’s professional ownership and operational arrangements. A CPA or tax adviser should assess the transaction’s accounting and tax questions without substituting for either legal review.

Preserve the Solicitation History

Counsel needs to know how the opportunity reached the investor—not just what the final agreement says.

Create a dated chronology of introductions, emails, messages, presentations, website statements and draft terms. Include communications by owners, staff, consultants and intermediaries. Identify who received each version and whether anyone was asked to forward it.

Useful intake questions include:

  • Was this a private conversation with a known person, or was the opportunity promoted more broadly?
  • Did a website, social post, event or group message mention investment terms?
  • Have financial projections or statements about expected returns already been shared?
  • Has anyone promised an introduction fee or other transaction-related compensation?
  • Has anyone signed, committed funds or transferred money?

Preserve original communications and flag inaccuracies for counsel. Do not quietly rewrite the history to match the transaction you now hope to complete.

Before distributing additional materials or taking the next transaction step, ask counsel what can proceed and what should wait. If money has already arrived, tell counsel and your CPA promptly; do not independently decide how to return, spend or characterize it.

Review Ownership Documents Alongside the Proposed Economics

A financially attractive proposal still needs review against the practice’s existing documents and applicable restrictions. Securities analysis and healthcare ownership analysis answer different questions; neither should be treated as a substitute for the other.

Give counsel current formation documents, governing agreements, ownership schedules, prior investment documents and amendments. Include relevant debt agreements and other contracts that may contain consent or change-of-control provisions.

Proposed feature Question for advisers
Ownership interest for a person or entity abroad Do professional-ownership or other restrictions affect this investor, entity or transaction?
Investor approval over budgets or leadership Could the proposed rights create concerns distinct from the stated ownership percentage?
Profit-linked payments How should the arrangement be evaluated under securities, healthcare and tax considerations?
Interest in an existing related entity What does that entity actually own or do, and how do its agreements connect to the practice?
Future conversion or transfer rights What further review would be needed before those rights could be exercised?
Purchase of an existing owner’s interest What approvals, restrictions and financial consequences need to be addressed?

Do not assume that routing funds through a related entity resolves an ownership concern. Present the full entity diagram, contracts and flow of funds for review rather than selecting a structure in advance.

Build an Adviser Intake Packet

A well-organized packet lets advisers work from the same facts. Assign one practice manager or owner to maintain the index, track missing items and distinguish drafts from executed documents.

1. Transaction summary and party map

Include the proposed instrument, parties, jurisdictions, rights, timing and current status. Add an entity diagram showing existing ownership and the proposed changes. Label proposed interests clearly so they are not confused with completed transactions.

2. Existing legal documents

Collect governing agreements, amendments, ownership records, prior financing documents and relevant consent provisions. Identify discrepancies between the ownership schedule and signed documents rather than attempting to resolve them informally.

3. Solicitation and negotiation record

Provide the dated chronology, presentation materials, term sheets, correspondence and intermediary arrangements. Keep a version list so advisers can see which terms each recipient received.

4. Financial records and funding plan

Prepare current balance sheets, income statements, cash-flow information, debt schedules and bank reconciliations. For a multi-location or multi-entity practice, show where the proposed funding will be received and where it will be used.

Explain material shared-cost allocations. An investor reviewing one location’s results needs to understand whether central administrative payroll, rent or other shared expenses are included.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciliation of bank records. Those practices provide a sound financial foundation for transaction preparation; they do not establish that a proposed investment is legally permissible.

5. Open-issues register

Use a short table listing each unresolved question, responsible adviser, requested document and next action. Ask advisers to supply applicable requirements and timing rather than entering assumed filing deadlines from an online checklist.

Connect the Capital Request to Practice Cash Flow

The funding plan should answer two separate questions: how much cash the practice needs and what financial obligations the proposed transaction creates.

Separate proceeds by recipient and use

Distinguish money entering the practice from money paid to an existing owner. Then map practice proceeds to specific uses, such as equipment, additional space or working capital during provider recruitment.

For each use, identify the responsible entity, expected timing and supporting estimate. Keep transaction expenses visible rather than treating all gross proceeds as available operating cash.

Test the period before additional collections arrive

A new location may incur rent and provider/admin payroll before its payer receipts and patient collections support those costs. Build a cash forecast showing the timing assumptions, not just a profitable annual projection.

Compare a base case with a delayed-collections case. Make clear which figures come from existing records, which are estimates and which depend on events that have not occurred. Ask the finance team to show how proposed repayment or distribution commitments would affect the cash available for operations.

Reconcile the plan with the legal terms

If the financial model assumes flexible payments but the draft agreement describes fixed obligations, flag the mismatch. Likewise, identify any forecast that assumes investor funds can move freely among related entities. Counsel and the CPA should review the proposed movement of funds before the model treats it as settled.

Use aggregate financial information for initial analysis. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack conversations or general-purpose AI tools. If detailed records are requested, coordinate the appropriate scope and handling process before sharing them.

Take Specific Questions to the Adviser Meeting

Ask for a coordinated assessment of the proposed transaction, not a generic explanation of international fundraising.

For securities counsel:

  • Does the proposed instrument or arrangement involve securities, and what facts determine that assessment?
  • Which U.S. federal, state and foreign requirements need evaluation?
  • How does the solicitation history affect available next steps?
  • What investor information, disclosures, agreements or filings would be needed if the transaction proceeds?
  • Do intermediary roles or compensation arrangements raise additional questions?

For healthcare counsel:

  • Do professional-ownership, control or other healthcare restrictions affect the proposed investor or rights?
  • What issues arise from the relationship between the practice and any related entities?
  • Are existing agreements, approvals or notices relevant to the proposal?

For the CPA or tax adviser:

  • How should proposed proceeds, transaction costs and subsequent payments be modeled and recorded?
  • What cross-border tax, reporting or withholding questions require specialist review?
  • What entity-level records will be needed if the transaction closes?

Ask the advisers to distinguish confirmed conclusions from questions requiring more facts. Leave with named owners for each next action and a clear understanding of which steps remain on hold.

FAQs

Does money from someone abroad automatically make the transaction a securities offering?

Do not decide from the source of funds alone. Give counsel the proposed instrument, economic rights, parties, jurisdictions and solicitation history. Ask whether securities requirements apply and what additional facts are needed before taking further steps.

Can a foreign friend or relative invest in my medical practice?

A personal relationship is not a substitute for legal review. Ask securities and healthcare counsel to assess the proposed investor, ownership or repayment rights, relevant jurisdictions and existing practice documents. Do not assume that a familiar investor can hold the proposed interest.

Is a loan or an investment through a related entity a simpler alternative?

It may change the questions, but the label does not settle them. Provide all proposed repayment, conversion, profit-sharing, approval and transfer rights. Counsel should evaluate the arrangement as a whole; this guide does not recommend a particular instrument or entity structure.

What if the investor has already sent funds?

Tell counsel and your CPA promptly. Preserve the transfer record, communications and any signed or proposed terms. Request instructions before spending, returning or recharacterizing the funds, and have the CPA reconcile the accounting treatment with the documented resolution.
