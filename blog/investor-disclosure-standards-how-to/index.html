Investor Disclosure Preparation for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Before sharing financial information with existing or prospective investors, a private medical practice needs more than a polished presentation. It needs a documented process for deciding what to provide, supporting each statement, and obtaining the appropriate approvals.

For a staffed practice, that work may span several sources: entity-level books, location reports, payer receipts, patient receivables, provider and administrative payroll, debt agreements, and owner-related transactions. A presentation can look internally consistent while still omitting an entity, using an outdated debt balance, or treating projected collections as actual results.

Start with a disclosure requirements matrix prepared with licensed counsel and your CPA. Use it to connect each requested item to its source, responsible person, recipient, deadline, and approval status. The goal is a complete, supportable package—not simply a faster investor update.

This guide describes a preparation workflow. Counsel must determine applicable legal requirements, ownership restrictions, and disclosure obligations; the workflow does not establish securities-law standards or permissible investment structures.

1. Define the request before collecting documents

Begin by identifying the purpose of the disclosure. An existing investor exercising contractual information rights is not the same as a prospective investor conducting diligence. Neither situation automatically establishes what another recipient may receive.

Ask the practice owner, manager, counsel, and CPA to agree on:

  • Purpose: Routine contractual reporting, a proposed investment, a transaction review, or another defined request.
  • Reporting perimeter: The exact legal entities and locations covered, including any separately owned management or real-estate entity relevant to the request.
  • Periods and dates: Historical periods, the financial statement cutoff, and the date through which subsequent developments will be reviewed.
  • Recipients: Named individuals and advisers authorized to receive each part of the package.
  • Statement basis: Cash, accrual, or another clearly identified accounting basis, with the CPA confirming suitability for the request.
  • Deliverables: Financial statements, supporting schedules, agreements, forecasts, and explanatory notes.

Do not assume that a practice-wide presentation and a single entity’s financial statements describe the same business. A location can operate within one entity, while another entity owns equipment or employs shared administrative staff. Define the boundaries before comparing results.

Separate contractual requests from legal requirements

Maintain distinct categories for each item:

Request category How to establish the scope Who resolves uncertainty
Contractual reporting obligation Review the executed agreement, amendments, recipient rights, and delivery provisions Counsel interprets the obligation; CPA assesses financial preparation needs
Investor diligence request Record the written request and proposed response Owner and counsel approve scope; CPA confirms available financial evidence
Potential legal requirement Flag the issue without assigning a rule based on a generic checklist Licensed counsel determines applicability, content, timing, and recipients
Voluntary management explanation Identify why the explanation is useful and what evidence supports it Owner approves the narrative with relevant adviser review

An investor’s preferred format is not automatically a legal requirement. Conversely, satisfying an investor’s checklist does not establish that all applicable obligations have been met.

2. Build the disclosure requirements matrix

Inventory governing documents before drafting the package. Include executed ownership and investor agreements, amendments, relevant resolutions, debt agreements, leases, and other documents counsel identifies as relevant. Keep unsigned drafts separate from executed versions.

Use the matrix below as a working structure. The entries describe preparation tasks, not a universal list of legally required disclosures.

Area Evidence to inventory Review question Responsible lead
Governing agreements Executed agreements, amendments, reporting provisions, relevant approvals Who is entitled to receive what information, and when? Counsel
Financial statements Requested income statements, balance sheets, cash flow statements, notes, and comparatives Do entity, period, accounting basis, and statement status match the request? CPA
Debt obligations Loan agreements, lender statements, repayment schedules, guarantees, covenant correspondence Do recorded balances and described obligations agree with supporting documents? Finance lead and counsel
Related-party transactions Owner loans, affiliated leases, management charges, reimbursements, outstanding balances Are relationships, amounts, terms, and approvals adequately explained? CPA and counsel
Ownership information Counsel-confirmed ownership records and executed transaction documents Are the records current and appropriate to share with this recipient? Counsel
Material uncertainties Supported summaries of significant collection issues, staffing dependencies, disputes, financing needs, or contract changes What needs investigation, explanation, or legal review? Owner, CPA, and counsel
Forecasts and funding plans Approved model, assumptions, financing needs, and intended uses of funds Are forecasts clearly separated from historical results? Owner and finance lead

Add columns for the exact agreement section or request reference, named recipient, due date, document link, preparer, reviewer, and release status. If applicability remains uncertain, use a status such as Pending counsel determination, rather than leaving the cell blank.

Record both the external deadline and the internal review deadline. Where delivery depends on a contractual event rather than a fixed date, record the trigger and have counsel confirm how the deadline is determined.

3. Turn the matrix into a document-request tracker

The requirements matrix explains why an item belongs in the package. The document-request tracker shows whether the team has obtained and checked it.

Use one accountable owner per request. “Finance team” is too broad when the manager expects the CPA to retrieve a statement and the CPA expects the manager to provide it.

The following is an illustrative tracker; its timing labels are workflow examples, not prescribed deadlines.

Request Supporting evidence Accountable owner Internal due point Completion test
Entity-level financial statements Approved ledger and reconciled balance sheet schedules CPA Before presentation review Period, basis, entities, and version confirmed
Payer and patient receivable summary Aggregate aging and relevant adjustment support Finance lead Before revenue review Totals reconciled to the ledger or differences explained
Provider and administrative payroll Payroll summaries, accrual support, benefits records Practice manager Before expense review Payroll expense and liabilities reconciled
Debt schedule Executed agreements and current lender statements Finance lead Before liquidity review Principal, interest, maturity, and guarantees checked
Related-party schedule Agreements, ledger detail, and balance confirmations where needed Owner Before adviser review Relationship, terms, amounts, and balances documented
Shared-cost allocation Allocation policy and supporting calculations CPA Before location reporting review Allocation basis applied consistently and explained
Final presentation Approved statements and reconciliation worksheet Owner Before release authorization Every financial claim has a source or a clearly labeled assumption

Track missing evidence separately from missing analysis. “Awaiting lender statement” requires document collection; “guarantee language under review” requires counsel’s judgment. Neither should be marked complete merely because a draft schedule exists.

4. Establish reliable financial support

Before using financial statements in an investor package, reconcile the underlying records. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, keeping business accounts separate, and reconciling bank records. It does not establish an investor disclosure standard, but these practices help create dependable financial support.

For a medical practice, focus the review on areas that can change the investor’s interpretation:

  • Revenue versus receipts: Distinguish recognized revenue from payer and patient cash collections. Explain the accounting basis and reconcile relevant differences.
  • Receivables: Have the CPA assess the treatment of outstanding balances and adjustments. Do not present the full aging balance as guaranteed future cash.
  • Payroll: Include relevant accrued provider compensation, administrative payroll, benefits, and other obligations under the chosen accounting basis.
  • Debt and liquidity: Separate loan proceeds from operating revenue. Reconcile debt balances and distinguish cash balances from amounts available for general operations.
  • Location results: Explain how rent, centralized administration, and other shared costs are allocated. State whether a location report excludes central overhead.
  • Entity boundaries: Identify intercompany balances and transactions. Have the CPA determine the appropriate combined or consolidated presentation and treatment of internal charges.

“Approved financial statements” should mean the identified version authorized for this package. It does not mean audited or reviewed unless a CPA has actually performed that engagement and issued the corresponding report.

5. Reconcile the investor presentation to approved statements

Create a reconciliation worksheet before approving the presentation. For each financial claim, record the slide or page, amount, period, entity scope, source, adjustments, and reviewer.

Illustrative example: correcting a presentation before release

The following figures are invented solely to demonstrate the workflow. Assume a practice is presenting historical results for a completed six-month period.

Presentation claim Approved supporting record Difference identified Required correction
Historical revenue of $2.40 million Approved income statement shows $2.25 million Presentation includes $150,000 of next-period forecast revenue Report $2.25 million as historical revenue; show the forecast separately with its period and assumptions
Cash available of $420,000 Balance sheet shows $420,000 total cash; supporting schedule identifies $60,000 subject to use restrictions Total cash was described as fully available Show total cash and separately explain the $360,000 available under the stated assumptions, after adviser verification
Location operating profit of $300,000 Location schedule shows $300,000 before $80,000 of allocated central costs Presentation omits shared overhead Show $220,000 after allocation, or clearly label the pre-allocation measure and reconcile it

The finance reviewer should check both arithmetic and meaning. A number can agree with a spreadsheet yet remain misleadingly labeled. Collections are not necessarily revenue; pre-allocation location results are not necessarily practice-wide profit.

Keep a written explanation for each adjustment. If management presents an adjusted performance measure, define it, reconcile it to the relevant statement line, and have the CPA and counsel review its use. Do not remove recurring costs simply to improve the presentation.

6. Review uncertainties and related-party activity explicitly

Ask the owner and manager to identify developments that may not yet be obvious in the statements: a significant payer payment delay, a planned provider departure, an unresolved lease negotiation, a debt renewal, or an owner-affiliated service arrangement.

For each issue, document what is known, the evidence date, the potential financial effect if reasonably supportable, and what remains uncertain. Avoid converting an unresolved possibility into either a confirmed loss or a confident assurance.

Related-party schedules should distinguish owner distributions, compensation, loans, rent, reimbursements, and management charges. A payment to an owner is not automatically the same type of transaction in every case. The CPA determines accounting treatment; counsel evaluates the relevant agreements and legal implications.

Counsel should determine which matters require disclosure, to whom, and when. Do not defer an identified concern solely because it falls outside the next routine reporting cycle; send it for adviser assessment.

7. Obtain sign-off and control release

Use a release checklist with named approvals:

  • Practice owner or manager: Confirms operational descriptions, known developments, and the completeness of management-provided information.
  • CPA or financial reviewer: Confirms the agreed accounting scope, reconciliations, financial sources, and unresolved accounting matters.
  • Licensed counsel: Determines applicable legal and contractual requirements, reviews relevant disclosures, and confirms authorized recipients and release conditions.
  • Release coordinator: Confirms that the approved files—not earlier drafts—are being distributed.

Record the version, approval date, reviewer, and any conditions. If an unresolved item affects the package, document the advisers’ decision to correct, qualify, narrow, or hold the affected material.

Prefer aggregate financial schedules over patient-level records. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains a standard with exceptions; ask the appropriate advisers to determine what applies to the proposed disclosure. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Maintain a distribution log showing recipients, files, versions, and release dates. Before a later release, check whether new information requires revisions or renewed approval.

Build the financial foundation before the next request

A useful disclosure process connects every request to evidence and every released statement to an accountable reviewer. Start with the agreements, establish the reporting perimeter, reconcile the financial package, and resolve open questions before distribution.

FAQs

Does every investor request create a legal disclosure obligation?

No. Record whether the request comes from an executed agreement, a diligence request, or a potential legal requirement. Counsel must determine the applicable obligations and any limits on sharing. An investor checklist is not a substitute for that review.

Does a medical practice need audited financial statements before sharing information?

There is no universal answer based solely on being a medical practice. Counsel and the CPA should assess the applicable requirements, agreements, and requested engagement. Bookkeeping, financial statement preparation, a review, and an audit are different services; describe the actual status accurately.

What if a requested document is missing?

Mark the item as unresolved, assign an owner, and explain what evidence is unavailable. Tell the CPA and counsel rather than substituting an unsupported estimate. They should assess whether alternative support is appropriate and whether the affected material must be qualified or held.

What changes after an investment closes?

Have counsel confirm continuing reporting obligations, authorized recipients, deadlines, and relevant changes to governing documents. Update the matrix and tracker accordingly. Preserve the approved package and repeat the evidence, reconciliation, and sign-off process for subsequent disclosures rather than assuming the original package remains current.
