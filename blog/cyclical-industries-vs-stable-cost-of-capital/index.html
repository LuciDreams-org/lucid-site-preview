Cyclical vs. Stable Cash Flows: Medical Practice Cost of Capital

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An expansion can look profitable on an annual forecast and still leave a medical practice short of cash when payroll and loan payments come due. The difference often lies in the pattern of receipts—not just their expected total.

More predictable cash flows can support financing, while cash flows that weaken sharply under stress may lead to tighter terms and a higher required return. But healthcare is not uniformly recession-proof, and a private practice should not borrow a public-company industry cost of capital as its investment benchmark.

For an owner considering more space, equipment or a second location, the useful question is: How much cash will this project produce, how variable could it be, and can the practice carry its fixed commitments through a weak period?

What Makes Practice Cash Flow Cyclical or Stable?

Economic cyclicality means activity rises and falls with broader economic conditions. For a private medical practice, exposure depends on its services, patient payment responsibilities, local employment conditions and other demand drivers. Services patients can defer may behave differently from recurring care, but neither category guarantees collections.

Not every fluctuation is an economic cycle. Separate these patterns before setting financing assumptions:

Cash-flow pattern Possible practice example Financial implication
Economic cyclicality Patients defer discretionary, self-pay services when household budgets tighten Model lower demand and slower recovery, not just a brief receipt delay
Seasonality Recurring vacation-related scheduling changes or seasonal demand Plan monthly staffing and cash reserves around recurring troughs
Collection timing Payer receipts arrive later while earned revenue remains similar Model the working-capital gap separately from lost revenue
Structural change A provider leaves or a significant payer relationship changes Rebuild the forecast rather than assuming an automatic rebound
Relatively stable operations Established services generate repeatable collections across different conditions Test whether stability persists after adding capacity and fixed costs

A historically stable practice can undertake a risky expansion. A new location may lack the established patient base, staffing continuity or payer receipt history of the original office.

Conversely, a project with variable receipts may be manageable if the owner can stage spending, limit fixed commitments and maintain sufficient liquidity.

Fixed Costs Turn Receipt Variability Into Financing Risk

Cash-flow risk depends on both receipts and the costs that remain when receipts fall.

Rent, equipment payments and much of provider and administrative payroll may continue during a slow period. Some supplies and other activity-related expenses may decline, but not necessarily at the same pace as collections. This fixed-cost exposure is often called operating leverage.

When comparing projects, identify:

  • Costs that change directly with activity.
  • Costs that can change only after a delay or contractual commitment.
  • Costs that remain regardless of near-term volume.
  • Additional working capital needed between service delivery and collection.

Use realistic compensation for provider and management work. A project should not appear attractive only because the forecast assumes an owner supplies substantial unpaid labor.

For multi-location practices, show both incremental project cash flow and the effect on the existing business. Moving staff or patients into a new office may shift activity rather than create entirely new receipts. Shared administrative costs should also be visible, with allocation methods explained rather than treated as new cash spending automatically.

Borrowing Cost Is Not the Owner’s Required Return

The cost of debt is the cost of borrowed funds. A lender quote may include interest, fees and other conditions that affect the financing burden. Scheduled debt service includes both principal and interest; it is a cash obligation, not simply an interest expense.

The owner’s required return serves a different purpose. It is the return the owner needs to justify committing capital to an uncertain investment instead of another available use. Owner capital is not free just because it has no monthly payment.

A project may comfortably cover its loan payments yet offer an inadequate return on the owner’s investment. The reverse can also occur: a project may have attractive long-term potential but require more cash during its ramp-up than the practice can safely provide.

Weighted average cost of capital, or WACC, combines debt and equity costs using an appropriate financing mix. For a private practice, estimating the equity component requires judgment. Public-company betas and industry averages are not ready-made rates for a closely held practice with concentrated ownership, provider dependence and limited marketability.

The underlying principle is useful: riskier expected cash flows generally warrant a higher required return. This explanation of discount rates and cash-flow risk provides the general finance framework—not a recommended rate for your practice.

How Lenders May Assess Cash-Flow Stability

Lenders evaluate repayment capacity, not simply whether a project’s average forecast is positive. Relevant factors may include historical collections, existing obligations, cash reserves, collateral, guarantees, provider concentration and the reliability of expansion assumptions.

Greater variability can affect more than the interest rate:

Financing input Why cash-flow stability matters What the owner should compare
Interest structure A floating rate can add payment uncertainty to variable operations Fixed versus variable pricing, reset terms and applicable limits
Loan term and amortization A shorter repayment period raises required periodic payments Payment schedule, maturity and any balloon payment
Owner contribution A lender may limit its exposure to an uncertain project Cash required at closing and liquidity remaining afterward
Fees Upfront charges change the effective borrowing cost Fees, net proceeds and payment timing
Covenants A weak period may put financial requirements under pressure Definitions, testing periods and required reporting
Collateral and guarantees Repayment uncertainty may influence requested support Assets and owner obligations exposed under the documents

Stable receipts do not guarantee favorable terms. Credit conditions and the borrower’s broader financial position still matter. Compare written quotes on a consistent basis rather than treating a lower headline rate as the best offer.

Illustrative Comparison: Two Expansion Plans

All dollar amounts, rates, terms and outcomes in this section are invented illustrative assumptions, not market quotes, benchmarks or predictions.

Suppose a staffed practice is comparing two projects:

  • Plan A: Expand an established location serving an existing patient base. Expected collections are relatively consistent, although the project still has ramp-up risk.
  • Plan B: Open a separate location emphasizing services whose demand is more sensitive to household spending. Expected annual cash flow is higher, but downside exposure is greater.

The assumed quotes below hold funding amounts and repayment periods constant to highlight the impact of different pricing and cash-flow patterns. Actual lenders might also propose different contributions, collateral or covenants.

Illustrative input Plan A: More stable expansion Plan B: More variable expansion
Initial project spending $800,000 $800,000
Owner contribution $200,000 $200,000
Loan principal $600,000 $600,000
Assumed fixed interest rate 8.0% 9.5%
Repayment structure Seven years, monthly payments, fully amortizing Seven years, monthly payments, fully amortizing
Approximate annual principal and interest $112,000 $118,000
Fees Excluded for this simplified comparison Excluded for this simplified comparison
Stabilized base-case annual cash available before debt service $200,000 $240,000
Stabilized downside annual cash available before debt service $160,000 $90,000

Cash available in this example means incremental receipts less cash operating costs, maintenance spending and working-capital needs, before financing and taxes. It includes appropriate compensation for work performed. These are stabilized annual figures, not opening-year forecasts.

Compare Repayment Capacity, Not Just Expected Surplus

For this illustration, coverage equals cash available before debt service divided by scheduled principal and interest. A lender’s contractual debt-service coverage calculation may differ.

Illustrative result Plan A Plan B
Base-case coverage About 1.79× About 2.03×
Downside coverage About 1.43× About 0.76×
Base-case cash remaining after debt service, before tax About $88,000 About $122,000
Downside cash remaining after debt service, before tax About $48,000 About negative $28,000

Plan B produces more expected cash, but its downside does not cover scheduled debt service. The additional expected return comes with a potential funding requirement precisely when operating performance is weak.

That does not automatically disqualify Plan B. It means the owner must evaluate whether a smaller launch, staged spending, more owner capital or a larger reserve changes the risk enough to justify proceeding.

Check the Lowest Month

Annual coverage can conceal a liquidity problem. In this same illustrative comparison, suppose the lowest operating-cash month produces $11,000 under Plan A and $5,000 under Plan B, before debt service. Monthly loan payments are approximately $9,400 and $9,800, respectively.

Plan B would need cash support during that month even if its annual base case remains strong. Add opening delays and slower collections, and the required reserve could be larger than an annual summary suggests.

Build a monthly ramp-up forecast separately from stabilized performance. Include deposits, pre-opening payroll, equipment payments and the lag between activity and collected cash.

Evaluate the Investment Without Mixing Financing and Project Returns

Use two related but distinct analyses.

Project Economics

Estimate incremental project cash flows before financing. Include initial spending, working-capital investment, ongoing maintenance and a supportable end-of-horizon value, if applicable. Evaluate those cash flows using a required return appropriate to the project’s risk and the model’s tax basis.

For the more cyclical plan, test both a weaker operating period and a slower recovery. Avoid valuing the project solely from an unusually strong year.

Owner Cash Flows and Liquidity

Separately model loan proceeds, fees, scheduled repayments and the owner’s contributions. This shows how financing changes the timing and risk of cash available to the owner.

Do not subtract debt service from project cash flows and then apply a blended debt-and-equity discount rate without adjusting the framework; that can double-count financing costs. Have the finance adviser keep project and equity analyses consistent, including their treatment of taxes.

A positive investment value does not eliminate the need for liquidity. A practice must still pay staff, vendors and lenders during an unfavorable period.

Build a Decision File That Explains the Variability

Before signing financing or construction commitments, assemble a concise comparison supported by:

  • Reconciled historical financial statements and monthly payer and patient receipts.
  • Per-location results and entity-level cash balances where relevant.
  • An explanation of unusual peaks, troughs and one-time collections.
  • Base, downside and delayed-ramp forecasts with explicit assumptions.
  • Written lender quotes and the lowest projected cash balance under each plan.
  • Predetermined triggers for slowing spending or reconsidering the project.

The IRS guidance on business records supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those basics help ensure that apparent stability is supported by reliable numbers.

The Decision: Match Commitments to Downside Cash Flow

The more stable project is not always the better investment, and the higher-return project is not automatically worth its risk. Compare expected value, weak-period repayment capacity and the cash required to bridge the ramp-up.

For a medical practice owner, cost of capital is most useful when it connects the investment decision to operating reality: what cash can vary, what costs cannot, and who funds the gap when results disappoint.

FAQs

Are medical practices considered stable businesses for financing?

Some practices have relatively predictable collections, but lenders assess the individual borrower and project. Service mix, payer concentration, provider dependence, patient balances and fixed commitments can all affect stability. A stable existing location does not make a new location equally predictable.

Should a practice use its loan interest rate to evaluate an expansion?

Not by itself. The loan rate measures one financing cost; it does not measure the return required on owner capital or the project’s full risk. Evaluate project economics separately from debt affordability, then check whether the financing structure leaves sufficient liquidity.

How can an owner reduce exposure to cyclical cash flows?

Consider staged construction or equipment purchases, a smaller initial footprint and staffing commitments aligned with a realistic ramp-up. Maintain liquidity based on the projected cash trough, not just annual profit. Evaluate changes against operational needs rather than assuming every cost can be reduced immediately.
