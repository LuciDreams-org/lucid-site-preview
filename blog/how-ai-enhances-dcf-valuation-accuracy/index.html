AI-Assisted DCF Valuation Review for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A discounted cash flow valuation can look precise while resting on uncertain assumptions. For a staffed private medical practice, the result may depend on when payer receipts arrive, whether provider compensation is sustainable, how much equipment needs replacing, and what happens after the detailed forecast ends.

The owner's job is not to accept an AI-generated value. It is to understand which assumptions drive the valuation, who approved them, and what happens when they change.

AI may help organize assumptions and flag inconsistencies. It does not establish that a forecast is achievable or that a discount rate is appropriate. Keep valuation conclusions and discount-rate selection with qualified valuation professionals, with accounting and tax input where needed.

This guide provides a review workflow and a workbook structure for examining a medical practice DCF—not a substitute for a professional valuation.

Start by defining what the DCF values

A DCF estimates the present value of future cash flows. Before reviewing formulas, ask the preparer to document:

  • Valuation date and purpose: What decision will the analysis support?
  • Scope: Does it cover one location, an operating entity, or several entities together?
  • Included assets and obligations: Are real estate, equipment, cash, debt and related-party balances included or treated separately?
  • Cash-flow basis: Does the model value operations before financing, or cash flows available to equity owners?
  • Operating assumptions: Does the forecast assume the current owner continues providing clinical and management services?

These choices must remain consistent throughout the model. A practice operating company should not absorb a related property entity's costs without a clear explanation of the arrangement. Likewise, intercompany management fees should not inflate consolidated revenue or be counted twice as expenses.

A common operating-business approach uses free cash flow to the firm, discounted at a rate appropriate for those cash flows. Its operating value then requires a documented bridge to equity value, including relevant debt, nonoperating assets and other adjustments. Do not mix this approach with cash flows already reduced by debt service.

Review the forecast cash flows before the valuation result

Revenue must connect to achievable collections

Start with service activity, collectible revenue and receipt timing—not gross charges alone. Review the forecast by provider or location where that detail explains the assumptions.

Ask whether projected growth comes from existing capacity, a new clinician, added sessions, a location opening, or a change in payer reimbursement. Each driver needs supporting evidence and an accountable owner.

For example, a forecast that adds provider revenue before the provider's expected start date needs correction. A forecast that assumes immediate full scheduling after hiring needs an explicit ramp assumption. Neither issue is solved by a more elaborate valuation formula.

Patient balances and payer receivables also need realistic collection assumptions. Do not treat every outstanding balance as future cash at face value.

Normalize expenses without removing recurring costs

Provider and administrative payroll, benefits, occupancy, supplies and shared administrative costs must support the projected activity.

Owner compensation deserves particular attention. Separate compensation for clinical or management work from ownership distributions. If the forecast removes owner compensation but still assumes the owner's work continues, it needs an appropriate replacement-cost assumption reviewed by the valuation professional.

Similarly, a cost labeled “one-time” should have a documented reason for exclusion. Recurring recruitment, equipment repairs or repeated temporary staffing costs may not disappear merely because management would prefer them to.

For multiple locations, reconcile location forecasts to entity-level accounts. Document shared-cost allocations, and distinguish an accounting allocation from an expense that would actually disappear if a location closed.

Reconcile earnings to free cash flow

Under a common unlevered DCF structure:

Free cash flow = operating profit after modeled taxes + depreciation and amortization − capital expenditures − increase in operating working capital.

This is a modeling framework, not a tax calculation. The valuation professional and tax adviser should determine the appropriate tax treatment for the entity and valuation basis.

Working capital: growth can consume cash

Operating working capital commonly includes collectible receivables and other operating current assets, less operating current liabilities. The preparer should define the included accounts and avoid mixing operating needs with financing balances or excess cash.

As payer receivables grow, the practice may fund payroll before collecting the related revenue. That increase in working capital reduces free cash flow. A reduction can release cash, but it cannot continue indefinitely without an operational explanation.

Avoid double counting receipt delays. If the model starts with accrual revenue, receivable movements help translate that revenue into cash. If it starts with forecast cash receipts, subtracting the same receivable increase again would understate cash flow. Ask the preparer to show the reconciliation.

Capital expenditure: depreciation is not a replacement budget

Depreciation is a noncash expense; equipment purchases use cash. Adding depreciation back without deducting appropriate capital expenditure can overstate cash available to investors.

Separate:

  • Maintenance spending: Replacing or maintaining assets needed for current operations.
  • Growth spending: Equipment and improvements required for additional capacity or locations.

Check the timing as well as the amount. Revenue from a planned expansion should not appear before the necessary equipment and buildout spending. Lease-related payments also need consistent treatment across cash flows, discounting and the bridge to equity value.

Test discounting and cash-flow timing

Under an end-of-year convention, the present value of a future cash flow is:

Present value = cash flow in period t ÷ (1 + discount rate)^t.

The timing convention matters. Monthly, midyear and year-end models use different timing assumptions. Ask why the chosen convention fits the practice's expected receipts and spending.

The discount rate must match the cash-flow definition, currency and inflation basis. Selection belongs with a qualified professional. Owners should ask how risks are reflected and whether a risk has been counted both in reduced cash flows and in a higher discount rate.

In the workbook, move delayed receipts into the later period rather than simply deleting them. Extend the forecast or reconcile amounts remaining beyond its endpoint. Distinguish a temporary collection delay from a permanent collection loss; the latter changes the amount as well as timing.

Examine terminal value separately

Terminal value represents cash flows beyond the explicit forecast. It should reflect a sustainable operating state, not an unusually strong final year.

For a constant-growth terminal calculation:

Terminal value at the forecast endpoint = next-period free cash flow ÷ (discount rate − perpetual growth rate).

The discount rate must exceed the perpetual growth rate. Terminal value must then be discounted back to the valuation date and added to the present value of explicit forecast cash flows.

Check whether the terminal cash flow includes sustainable provider compensation, maintenance capital spending and working-capital investment consistent with growth. A terminal period that assumes growth but no supporting reinvestment needs explanation.

These are undiscounted terminal values at the forecast endpoint, rounded to the nearest thousand—not present enterprise or equity values.

In the actual workbook, have the valuation professional select supported sensitivity ranges. Show the resulting present operating value and equity-value bridge for each combination. Also disclose how much of operating value comes from terminal value. Heavy dependence on that component warrants closer scrutiny of the long-run assumptions, not automatic rejection or acceptance.

Use AI for checks, not assumption approval

AI may assist with a defined review task, subject to human verification:

AI-assisted task Required human check
Compare approved assumptions with forecast inputs Confirm the correct source versions and model cells were compared
Flag inconsistent units, dates or signs Recalculate the affected formulas
Identify growth without supporting payroll or capital spending Decide whether the operating plan is feasible
Draft explanations of sensitivity movements Verify the arithmetic and economic interpretation
Summarize unresolved assumptions Confirm nothing material was omitted or falsely marked resolved

Use spreadsheet formulas for the calculations and retain a reviewed model version. AI-generated explanations can sound persuasive even when they misunderstand a formula or overlook a dependency. More scenarios do not establish greater accuracy.

Use aggregate financial data wherever possible. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool. Where financial work involves protected health information, obtain appropriate privacy and contractual guidance; HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities.

Close the review with decisions, not just a number

Before relying on the DCF, ask for a reproducible model, a completed assumption register, sensitivity results and an unresolved-issues list. Material unsupported assumptions should remain visible in the conclusion.

FAQs

Can AI determine my medical practice's DCF value?

AI can produce calculations or commentary, but that does not validate the assumptions. A qualified valuation professional should determine the appropriate methodology, discount rate and conclusion. Owners should require traceable inputs and independently checked calculations.

Why can a profitable practice have lower free cash flow?

Profit does not capture every cash requirement. Growing receivables, equipment purchases and other working-capital needs can reduce cash flow. The DCF should reconcile earnings to cash without counting the same cash movement twice.

What should I challenge if terminal value drives most of the result?

Ask whether the final forecast year represents sustainable operations, whether reinvestment supports assumed growth, and how the conclusion changes under professionally selected discount-rate and terminal assumptions. Request the present value of terminal cash flows separately from the explicit forecast.
