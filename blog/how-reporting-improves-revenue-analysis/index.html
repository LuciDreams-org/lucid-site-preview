Revenue Analysis Reports for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A medical practice can receive more cash while earning less revenue from the services performed that month. It can also report higher revenue because of an accounting correction rather than increased activity. Looking at the top line alone will not tell an owner which explanation is right.

A useful revenue analysis report answers a narrower question: What changed in the completed period, and what evidence explains it? For a staffed private practice, the answer may involve service activity, payer mix, contractual adjustments, collection timing, or corrections to the books.

Start by reconciling the underlying reports to the general ledger. Then separate service-period activity from receipts and build a bridge between comparable periods. This sequence helps owners avoid treating a delayed payer payment as growth—or an accounting cleanup as an operating decline.

Define What “Revenue” Means Before Comparing It

Billing reports, bank deposits, and the practice’s income statement may show different amounts without any of them necessarily being wrong. They measure different things.

Measure What it describes Main interpretation risk
Gross charges by service date Charges associated with services performed during a period Charges are not necessarily the amount the practice expects to collect
Estimated net service revenue Service-period charges less applicable contractual reductions and other revenue adjustments under the practice’s accounting policy Estimates may change as payer responses and other information arrive
Cash receipts by posting date Payments recorded during a period, potentially covering several service periods A posting date may differ from the bank settlement date
Ledger revenue Revenue recognized under the practice’s documented accounting basis It can include corrections or adjustments relating to earlier services

For accrual-basis reporting, service-period revenue and cash receipts usually need separate explanations. For cash-basis reporting, collections generally drive reported service revenue, subject to the practice’s accounting policies and classification of receipts. A loan advance, owner contribution, or transfer between practice accounts is not patient-service revenue merely because it appears as a deposit.

Have the CPA confirm the reporting basis and treatment of refunds, patient prepayments, contractual adjustments, and prior-period corrections. Do not quietly combine a cash-basis income statement with an accrual-style operating report and call the result a single revenue measure.

Reconcile the Reports Before Explaining the Change

A persuasive explanation built on unreconciled totals can still be wrong. Complete these checks before attributing a change to payer mix or practice activity.

1. Fix the reporting scope and cutoff

Record the legal entity, locations, service dates, payment-posting dates, bank settlement cutoff, and report extraction date. Identify whether the period is closed or still subject to late postings.

For multi-entity practices, reconcile each entity first. Eliminate intercompany transfers from the combined revenue view. For multiple locations, use a consistent location assignment rule and show unassigned amounts separately rather than distributing them without support.

2. Reconcile receipts to bank activity and the ledger

Compare aggregate payer and patient payment reports with bank settlements and the cash entries in the ledger. Explain differences such as deposits in transit, refunds, payment processing deductions, and payments posted in a different period from their settlement.

If a processor settles receipts net of fees, distinguish gross receipts from the fee expense under the practice’s accounting policy. A net bank deposit should not silently become the definition of revenue.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts, and reconciled bank records. Those controls provide the foundation for revenue analysis, although they do not determine every revenue-recognition judgment.

3. Reconcile service activity and adjustments to ledger revenue

On an accrual basis, connect aggregate service-period charges, contractual adjustments, other applicable revenue adjustments, and closing journal entries to ledger revenue. Separate changes to estimates for earlier services from amounts attributable to current services.

If the practice maintains receivables in the ledger, reconcile opening and closing net receivables as well. A receivables rollforward often exposes a missing adjustment or a receipt posted to the wrong account.

On a cash basis, reconcile recognized collections to ledger revenue. Keep service-date reporting as a supplemental operating analysis rather than implying that it is the booked revenue figure.

4. Keep an exception log

For every unresolved difference, record the amount, affected period, source reports, responsible reviewer, and next step. Label conclusions as provisional when a difference could change the explanation. Do not force a report to balance with an unexplained adjustment.

Separate Service-Period Activity From Cash Receipts

The owner’s report should show two views side by side: what happened to services performed and what happened to money received.

Illustrative example: The following fictional practice uses accrual-basis management reporting. Estimated net service revenue reflects applicable revenue adjustments. All receipts shown reduce service receivables; there are no patient prepayments, unapplied cash, refunds, or separate receivables movements in this simplified example. Dollar amounts are illustrative, not benchmarks.

Report line March April Interpretation
Gross charges for services performed in the month $400,000 $380,000 Charge activity declined; this is not yet a collectible-revenue measure
Reductions from current-service charges to estimated net revenue ($160,000) ($154,000) Reflects applicable contractual and other revenue adjustments
Estimated net revenue from current-month services $240,000 $226,000 Current-service revenue declined by $14,000
Revenue adjustment relating to earlier service periods $0 $6,000 April includes a favorable revision to an earlier estimate
Total ledger revenue $240,000 $232,000 Reported revenue declined by $8,000
Receipts applied to current-month services $95,000 $90,000 Only part of current-service revenue was collected that month
Receipts applied to earlier services $130,000 $160,000 Earlier services supplied more of April’s cash
Total cash receipts $225,000 $250,000 Collections increased despite lower current-service revenue

The important conclusion is not simply that April revenue fell or that cash improved. Current-service revenue fell, earlier-period collections increased, and a prior-service adjustment softened the reported revenue decline. Those are three different findings.

The receivables reconciliation confirms the relationship:

Net service receivables rollforward March April
Opening net receivables $300,000 $315,000
Plus ledger revenue $240,000 $232,000
Less cash applied to service receivables ($225,000) ($250,000)
Closing net receivables $315,000 $297,000

This simplified rollforward works because its assumptions exclude other movements. A real practice should add separate lines for any other applicable receivables changes rather than assume the same equation will always be sufficient.

For a cash-basis practice, the collection analysis would remain central to the ledger explanation. The service-period view would still be useful, but its estimated net revenue should be clearly labeled as supplemental and not represented as recognized ledger revenue.

Build a Revenue Bridge That Explains the Difference

A revenue bridge starts with the prior period’s reconciled revenue and assigns the change to supported drivers. It should end exactly at the current period’s reconciled revenue.

Illustrative example: Continuing the fictional accrual-basis practice above, assume a documented sequential analysis supports the following allocation. These driver amounts are illustrative assumptions, not conclusions derived from the summary totals alone.

Revenue bridge Effect Running total
March ledger revenue — $240,000
Lower service activity at the comparison-period service and payer mix ($12,000) $228,000
Change in service and payer mix, holding comparison rates fixed ($8,000) $220,000
Change in contractual allowances and current-service revenue estimates $6,000 $226,000
Favorable correction relating to earlier services $6,000 $232,000
April ledger revenue — $232,000

Keep driver definitions mutually exclusive

For the activity step, apply comparison-period net revenue assumptions to the change in a consistently defined activity measure. A completed encounter count may be useful, but it will not explain revenue reliably if the service composition changes substantially.

For the mix step, examine service categories and payer categories while holding comparison rates fixed. Distinguish payer mix from service mix where the data supports it. A single blended average cannot establish which one caused a change.

For the contractual step, isolate rate changes and revisions to expected collectible amounts that have not already been counted in the mix step. Keep true contractual adjustments separate from other adjustment categories when the source data allows it.

Finally, identify accounting corrections and earlier-service estimate changes separately. Their effects may matter to reported earnings, but they do not demonstrate increased current-period activity.

The order of a sequential bridge can affect the allocation between activity, mix, and rate effects. Document the method and use it consistently. If available data cannot isolate the drivers, report a combined effect or an explicitly unexplained residual rather than inventing precision.

Compare Periods That Can Support a Fair Conclusion

A reconciled report can still mislead if the periods are not comparable.

  • Operating days: Show total activity alongside activity per open day when calendars differ. Neither measure should replace the other.
  • Provider availability: Note changes in staffed schedules, leave, or available sessions before interpreting lower activity as weaker demand.
  • Location scope: Show comparable-location results separately from newly opened, acquired, or closed locations.
  • Posting maturity: A recently completed service month may have less complete charge and adjustment data than an older month. Compare equally mature extracts where practical, or flag the imbalance.
  • Payer classification: Keep category mappings stable. Reclassification can create an apparent mix shift without a genuine economic change.
  • Accounting policy: Explain changes in recognition, estimation, or account mapping. Where appropriate, provide a comparable management view without obscuring the booked figures.

Use month-over-month and prior-year comparisons when they answer different questions. The former shows recent movement; the latter may provide context for recurring calendar patterns. Neither automatically establishes the cause of a change.

Turn Findings Into a Focused Owner Review

A monthly revenue review should end with a short explanation and a specific follow-up, not a dashboard tour.

Finding What the owner should ask next
Current-service revenue fell with lower activity Does the decline align with operating days and provider availability?
Activity was stable but payer or service mix changed Which categories changed, and are the classifications consistent?
Contractual adjustments increased Is the movement supported by rate terms, payer responses, or a revised estimate?
Cash rose while current-service revenue fell How much came from earlier service periods, and how did net receivables change?
Ledger revenue changed because of a correction Which period does it concern, and does the comparison need a separate explanatory view?
A location shows unexplained movement Are services, receipts, and adjustments assigned to the same location consistently?

Revenue analysis alone does not establish profitability. If the owner’s next decision concerns staffing or location economics, bring the reconciled findings into the relevant P&L review, including provider and administrative payroll and consistently allocated shared costs. Do not infer margin from collections alone.

Maintain a Repeatable, Privacy-Conscious Workflow

For each closed month, retain the source extracts, reconciliation, accounting-basis note, bridge methodology, and owner explanation together. Record the extraction date so later revisions can be distinguished from the original report.

Use aggregate financial information for owner reporting whenever possible. Avoid patient names, account identifiers, or clinical details in general management discussions. The HHS minimum necessary guidance provides relevant context, with exceptions; access and handling requirements should be evaluated for the actual workflow. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

Why can collections increase while medical practice revenue falls?

Collections may include payments for earlier services. On an accrual basis, current-service revenue can decline while those older balances produce more cash. On a cash basis, recognized collections generally drive service revenue, so the report must clearly distinguish booked revenue from supplemental service-period activity.

Which reports should be reconciled before analyzing revenue changes?

Start with aggregate service activity and adjustment reports, payer and patient payment summaries, bank activity, and ledger revenue accounts. For accrual reporting, include the receivables rollforward and closing journal entries. Document differences in cutoff, settlement timing, and classification before explaining operating trends.

How often should an owner review revenue analysis reports?

Review the completed monthly analysis after reconciliation and close. More frequent collection checks can help monitor cash, but they should not be presented as a final explanation of monthly service revenue. Mark incomplete reports as provisional and identify which late postings or unresolved differences could change the conclusion.

Can a revenue bridge prove that payer mix caused a decline?

Only if the underlying data and method can distinguish payer mix from activity, service mix, rate changes, and accounting adjustments. Hold comparison rates fixed when measuring mix and use consistent categories. If the available reports cannot separate those effects, disclose the limitation and show a combined effect rather than an unsupported attribution.
