Initial Equity Funding Preparation for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A first outside equity contribution is more than an agreement on price. For a staffed medical practice, it raises questions about who may own an interest, which entity receives the money, how decisions will be made, and what happens to future distributions and sale proceeds.

The owner's first job is to prepare a decision-ready package—not select a startup financing template. That package should connect accurate financial records with the proposed capital uses, ownership economics, governance preferences and exit expectations. It gives counsel and the CPA a common starting point before documents are drafted or terms become difficult to change.

This guide covers preparation for an initial equity transaction in an established or newly organized US private medical practice. Investor eligibility, professional ownership restrictions, securities requirements and transaction structure require jurisdiction-specific legal advice. Do not assume a SAFE, convertible note or preferred-stock template is suitable for your practice.

1. Establish What Can Be Funded and Who Can Participate

Before discussing a percentage interest, prepare a simple diagram of the existing entities and their owners. Identify the professional practice entity, any separate administrative or property entities, and the bank accounts associated with each. Include only entities that actually exist; creating a new structure is a question for advisers, not a prerequisite to seeking capital.

Give counsel the proposed investor's identity, ownership structure, professional credentials where relevant, and intended role. Ask counsel to determine whether the proposed investor and investment are permissible in the relevant jurisdictions and entities.

The legal review should address:

  • Which entity, if any, may accept the proposed investment.
  • Whether the investor may hold the proposed ownership and control rights.
  • Whether existing agreements require approvals or limit transfers.
  • What transaction documents, securities analysis, notices or filings are applicable.
  • Whether payer, lender, lease or other agreements require review for ownership-change provisions.

Do not treat investment in a separate administrative entity as an automatic solution to professional ownership restrictions. Counsel must evaluate the complete arrangement, including governance and financial relationships.

2. Separate Practice Capital From an Owner's Sale Proceeds

Two proposals can offer the same ownership percentage but provide very different funding to the practice.

A new equity contribution generally puts investment proceeds into the entity issuing the ownership interest. A purchase of an existing owner's interest pays the selling owner. A transaction can combine both, but the amounts and recipients must be explicit.

Preparation question Why it matters
Which entity receives new capital? Determines where funds are available and which operations they support.
Does any payment go directly to an existing owner? Distinguishes practice funding from owner liquidity.
Who pays transaction expenses? Affects the cash available after closing.
Is funding delivered at once or in stages? Changes when the practice can commit to hiring, equipment or expansion.
Are any funds restricted to specific uses? May limit management's flexibility after closing.

Build a sources-and-uses schedule showing gross investment proceeds, owner-sale proceeds, estimated transaction costs and net cash available to the practice. Keep uncertain amounts clearly identified as estimates.

3. Assemble Records That Explain the Practice's Economics

Investors and advisers need to understand both reported earnings and cash requirements. A profitable practice can still face pressure when payer receipts lag payroll or when a new location incurs costs before collections stabilize.

Start with reconciled books and identify the accounting basis used. The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. It is useful recordkeeping guidance, not a valuation method or transaction-approval standard.

For a newly organized practice, replace unavailable operating history with opening balances, signed commitments, staffing plans and clearly labeled forecasts. Do not present projected payer receipts as established collections.

Reconcile ownership records against signed documents. Flag undocumented promises, disputed interests and unexplained owner advances rather than treating them as settled facts.

Limit sensitive information in financial diligence

Begin with aggregate financial reports rather than patient-level exports. If additional detail is necessary, have the appropriate advisers establish a suitable sharing process. HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities when protected health information is involved.

Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. A financial diligence request is not, by itself, a reason to disclose patient details.

4. Connect the Capital Request to a Cash Plan

A capital request should explain what the money will accomplish and when it will be spent. Separate one-time expenditures from continuing obligations.

For example, a second location may need build-out spending, equipment deposits, recruitment costs and payroll funding while collections develop. The initial spending budget is only part of the requirement: the practice also needs to assess the cash needed between opening and dependable receipts.

Prepare a monthly cash forecast with:

  • Opening cash and expected funding dates.
  • Payer and patient receipts based on documented assumptions.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, occupancy and other operating payments.
  • Capital expenditures and transaction costs.
  • Debt payments and other existing commitments.
  • Proposed distributions and the minimum operating cash balance management wants to preserve.

Show a base case and a downside case. The downside case might assume slower provider onboarding, delayed receipts or higher opening costs. Explain which commitments management would defer if the investment arrives late or is smaller than requested.

For a multi-entity practice, identify how money would reach the operation that needs it. Ask advisers to review any proposed intercompany funding rather than moving cash between accounts without supporting documentation.

5. Document Valuation Assumptions Before Negotiating Ownership

A valuation discussion needs a defined subject. Is the proposed value for the operating entity, one location or a group of entities? Does it include cash, debt, equipment or real estate? Are all existing owners participating on the same terms?

Prepare a bridge from reported results to the earnings measure being discussed. List each proposed adjustment and its support. Owner compensation deserves particular attention: removing an owner's entire compensation expense can overstate earnings if the practice still needs someone to provide those clinical or management services.

Likewise, identify shared costs that may change after the transaction. A location-level P&L that excludes necessary central administration may not represent the economics of operating that location independently.

Illustrative example—not a valuation recommendation: An investor contributes $300,000 entirely as new capital at an agreed pre-investment equity value of $1.2 million. Assuming no other ownership changes or special economic rights, the post-investment equity value is $1.5 million, and the investor receives 20%: $300,000 divided by $1.5 million.

That calculation does not establish a fair price. It also does not apply unchanged if part of the payment buys an existing owner's interest, the transaction uses a different valuation definition, or the parties negotiate preferential rights. Ask the CPA and counsel to reconcile the ownership calculation to the actual terms.

6. Use a Proposed-Terms Comparison Worksheet

Compare complete proposals, not just valuation. Enter the actual terms under each proposal and mark unanswered items as “Not yet agreed.” Legal permissibility should be a gate, not a scoring category that a higher price can overcome.

7. Resolve Governance, Distribution and Exit Expectations

A minority ownership interest can carry substantial approval rights. Identify the decisions management must make routinely—such as staffing within an approved budget—and distinguish them from major decisions requiring owner approval. Ask counsel to address boundaries around professional and clinical decision-making.

Separate three financial concepts in the discussion:

  • Compensation: payment for clinical, administrative or management work.
  • Distributions: payments associated with ownership under the governing documents.
  • Exit proceeds: amounts allocated when an interest or the business is sold.

A profitable month does not necessarily produce distributable cash. Discuss how payroll, reserves, debt obligations and planned investment affect distributions. Ask the CPA to model the owners' tax and cash-flow implications without assuming distributions will always match tax obligations.

Exit terms deserve attention before the first contribution. Ask advisers to address proposed transfers, retirement, death, disability, loss of required professional eligibility, owner disputes and a sale of the practice. Identify the valuation process, payment terms and funding source for a possible buyout. A buyout obligation is not financially workable merely because it appears in an agreement.

8. Bring Advisers a Focused Question List

Questions for transaction counsel

  • Is the proposed investor eligible to hold the interest in the relevant entity and jurisdictions?
  • Which structure fits the practice, and why would a startup financing instrument be appropriate or inappropriate?
  • What approvals, contract reviews and securities requirements apply?
  • Which preliminary terms are binding?
  • How should governance, deadlocks, future funding and exit provisions work?

Questions for the CPA or financial adviser

  • Are the books reliable enough to support negotiations, and what needs correction?
  • Which earnings adjustments are supported, especially for owner compensation and shared costs?
  • How do the proposed structure and cash destinations affect the practice and existing owners?
  • Does the downside cash forecast support the proposed spending and distribution expectations?
  • How should the contribution and any owner-sale proceeds be recorded after closing?

Prepare the Decision Before Preparing the Documents

Proceed when the practice can explain the permissible investment path, the destination and use of funds, the valuation assumptions, and the rights attached to ownership. Keep an unresolved-items list with an assigned adviser for each question. Do not treat an unsigned assumption as an agreed term.

FAQs

Can a medical practice use a SAFE or convertible note for its first investment?

Do not assume it can or should. Counsel must assess investor eligibility, entity restrictions, potential conversion rights and applicable transaction requirements. A familiar startup document does not resolve medical-practice ownership questions.

What should an owner prepare before requesting a valuation?

Prepare reconciled financial statements, ownership documents, debt and cash balances, owner compensation details, supported earnings adjustments and location or entity reporting where relevant. Define exactly which business interests and assets are being valued.

Does selling an equity interest always fund the practice?

No. A purchase from an existing owner generally directs proceeds to that owner, while newly issued equity can direct capital to the issuing entity. A mixed transaction should show each payment and recipient separately so the operating budget reflects only funds actually available to the practice.
