AI-Assisted Credit Risk Analysis for Medical Practice Borrowers

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Before borrowing for equipment, a new location, or working capital, a private medical practice needs to answer a practical question: Can the practice make the proposed payments without putting payroll and essential operations under pressure?

Reported profit alone cannot answer that question. Payer receipts may arrive unevenly, patient balances may remain uncollected, and an expansion may require spending before it generates cash. Existing loan principal payments and owner distributions also consume cash without appearing as operating expenses on the income statement.

AI-assisted credit risk analysis can help organize the records and assumptions behind a borrowing decision. Its role is to support a traceable financial assessment—not generate a credit score or predict approval. A CPA should verify every input, adjustment, and calculation before the owner relies on the results or shares them with a lender.

This guide focuses on the medical practice as borrower, not patient credit scoring or lending decisions about others.

Start with the borrowing request and repayment source

Define the request before analyzing affordability. An equipment purchase, a location buildout, and a temporary collection gap create different repayment demands.

Write a short borrowing summary covering:

  • Borrower: The legal entity requesting the loan and the entities whose cash flows support the analysis.
  • Purpose: What the funds will purchase or finance.
  • Funding need: Project costs, available owner funding, and a documented contingency allowance.
  • Repayment source: Existing operating cash flow, expected incremental cash flow, or collection of a specific receivable balance.
  • Proposed structure: Amount, term, payment frequency, interest assumptions, and any interest-only period or balloon payment.
  • Timing: When funds are needed and when payments begin.

Ask the lender for its document requirements and calculation definitions early. An internal assessment may use a different definition of cash available for debt service than the lender uses. Do not assume that a favorable internal ratio satisfies the lender’s requirements.

For a new location, separate established operations from expansion assumptions. A consolidated forecast can conceal the cash support that the existing practice must provide while the new location ramps up.

Build a verified borrower file

Use a common reporting cutoff and label each statement’s accounting basis. Cash-basis tax reporting, accrual financial statements, and bank deposits describe different aspects of the business; they are not interchangeable.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices also provide a sound foundation for a borrower’s financial review.

Borrower-readiness worksheet

Use this worksheet with the practice manager and CPA. For each row, record the responsible person, reporting period, completion status, and unresolved differences.

Borrowing purpose Project budget, vendor quotes, funding plan Does the requested amount match documented uses?
Historical performance Monthly income statements, balance sheets, year-end statements, and requested tax returns Are periods comparable, reconciled, and clearly labeled by accounting basis?
Cash and liquidity Bank reconciliations, statements, outstanding checks, restricted balances How much cash is actually available for operations?

A missing record is not a zero balance. Mark unavailable information explicitly and identify which conclusions depend on obtaining it.

Give AI a bounded role—and require CPA verification

Depending on the tool and document quality, AI assistance may help extract statement fields, organize monthly results, draft a debt schedule, or flag inconsistent labels. These are preparation tasks, not proof that the records are correct.

A controlled workflow should include:

  1. Approve the input files. Use final reconciled records where available, keeping drafts separate.
  2. Attach source references. Each extracted figure should identify its document, entity, period, and source location.
  3. Verify every input. The CPA checks extracted amounts against the underlying records, including signs, units, dates, and accounting treatment.
  4. Reconcile totals. Cash ties to reconciled bank balances; debt ties to lender statements; financial statements tie to the accounting records.
  5. Validate calculations. Recompute formulas in a reviewable worksheet rather than accepting an AI-generated narrative.
  6. Approve the final version. Record unresolved items and distinguish historical facts from forecast assumptions.

Common errors include treating transfers as revenue, interpreting parentheses incorrectly, omitting a loan, and mixing monthly expenses with annual debt payments. AI can also produce a plausible explanation for a variance without supporting evidence. Require the practice team to substantiate the explanation.

Keep patient information out of the analysis where possible

Borrower analysis generally needs aggregate financial information, not patient identities. Use summarized collections and receivables reports with unnecessary patient details removed. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool.

If protected health information is involved, assess the workflow and service-provider responsibilities before sharing it. HHS explains covered entities and business associates and provides minimum necessary guidance. These sources do not establish that a particular vendor or workflow is compliant.

Reconcile reported profit to cash available for debt service

A profitable practice can still struggle with debt payments. Accrual revenue may not yet be collected, loan principal is not an income-statement expense, and necessary equipment purchases use cash.

Build a bridge from reported profit to repayment capacity. Define the starting profit measure precisely, then show each adjustment once.

Illustrative annual reconciliation

The following figures are an invented example, not a practice benchmark or lender standard. This example starts with accrual profit before income taxes, after interest expense.

Reconciliation item Illustrative amount Treatment
Reported profit before income taxes $360,000 Starting point; includes interest expense
Add depreciation and amortization $60,000 Noncash expense; necessary replacement spending is addressed below
Add interest expense $40,000 Added back because total principal and interest payments are deducted later
Subtract operating working-capital cash use ($70,000) Net effect of receivables, payables, and other operating balance changes
Subtract necessary capital spending ($55,000) Cash spending not already charged as an expense
Subtract applicable cash income taxes or planned tax distributions ($35,000) CPA determines the appropriate entity/owner treatment; avoid double counting
Internal cash available for debt service $300,000 Defined specifically for this assessment
Existing and proposed principal and interest payments ($180,000) Includes the proposed borrowing
Cash remaining after debt service $120,000 Before other owner distributions and additional reserve funding

For this example, internal debt-service coverage is $300,000 ÷ $180,000 = 1.67 times. That calculation does not establish loan eligibility. Lenders may calculate coverage differently and apply their own requirements.

Owner cash needs still matter. If this illustrative practice plans another $45,000 of discretionary distributions, only $75,000 remains for additional reserves and unexpected costs. Do not describe all post-debt cash as freely available.

If the books use the cash basis, do not apply an accrual working-capital adjustment mechanically. The CPA should adapt the bridge to avoid counting collection timing twice. Similarly, add back a claimed one-time expense only when its nature, amount, and nonrecurrence are supported.

Examine liquidity and monthly cash-flow variability

Annual coverage can hide a payment problem in a particular month. Build a monthly cash schedule showing opening cash, payer and patient receipts, payroll, occupancy, other operating payments, capital spending, taxes, debt service, and distributions.

For a staffed practice, investigate:

  • Collection timing: Does a payer delay move cash into a later month, or indicate a permanent collection shortfall?
  • Payroll timing: Do payroll dates and provider compensation payments create cash pressure before major receipts arrive?
  • Provider availability: How would an absence affect receipts while fixed staffing and occupancy costs continue?
  • Patient balances: Does the collection forecast reflect historical experience rather than assuming all balances convert to cash?
  • Expansion spending: Does the model include recruiting, deposits, and other pre-opening cash needs?

Separate unrestricted cash from amounts unavailable for general operations. Show available credit-line capacity separately from cash, confirm borrowing conditions, and include any assumed draws and repayment in the forecast. Borrowing is not operating income.

For multiple entities, distinguish combined results from the borrowing entity’s repayment capacity. Eliminate intercompany transfers from consolidated revenue and document shared-cost allocations. Do not assume that cash in another entity can support the borrower without confirming the arrangement with the lender and appropriate advisers.

Test downside repayment capacity

A useful stress test asks what happens if a specific assumption fails. It should not simply reduce every line by the same percentage.

The scenarios below are illustrative and extend the annual example above. They hold scheduled debt service constant solely to show the effect of lower cash availability.

Scenario Illustrative assumption Cash available for debt service Debt service Coverage Cash after debt service
Base case Reconciled operating plan $300,000 $180,000 1.67x $120,000
Collection-delay case Net cash shifted beyond the review period totals $90,000 $210,000 $180,000 1.17x $30,000
Combined downside Collection timing and operating disruption reduce cash by $150,000 in total $150,000 $180,000 0.83x ($30,000)

A collection delay may reverse later; a permanent revenue loss may not. Model that distinction explicitly. Do not count the same shortfall as both reduced receipts and an additional receivables adjustment.

For each scenario, document a response: defer nonessential capital spending, reduce discretionary distributions, contribute owner funds, resize the project, or discuss a different financing structure. Confirm whether the response can occur before cash runs short. Longer repayment terms may reduce scheduled payments but can increase total interest cost; compare the full terms rather than payment size alone.

Also test variable-rate exposure, expiring interest-only periods, and balloon payments where relevant. Do not assume refinancing will be available.

Prepare an explainable lender package

The final package should let a reviewer trace the repayment story without relying on an AI-generated score. Include:

  • The borrowing request and sources-and-uses summary.
  • Reconciled historical statements and a complete debt schedule.
  • The profit-to-cash bridge with adjustment support.
  • A monthly forecast, downside cases, and management responses.
  • Clear entity/location schedules where relevant.
  • An assumptions register and a list of unresolved items.

Each material conclusion should identify its source, calculation, assumption, and reviewer. If records remain incomplete, show the limitation and its potential effect rather than presenting a precise but unsupported result.

This internal assessment informs the owner’s decision. The lender independently evaluates creditworthiness, documentation, collateral, guarantees, loan terms, and its own credit policies. Strong preparation does not guarantee approval.

Make the borrowing decision before submitting the application

The purpose of AI-assisted credit risk analysis is not to make the practice look stronger than it is. It is to determine whether the proposed debt fits the practice’s verified cash flow—and what must change if it does not.

FAQs

Can AI determine whether my medical practice will qualify for a loan?

No. AI can assist with organizing documents and preparing analysis, but it cannot establish approval. A CPA should verify every input and calculation, and the lender makes its own decision using its requirements and judgment.

What if our financial records are incomplete?

Identify missing periods and unreconciled balances before modeling repayment capacity. Prioritize bank reconciliations, complete debt balances, payroll obligations, and comparable financial statements. Label estimates and show how uncertainty affects the result. Do not let AI fill gaps with invented figures.

Why might a profitable practice struggle to repay debt?

Profit may include revenue not yet collected. Principal payments, capital purchases, and owner distributions also use cash without necessarily reducing reported operating profit. A profit-to-cash reconciliation and monthly forecast reveal these differences.

Should we include every practice entity in the analysis?

Start with the proposed borrower and identify any other entities whose cash flows are expected to support repayment. Provide entity-level statements and reconciliations where relevant. Combined reporting can be useful, but it should not conceal intercompany transfers or assume that all group cash is available to the borrower.
