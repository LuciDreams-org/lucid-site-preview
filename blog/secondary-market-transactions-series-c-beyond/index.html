Secondary Ownership Sales in Established Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An owner selling part of an established medical practice needs two separate financial pictures: what the seller receives and what happens to the practice’s cash. Confusing those pictures can leave the buyer, remaining owners and practice manager working from different assumptions.

A secondary ownership sale transfers an existing ownership interest from a selling owner to a buyer. In a direct owner-to-buyer transaction, the purchase price goes to the seller—not into the practice’s operating account. The practice does not receive new working capital simply because its ownership changes.

For a staffed private practice, the practical task is to document the interest being transferred, substantiate the financial information behind the price, and keep the transaction separate from payroll, payer receipts and ordinary operating expenses. Transfer eligibility, approvals, healthcare ownership restrictions and tax consequences require review by qualified legal and tax advisers.

This guide focuses on a direct sale of an existing interest, not fundraising, an asset sale or a practice-funded buyout.

Separate Seller Proceeds From Practice Cash

Start with a transaction map identifying the buyer, seller, exact legal entity and interest being sold. A practice’s public-facing name may cover several entities, but an interest in one entity should not be assumed to include interests in the others.

The same distinction applies to money. A purchase payment, an owner distribution, a compensation payment and repayment of an owner loan are different items. Even if they occur near closing, show each separately.

Financial item Who receives or pays it? What to clarify
Purchase price for an existing interest Buyer pays the selling owner What interest and rights does the price cover?
Pre-closing owner distribution Practice pays an owner if appropriately authorized Is it separate from the price, and does the practice retain sufficient cash?
Outstanding owner loan Borrower pays the lender identified in the records Is the balance being repaid, assigned or left outstanding?
Provider compensation Practice pays for services under applicable arrangements Will work duties or compensation change after closing?
Transaction expenses Buyer, seller or practice, as agreed Who bears each expense, and how should it be recorded?

A direct secondary sale does not itself increase the number of outstanding ownership units. The seller’s percentage falls, the buyer’s rises, and other owners’ percentages can remain unchanged. Voting, distribution and management rights still need to be checked against the governing documents.

If the practice itself purchases the interest, the cash-flow analysis is different. Ask advisers to evaluate that separately rather than treating it as the same transaction.

Illustrative Transaction Sources and Uses

Illustrative example—not a valuation recommendation, fee estimate or legal structure: An established practice has 1,000 outstanding ownership units. Owner A holds 600 units and sells 200 existing units to an eligible buyer for an agreed price of $600,000. Owner B retains 400 units. No new units are issued, and the practice does not fund the purchase.

For this example, assume the seller pays $20,000 of transaction expenses from the purchase proceeds and the buyer pays $15,000 of separate advisory expenses.

Closing sources Illustrative amount
Buyer funds for purchase price $600,000
Buyer funds for buyer-side expenses $15,000
Practice operating cash $0
Total sources $615,000
Closing uses Illustrative amount
Cash to seller after seller-side expenses, before taxes $580,000
Seller-side transaction expenses $20,000
Buyer-side transaction expenses $15,000
New cash received by the practice $0
Total uses $615,000

The seller’s $580,000 is not an after-tax proceeds estimate. Taxes, any holdback, installment payments or other obligations would need separate analysis. This example assumes none of those additional closing items.

The practice’s operating cash is unchanged by the illustrated closing transfers. That does not mean every ownership sale is cash-neutral for the practice: practice-paid expenses, distributions or debt repayments could change the result.

Before-and-after ownership record

Illustrative ownership record for the same example:

Owner Units before Ownership before Units after Ownership after
Owner A, seller 600 60% 400 40%
Owner B, continuing owner 400 40% 400 40%
Buyer 0 0% 200 20%
Total 1,000 100% 1,000 100%

The agreed price implies a $3 million total equity value by simple proportional arithmetic. It does not independently establish the practice’s value or prove that other ownership blocks should trade at the same price. Rights, restrictions and transaction terms need separate evaluation.

Maintain this ownership record alongside—not as a substitute for—the legal transfer documents and accounting records. Have counsel confirm the ownership rights and the CPA determine any related accounting treatment.

Build a Diligence Package Around the Interest Being Sold

The buyer needs information about the entity being acquired, not just a combined report for everything the owners operate. Assign one person to coordinate requests and identify the preparer, reporting period and unresolved questions for each schedule.

Diligence request Records to gather Financial question it helps answer
Governing agreements Current governing documents, amendments, buy-sell provisions and ownership register What exactly is owned, and which transfer provisions need counsel’s review?
Debt restrictions Loan agreements, credit facilities, guarantees, security documents and current balances Could the transfer require lender consent or affect obligations?
Historical distributions Distribution ledger by owner and date, supporting authorizations and bank activity Were payments distributions, compensation, reimbursements or loan repayments?
Financial statements Historical and current balance sheets, P&Ls, general ledger and reconciliations Do the statements reconcile, and what reporting basis is used?
Revenue and receivables Aggregate payer receipts, patient-balance aging, adjustments and collection trends How much reported revenue has converted to cash?
Payroll and owner services Provider and administrative payroll summaries, owner compensation and work responsibilities What labor costs support current earnings?
Valuation inputs Proposed earnings adjustments, debt, cash, capital spending and related-party arrangements Which assumptions support the price?
Locations and related entities Entity-level and location-level reports, intercompany balances and allocation methods Which earnings and obligations belong to the interest being sold?

Use complete records rather than reconstructed totals where possible. IRS Publication 583 provides general guidance on business recordkeeping, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. It is not guidance on how to structure an ownership sale.

Explain earnings adjustments rather than just presenting them

A buyer should be able to trace each proposed adjustment from reported earnings to the supporting record. Label the adjustment, explain the rationale and identify whether it is expected to recur.

Owner compensation deserves particular attention. If the seller continues seeing patients or managing staff, those services still have a cost. Removing all seller compensation from expenses without considering continuing duties can overstate the earnings available to owners.

Similarly, examine related-party rent, unusual professional fees, deferred equipment purchases and temporary staffing costs individually. Do not assume that every unusual expense should be added back or that every proposed adjustment will be accepted.

Distinguish collections from receivables

Payer receipts and patient balances can create timing differences between reported earnings and available cash. State whether the financial statements use cash or accrual accounting and explain how receivables appear in the analysis.

Clarify how the transaction documents address cash, receivables and liabilities around closing. Avoid counting the same economic item in both the ownership price and a separate settlement payment without an explicit explanation.

Make shared costs visible

For multi-location or multi-entity practices, provide an allocation schedule for shared administration, billing support, rent and other common costs. Show whether allocations are based on staffing, usage, space or another documented method.

If the allocation method changed, explain the effect on reported results. A location P&L is less useful for pricing discussions when its apparent improvement comes from moving expenses elsewhere.

Resolve Adviser Questions Before Treating the Sale as Ready

Financial preparation cannot establish whether a proposed transfer is permitted. Give qualified healthcare transaction counsel and the CPA the same transaction map and proposed terms early.

Questions for legal counsel

  • Is the proposed buyer eligible to hold this interest under the applicable state’s healthcare ownership restrictions and the entity’s governing documents?
  • Which owner, board, lender or other approvals are needed?
  • Do rights of first refusal, purchase options, notice provisions or other transfer restrictions apply?
  • Could payer agreements, leases or other contracts require notices or consents?
  • What happens to the seller’s guarantees, management authority and other continuing obligations?
  • Which documents establish the transfer’s effective date and the buyer’s economic and voting rights?

Do not substitute a generic approval timetable for counsel’s review of the actual documents.

Questions for the CPA and tax advisers

  • How do the entity’s tax classification, seller’s basis and transaction terms affect the tax analysis?
  • Which components of the seller’s proceeds need separate treatment?
  • How should transaction expenses, owner loans and pre-closing distributions be handled?
  • What reporting, elections or ownership-related accounting adjustments, if any, require consideration?
  • How should income and distributions around the effective date be addressed?

Request a seller-proceeds estimate that clearly separates gross price, expenses, other settlement items and estimated taxes. Do not assume that the full purchase price is taxable gain or that the entire gain receives one tax treatment.

Protect Operating Liquidity Through Closing

Because the purchase price goes to the seller, it cannot also fund the practice’s next payroll or vendor payments. Prepare a separate operating cash forecast using expected payer receipts, patient collections, provider and administrative payroll, rent, debt service and other scheduled payments.

If a pre-closing distribution is proposed, model it explicitly. Test whether the practice can meet its obligations if receipts arrive later than expected. Keep this operating forecast distinct from the buyer’s purchase financing and the seller’s personal proceeds.

Before closing, reconcile three records:

  1. The ownership schedule: The interests transferred agree with the approved transaction documents.
  2. The sources-and-uses schedule: Every payment has a source, recipient and stated purpose.
  3. The practice ledger: Practice-paid items are supported, and seller proceeds are not mistaken for practice revenue or operating cash.

After closing, update ownership records and review distribution instructions, authorized access and reporting responsibilities as appropriate. An ownership change should not automatically be treated as a payroll change; employment duties and compensation need their own review.

Share Financial Information Without Unnecessary Patient Detail

Start diligence with aggregate financial reports and de-identified schedules where feasible. Patient-level clinical information is generally unnecessary to explain payroll costs, location profitability or a sources-and-uses schedule.

If a request could involve protected health information, have the practice’s privacy adviser determine the appropriate process, agreements and safeguards. HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions. Do not assume an ordinary messaging channel or general-purpose AI tool is suitable for patient-identifiable information.

FAQs

Does selling an ownership interest give the medical practice more cash?

Not in a direct sale where the buyer pays the selling owner. The seller receives the purchase proceeds. Separately identify any practice-paid expenses, distributions or other transactions that affect operating cash.

Does a secondary sale dilute the other owners?

A transfer of existing units, without issuing or canceling units, can leave the other owners’ percentages unchanged. The seller transfers part or all of an existing interest to the buyer. Counsel should confirm the associated voting, management and distribution rights.

Which financial records should be ready before price discussions?

Prepare reconciled financial statements, an ownership schedule, debt and owner-loan balances, historical distributions, compensation records and documented valuation adjustments. For multiple locations or entities, include shared-cost allocations and intercompany balances.

Can any buyer purchase an interest in a private medical practice?

Do not assume so. Ask qualified healthcare counsel to evaluate buyer eligibility, applicable state ownership restrictions, governing agreements and required approvals before relying on a proposed transfer.

How much will the selling owner owe in taxes?

The purchase price alone does not answer that question. Ask the seller’s CPA to evaluate basis, entity tax classification, transaction terms, expenses and other relevant facts. Keep estimated taxes separate from the closing payment schedule so gross proceeds are not confused with after-tax cash.
