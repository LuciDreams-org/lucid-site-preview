Gross Margin Benchmarks for Medical Practices: What Transfers From SaaS?

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A quoted gross-margin benchmark is useful only when its revenue definition and included costs match yours. For a staffed private medical practice, a software-company benchmark usually fails that test.

The transferable idea is the calculation: measure how much revenue remains after the costs of delivering the service. The percentage itself does not transfer. A practice must account for clinical labor, supplies, and the resources used to deliver care—not just software hosting or customer support.

Before using a benchmark to evaluate a location or service line, define a consistent direct-service margin, compare your own operations on that basis, and then assess whether an external reference is genuinely comparable. This article provides a worksheet and review process for making that decision.

What transfers from SaaS—and what does not?

Gross margin generally expresses revenue less cost of revenue as a percentage of revenue. But businesses do not necessarily include the same expenses in cost of revenue. A familiar metric name can conceal very different calculations.

Software delivery can serve additional customers without adding a corresponding amount of hands-on labor. A medical practice delivers services through staffed schedules, clinical space, supplies, and provider time. Additional volume may use available capacity initially, but it can also require another clinician, support shift, or room.

Subscription economics also differ from payer and patient revenue. A recurring software contract is not a substitute for understanding a practice's recognized service revenue, contractual adjustments, and collection timing.

Concept What transfers to a practice What does not transfer cleanly
Margin formula Compare defined revenue with defined delivery costs A SaaS percentage as a practice performance target
Cost consistency Apply the same classifications across periods and locations Assuming another industry includes comparable labor costs
Capacity utilization Examine whether existing resources support delivered services Assuming additional revenue requires little additional labor
Revenue mix Separate services with materially different cost structures Software subscription and usage-based benchmark categories
Trend analysis Investigate changes using a stable calculation Treating every increase as an operational improvement

A higher reported margin could reflect better resource use. It could also reflect moving clinical payroll out of direct costs. The second change improves the presentation, not the economics.

Define a practice-specific direct-service margin

For internal management reporting, use a clearly labeled measure:

Direct-service margin = (Net recognized service revenue − Defined direct-service costs) ÷ Net recognized service revenue × 100

This is a management measure, not a claim that every medical practice should present its financial statements this way. Have your CPA align the supporting schedule with your books and explain differences from any reported gross-profit line.

Start with net recognized service revenue

Use revenue recognized for the services and reporting period under your established accounting policy, reflecting applicable contractual and other revenue adjustments. Do not use gross billed charges as though the full amount were collectible revenue.

Payer receipts and patient payments are important reconciliation inputs, but cash arriving this month may relate to services delivered earlier. Similarly, an outstanding patient balance is not automatically additional revenue; it may already be included in recognized revenue.

If your books are maintained on a cash basis, do not quietly label collections as accrual-based recognized revenue. Ask your CPA whether a supported management adjustment is appropriate. Otherwise, label the cash-based measure accurately and avoid comparing it directly with an accrual-based benchmark.

Define direct-service costs in writing

For this article's management measure, direct-service costs include clinical labor and supplies attributable to delivering the reported services. Your policy should address:

  • Employed clinicians: Include compensation and the associated employer payroll taxes and benefits attributable to clinical work.
  • Contract clinicians: Include the cost of clinical services for the corresponding period, without also counting the same work through another payroll allocation.
  • Clinical support staff: Include attributable medical assistant, nursing, technician, and other direct-care support labor.
  • Clinical supplies and purchased services: Include supplies consumed and outside services attributable to the service line. Review material inventory timing differences rather than assuming every purchase was consumed immediately.
  • Mixed-role staff: Split clinical and administrative duties using a documented, reasonable method.

Direct does not mean variable. A salaried clinician's compensation can be a direct-service cost even when it does not change with each additional visit.

Keep shared expenses visible

Central scheduling, billing administration, bookkeeping, general management, and other shared administrative functions should remain outside this defined direct-service margin. They still belong in the broader location or entity P&L.

For occupancy and shared clinical equipment, document your reporting convention explicitly. You might show them in a separate location-cost layer beneath direct-service margin. If another report includes them in cost of revenue, reconcile that difference before comparing percentages.

The objective is not to hide overhead. It is to distinguish service-delivery economics from the separate question of whether a location covers all its costs.

Classification worksheet: how labor changes apparent margin

The following is an illustrative example, not a benchmark or an actual practice result. It uses the same monthly revenue and spending under two reporting approaches.

The inconsistent report includes provider wages but leaves associated payroll costs and shared clinical support below the margin line. The consistent report includes all defined direct clinical labor.

Worksheet item Supporting allocation Inconsistent report: included in direct costs Consistent report: included in direct costs
Net recognized service revenue Revenue schedule reconciled to books $200,000 revenue $200,000 revenue
Employed provider clinical wages Payroll by clinical assignment $80,000 $80,000
Employer payroll taxes and benefits attributable to providers Payroll and benefit schedule $0 $16,000
Shared clinical support labor, including associated employer costs Documented time allocation $0 $12,000
Clinical supplies consumed and attributable outside services Purchasing and usage records $22,000 $22,000
Total direct-service costs Sum of included costs $102,000 $130,000
Direct-service margin dollars Revenue less direct-service costs $98,000 $70,000
Direct-service margin percentage Margin dollars divided by revenue 49% 35%

The apparent difference is 14 percentage points, entirely from classification. No revenue increased and no spending decreased. The omitted costs still exist elsewhere in the P&L.

This is why a location reporting a higher margin is not necessarily more efficient. First check whether both locations include the same categories of labor and employer costs.

Add these fields to your working worksheet

For each cost account, record the responsible entity, location or service line, direct versus shared treatment, allocation driver, supporting record, and reviewer. Include the policy's effective period so changes can be traced and prior comparisons can be restated where practical.

If provider time spans locations, use documented assignments or representative time records. Revenue share alone may misallocate labor when reimbursement differs between sites.

Handle owner labor and shared costs consistently

Owner compensation can make comparisons especially misleading. One practice may pay an owner through payroll for clinical work, while another's financial statements may show distributions without a comparable clinical compensation expense.

Do not treat distributions as payroll or assume that missing compensation means owner-delivered services have no economic labor cost. For management comparisons, show a separate, clearly labeled owner-clinical-labor adjustment when needed. Document its basis with your CPA and avoid adding it again where equivalent compensation is already included.

For other shared resources, choose a driver that reflects use:

Shared resource Potential allocation basis Check before using it
Clinician working at several locations Documented clinical sessions or hours Does the allocation follow actual assignments?
Clinical support team serving multiple service lines Supported time allocation Are clinical and administrative duties separated?
Supplies drawn from a common stockroom Recorded consumption or supported usage estimate Are purchases and transfers being counted twice?
Shared clinical equipment Documented usage in a separate cost layer Is equipment treatment consistent across reports?
Central administrative team Relevant workload measure below direct-service margin Is administrative cost being confused with clinical delivery cost?

For multi-entity practices, distinguish an allocation from an intercompany charge. Reconcile entity-level records, and prevent the same underlying cost from being counted twice in consolidated management reporting. Keep intercompany revenue from inflating consolidated service revenue.

Compare internally before looking outside

Your own comparable periods and locations are usually the most useful starting point because you can inspect the underlying records.

Start with one service line over time using a consistent revenue basis and classification policy. Then compare that service line across locations. A whole-practice comparison can conceal differences in provider mix, supply intensity, and reimbursement.

When a gap appears, review these questions in order:

  1. Is the reporting basis consistent? Check revenue timing, payroll coverage, supplies, and classification changes.
  2. Is the service mix comparable? A location delivering more supply-intensive services may have a different direct-service margin without being poorly managed.
  3. Is reimbursement different? Separate changes in net revenue per comparable service from changes in labor or supply costs.
  4. Is staffing aligned with delivered activity? Review provider and support coverage, paid clinical time, and available capacity without treating this financial measure as a staffing-safety rule.
  5. Are unusual items distorting the period? Identify temporary coverage, delayed invoices, or prior-period adjustments rather than silently removing them.

Use the findings to select a specific review: labor allocation, purchasing, schedule coverage, or revenue adjustments. Do not make a service-line closure or staffing decision from the margin percentage alone.

Require evidence before adopting an external benchmark

No external medical-practice margin range is presented here because a useful comparison requires matching revenue and cost definitions. A SaaS range cannot fill that evidence gap.

Before adopting a quoted practice benchmark, request the original source and its methodology—not just a chart copied into a presentation.

Comparability test Evidence to request Why it matters
Source quality Identifiable publisher, reporting period, sample and methodology An unexplained average cannot support a sound comparison
Revenue definition Charges, collections, or net recognized revenue Different denominators produce different margins
Labor treatment Provider, support staff, employer costs, and owner labor definitions Labor exclusions can create an artificial advantage
Expense scope Supplies, purchased services, occupancy, and equipment treatment The same metric label may cover different costs
Practice characteristics Specialty, service mix, staffing model, and organizational scope Delivery economics differ across practices
Calculation method Aggregate margin, average, median, and any weighting Summary statistics can describe different things

Classify the benchmark as comparable, adjustable, or not comparable. An adjustable benchmark needs a documented reconciliation of the differences. If the source does not disclose enough detail to reconcile it, do not use it as a performance target.

Build a repeatable margin review

At each reporting close, reconcile revenue, payroll, and supply schedules to the books. Confirm allocation methods, explain material changes, and report both margin dollars and percentage. Keep the full location P&L alongside the direct-service schedule so excluded shared expenses remain visible.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices support reliable inputs; they do not establish a medical-practice margin benchmark.

Use aggregated financial data where possible. Margin analysis generally does not need patient names or clinical details. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; confirm appropriate handling for your workflow rather than sending patient-identifiable information through ordinary messaging or general-purpose AI tools.

Conclusion

What transfers from SaaS is the discipline of defining delivery costs and comparing like with like—not a target percentage.

For your practice, establish net recognized service revenue, include consistently classified clinical labor and supplies, and show shared expenses separately. Compare internal results first. Use an external benchmark only when credible source documentation lets you reconcile its definition to yours.

FAQs

What is a good gross margin for a medical practice?

There is no defensible universal percentage without specifying the service mix, revenue basis, and included costs. Start with a consistently defined direct-service margin and comparable internal results. An external target needs credible methodology and matching labor treatment, including owner clinical work.

Should clinical payroll be included in direct-service margin?

Under the management definition used here, yes. Include attributable provider and clinical support compensation, associated employer payroll taxes and benefits, and contract clinical labor. Allocate mixed clinical and administrative roles consistently. A different report may use another convention, so reconcile its definition before comparing results.

Can a high direct-service margin mean a location is profitable?

Not by itself. Direct-service margin excludes the shared administrative and other costs specified in your policy. Review the complete location P&L before concluding that the location covers all its costs. A high percentage can also reflect excluded labor rather than stronger service economics.

What should I do when a benchmark does not disclose its cost definitions?

Request the original methodology. If you cannot determine how revenue, clinical labor, owner compensation, and shared expenses were treated, classify it as not comparable. Do not use it to rank locations or set service-line targets.
