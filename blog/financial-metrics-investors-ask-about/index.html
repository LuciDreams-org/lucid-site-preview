Financial Metrics Investors Ask Medical Practice Owners About

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

When a prospective or existing investor reviews a private medical practice, the central question is not simply whether revenue increased. It is whether reported earnings are repeatable, collections support those earnings, and the practice can meet its obligations without relying on unexplained owner funding.

For owners and managers of staffed US practices, preparation means connecting financial measures to underlying records. A persuasive answer includes a calculation, supporting evidence and an explanation of its limitations—not a universal industry target.

This guide focuses on the questions behind the numbers: earnings quality, cash conversion, revenue concentration, staff costs, debt commitments and location-level performance.

Establish the reporting basis before discussing results

Start with a short reporting memo that defines:

  • Scope: Which legal entities and locations are included, and which are excluded?
  • Period: The latest closed month, year-to-date results and comparable prior periods.
  • Accounting basis: Cash, accrual or another clearly described management-reporting basis.
  • Revenue definition: Patient-service revenue, payer and patient collections, and any separately reported nonpatient income.
  • Adjustments: Whether results are recorded figures or include proposed normalization entries.
  • Status: Which accounts are reconciled and which estimates or differences remain unresolved.

Do not compare cash-basis earnings in one period with accrual-basis earnings in another without explaining the effect. Likewise, gross charges, net patient-service revenue and cash receipts are not interchangeable.

Reconciled records are the foundation. IRS Publication 583 discusses complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciling bank records. Those practices also make investor questions easier to answer.

Build an investor question-and-evidence matrix

Use a matrix to assign responsibility and identify gaps before sharing a reporting package. Each calculation should lead back to a saved source report or schedule.

Explain earnings quality, not just adjusted EBITDA

An earnings-quality question tests whether current profits reflect ordinary operations and whether those profits could continue under the proposed ownership arrangements.

A common starting calculation is:

EBITDA = net income + interest expense + income tax expense + depreciation + amortization

Only add back amounts included in the starting net income figure. “Taxes” here means income tax expense, not payroll taxes or every account containing the word tax. Some practice entities may not record entity-level income tax expense; have the CPA confirm the treatment.

EBITDA is not cash flow. It excludes capital spending, debt principal payments and working-capital changes. It also does not automatically remove unusual expenses.

Support proposed adjustments individually

Keep reported EBITDA and adjusted EBITDA visibly separate. For each proposed adjustment, show the ledger account, amount, source document, reason and expected future treatment.

Owner compensation needs particular care. An owner who provides patient care or management work may need to be replaced or retained. Adding back all compensation without recognizing the cost of that work overstates sustainable earnings. Owner distributions generally are not P&L expenses and should not be added back as though they reduced net income.

Illustrative example: A practice reports EBITDA of $420,000. Management proposes adding back $30,000 of documented, nonrecurring office-relocation expense. It also proposes a $60,000 owner-compensation adjustment. If supporting analysis indicates that replacing the owner's work would cost $45,000, the proposed net compensation adjustment is $15,000—not $60,000. Proposed adjusted EBITDA would be $465,000, subject to investor review.

Do not automatically exclude recurring recruiting, temporary staffing or equipment maintenance merely because management hopes those costs will decline. Also review unusual gains and income: normalization can reduce earnings as well as increase them.

Show how earnings convert into cash

Cash conversion tests whether the practice can fund payroll, suppliers and other operating needs from its activity rather than new borrowing or owner contributions.

For accrual reporting, prepare a bridge from net income to operating cash flow. Identify noncash expenses and changes in operating receivables, payables, accrued payroll and other working-capital accounts. Separately show equipment purchases, debt movements, owner funding and distributions.

A possible summary measure is:

Operating cash conversion = operating cash flow ÷ EBITDA

Define both figures and use matching periods. This ratio becomes difficult to interpret when EBITDA is negative or near zero; the dollar bridge is often more useful. There is no universal conversion target for all practices.

For cash-basis books, explain collection timing directly. A strong month may include receipts for services delivered much earlier, while delayed supplier payments can temporarily improve apparent cash results. A billing receivables report can help explain timing even when receivables are not recorded on the accounting balance sheet.

Qualify receivables measures

Separate payer receivables from patient balances, and state whether aging runs from service date, claim date or another system date. Present older balances alongside subsequent collections and documented adjustments rather than assuming every balance will become cash.

If reporting days in accounts receivable, a common calculation is:

Days in A/R = ending net patient receivables ÷ average daily net patient-service revenue

Use revenue and receivables measured on compatible bases. Disclose the revenue period and allowance method. Do not divide gross-charge receivables by net revenue without explaining and correcting the mismatch.

Explain revenue concentration and dependence

Concentration analysis tests exposure to changes in payer arrangements, provider availability or a particular location—not simply whether the largest revenue source is large.

Calculate payer concentration as:

Payer share = revenue attributable to the payer ÷ total revenue on the same basis

If the available report measures collections, call it a collection share instead. Explain whether related payer products are grouped together and keep patient responsibility distinct from insurer payments to avoid double counting.

Illustrative example: One payer group accounts for $900,000 of $3 million in annual patient collections, or 30%. That establishes concentration; it does not establish that the arrangement is secure or that 30% is unacceptable. Supporting discussion should cover relevant agreement terms, recent payment patterns and known changes.

Analyze provider dependence separately. Explain whether the report attributes activity to the rendering provider, billing provider or another field. Collections attributed to a provider may relate to earlier services, so they are not automatically a measure of current production.

Where relevant, discuss referral-source dependence using aggregate information. Do not turn concentration analysis into patient-specific disclosure or assumptions about future clinical demand.

Relate staffing costs to the work the practice supports

Staff-cost questions test whether earnings depend on temporary vacancies, unpaid owner work or staffing arrangements that cannot continue.

Start with a defined calculation:

Staff cost ratio = included staffing costs ÷ same-period net patient-service revenue

For cash-basis reporting, a separately labeled staffing-cost-to-collections measure may be more consistent. Do not switch denominators between periods without explanation.

Break the numerator into useful groups:

  • Provider compensation, including relevant contractor costs.
  • Clinical-support wages and associated employer costs.
  • Administrative wages and associated employer costs.
  • Temporary staffing, overtime, recruiting and bonuses where material.

State whether benefits, employer payroll taxes and accrued bonuses are included. Payroll cash withdrawals alone do not establish payroll expense; withholding, employer costs and payment timing can create differences.

Result that needs explanation Supporting analysis
Staff ratio falls while vacancies remain open Vacant roles, temporary coverage and expected replacement costs
Provider costs rise before collections Start dates, compensation commitments and collection timing
Administrative costs move between locations Assignment records and a consistent shared-cost method
Owner compensation falls Changes in duties, compensation treatment and any replacement requirement

Avoid treating the lowest staffing ratio as inherently best. Specialty, service mix, hours, staffing model and outsourced work affect comparisons. The useful answer explains what changed and whether the current cost structure is sustainable.

Separate debt service from operating profitability

Debt questions test whether the practice can meet contractual payments after allowing for operating needs. Supply a schedule by legal borrower, not just a consolidated debt total.

Include outstanding principal, interest terms, scheduled payments, maturity dates and material collateral, guarantee or covenant provisions identified in the agreements. List leases and other significant commitments separately so that cash requirements are not missed or double counted.

Principal repayment generally reduces debt rather than P&L earnings. Interest expense can differ from cash interest paid because of timing. Reconcile both to the relevant statements and ledger accounts.

If an investor requests debt-service coverage, agree on the definition first:

Debt-service coverage = defined cash available for debt service ÷ defined debt service

A lender's agreement may prescribe its own calculation. Do not substitute EBITDA for cash available without explaining exclusions such as working-capital needs, taxes or necessary capital spending. Identify any balloon payment or refinancing assumption separately; an annual average can hide a concentrated cash requirement.

Make location results tie to entity results

Location analysis tests whether growth is broad-based or whether profitable sites support weaker ones. For multi-entity owners, it also shows where earnings, cash and obligations actually reside.

Present location economics in layers:

Location direct contribution = location revenue − directly attributable operating costs

Location result after shared costs = direct contribution − allocated central costs

Define the costs included at each layer. Direct contribution is not a standardized measure and should not be presented as equivalent to EBITDA.

Allocate rent directly where possible. Assign provider and staff costs using supportable work-location information. Allocate shared administration using a documented basis that reasonably reflects use, such as staff time or transaction volume. Revenue-based allocation may be practical, but it can burden higher-revenue sites without reflecting actual support needs.

Show the allocation method and any changes. Keep genuinely central costs visible. Reconcile locations plus central activity to each entity's P&L, then reconcile combined results after applicable intercompany eliminations. A location's profit does not mean its cash is freely available to pay another entity's debt.

Assemble a package that can withstand follow-up questions

Before release, have the owner, practice manager and CPA review one consistent package:

  • Closed financial statements and a reporting-basis memo.
  • Supporting schedules indexed to the question-and-evidence matrix.
  • Separate reported and adjusted results.
  • A log of unresolved differences, assumptions and responsible reviewers.
  • A concise explanation of material period-to-period changes.

Use aggregated financial information wherever possible. Accounting work can involve protected health information and business-associate responsibilities, as described in the HHS HIPAA overview. Apply appropriate access and sharing procedures; do not place patient-identifiable reports in ordinary Slack or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

What if our medical practice uses cash-basis bookkeeping?

Label the basis clearly and reconcile collections to bank activity. Supplement the P&L with billing receivables, unpaid obligations and collection-timing analysis. If investors request accrual results, work with the CPA on a documented conversion rather than relabeling cash-basis figures.

Can all owner compensation be added back to earnings?

No. Separate compensation for actual provider or management work from distributions and other payments. Any proposed normalization should recognize the cost of continuing that work and be supported by records. Show the adjustment separately from reported earnings.

What should we do when billing reports do not match the financial statements?

Identify differences in scope, dates, accounting basis, refunds, adjustments, payment processing and posting timing. Create a reconciliation schedule and quantify unresolved amounts. Do not force reports to agree through an unexplained adjustment or describe unreconciled results as final.
