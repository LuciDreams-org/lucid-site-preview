Simulating Profitability Scenarios for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Extending office hours can increase revenue without improving profit. Additional appointments may require a full coverage shift, more administrative support and cash for payroll before payer receipts arrive. Keeping current hours has trade-offs, too: unused rooms or unmet appointment demand may represent capacity the practice could use profitably.

A medical practice profitability simulation compares specific operating configurations before you commit. Instead of asking only whether revenue will grow, it asks: What changes in collected revenue, expenses, capacity and cash if we operate differently?

For an established private practice, useful alternatives include maintaining current hours, adding evening sessions, changing provider coverage or adding administrative capacity. The goal is to compare feasible choices—not to produce a single break-even number or a precise-looking long-range forecast.

Define the Operating Decision Before Building the Model

Write down the alternatives in operational terms. “Grow revenue” is not a configuration. “Add evening sessions using paid provider and support coverage” is.

For each option, document:

  • Which location and entity will earn the revenue and pay the expenses.
  • Available appointment slots and expected completed visits.
  • Required provider, clinical-support and administrative coverage.
  • Which costs remain committed even if appointments go unfilled.
  • Any setup spending, minimum shifts or cancellation commitments.

Compare the options over the same representative operating period. Keep assumptions that do not change—such as existing rent—consistent across alternatives. Show both total practice profit and the incremental difference so that shared overhead does not hide the economics of the decision.

Build the Model Around Practice-Level Drivers

Use separate sections for assumptions, calculations and results. Each important assumption should have a source, such as reconciled financial statements, aggregate billing reports, payroll schedules or a vendor quote.

Driver Practical modeling input Important check
Completed visits Available slots × expected completion rate Account for cancellations, no-shows and displaced daytime visits
Collectible revenue per visit Expected payer and patient receipts attributable to completed services Use expected realizable amounts, not gross charges
Visit-dependent costs Supplies, processing costs or other expenses that vary with activity Avoid counting costs already included in payroll or another expense line
Staffing commitments Paid coverage hours × actual loaded hourly cost, or committed compensation Include applicable employer costs, benefits and coverage terms
Administrative capacity Scheduling, check-in, payment posting and follow-up workload Determine whether existing staff can absorb the work
Fixed overhead Rent, insurance and other continuing commitments Separate unchanged overhead from new recurring costs
Cash timing Expected receipt timing and payment dates Keep service-period economics separate from bank activity

Estimate Revenue From Completed Services, Not Booked Slots

Start with appointments the practice can realistically complete. Then apply expected collectible revenue based on the relevant service and payer mix.

Expected collectible revenue = completed visits × expected collectible revenue per visit

A single average may be sufficient when the mix is stable. Use separate categories when extended hours could attract a materially different mix. Include patient balances only to the extent you reasonably expect to collect them; billed balances are not equivalent to receipts.

Also distinguish genuinely additional visits from visits moved out of existing sessions. Moving an appointment to the evening does not necessarily add revenue, even though it adds evening staffing costs.

Separate Variable Costs, Commitments and Step Costs

Some expenses rise with every completed visit. Others are committed once a session is staffed, regardless of attendance.

A step cost appears when activity exceeds a capacity threshold. For example, existing administrative staff might support the initial expansion, but a larger schedule could require another paid coverage block. Do not spread that extra block smoothly across all visits if the practice must purchase it as a unit.

Use the actual coverage arrangement. Salaried capacity is not automatically free: additional work may require overtime, replacement coverage or a different compensation agreement. Include compensation for an owner's added clinical work when evaluating the economic value of the option, and distinguish that compensation from owner distributions.

Reconcile Collections-Based Inputs to the Reporting Basis

Historical bank receipts are useful, but this month's deposits may relate to services delivered in earlier periods. They should not automatically become this month's service revenue assumption.

Build two connected views:

  1. Operating economics: Expected collectible revenue attributable to services in the modeled period, less the expenses required to deliver them.
  2. Cash consequences: Receipts expected to reach the bank during that period, less payments actually due.

If the practice reports on a cash basis, its reported profit will reflect collection timing. An analysis that matches services with expected receipts is a separate management view and should be labeled accordingly.

If the practice reports on an accrual basis, reconcile the model to the accounting team's recognized net revenue and receivables methodology. Expected collectible revenue is a planning input, not an instruction to change accounting entries.

Before using historical figures, reconcile bank receipts to the books and investigate material differences. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those controls help establish a dependable starting point; they do not validate the scenario's assumptions.

Worked Example: Current Hours Versus Extended Hours

All figures below are hypothetical and illustrative, not medical-practice benchmarks. The example compares two configurations for a representative month at one established location.

The practice currently completes 1,000 visits monthly. The extended-hours option adds paid provider and support coverage, with capacity for up to 160 additional completed visits. Management's working assumption is 120 additional visits, with no displacement of daytime appointments.

The example uses expected collectible revenue attributable to those services. Its operating result is before depreciation, interest and income taxes. Existing provider compensation, including compensation for owner clinical work, is included in baseline payroll. Owner distributions are excluded.

Monthly driver or result Maintain current hours Extend hours
Completed visits 1,000 1,120
Expected collectible revenue per visit $150 $150
Expected collectible revenue $150,000 $168,000
Visit-dependent expense at $20 per visit $20,000 $22,400
Existing provider and staff payroll $80,000 $80,000
Additional provider and support coverage $0 $12,000
Additional administrative coverage $0 $1,500
Existing fixed overhead $30,000 $30,000
Additional recurring overhead $0 $500
Total modeled operating expenses $130,000 $146,400
Modeled operating profit $20,000 $21,600
Profit difference versus current hours — +$1,600

The extension produces $18,000 of additional expected revenue, but only $1,600 of additional operating profit:

$18,000 − $2,400 − $12,000 − $1,500 − $500 = $1,600

That is a much narrower result than the revenue increase alone suggests. The owner should decide whether the incremental return justifies the coverage commitment, scheduling complexity and cash exposure.

Test Demand Without Pretending Coverage Costs Disappear

Keep committed coverage costs in the model when testing lower attendance. Otherwise, the downside case will look artificially forgiving.

The following sensitivity cases are also illustrative. Each assumes the same revenue per visit, variable expense and committed extended-hours coverage.

Extended-hours outcome Additional completed visits Additional expected revenue Incremental operating profit Total practice operating profit
Lower attendance 60 $9,000 −$6,200 $13,800
Working assumption 120 $18,000 +$1,600 $21,600
Current coverage capacity 160 $24,000 +$6,800 $26,800

These are alternative assumptions, not statistically established probabilities. The lower-attendance case is not a worst-case guarantee.

Make Capacity Limits Explicit

In this example, the current extended-hours coverage supports no more than 160 additional completed visits. The model should flag higher volume as infeasible unless the operating configuration changes.

A proposal for more visits would need a new scenario with the required provider, room, support and administrative capacity. It might trigger another paid shift or exceed available room hours. Revenue should not continue rising past the capacity limit while costs remain frozen.

Show the Cash Consequences Separately

An option can improve service-period economics while reducing near-term cash.

For this illustrative first-month cash comparison, assume:

  • Baseline cash receipts are $150,000 and baseline cash operating payments are $130,000.
  • Only 60% of the extension's $18,000 expected collectible revenue arrives during the first month.
  • All $16,400 of incremental recurring operating costs are paid that month.
  • The extension also requires a $3,000 equipment purchase, treated as a capital outlay rather than an operating expense in this simplified example.
  • There are no other cash movements in the comparison.
First-month cash item Maintain current hours Extend hours
Baseline net operating cash inflow $20,000 $20,000
Receipts from additional services $0 $10,800
Incremental recurring cash payments $0 −$16,400
Equipment purchase $0 −$3,000
Net cash increase before other cash movements $20,000 $11,400
Cash difference versus current hours — −$8,600

The extension improves modeled operating profit by $1,600 but leaves the practice with $8,600 less cash than maintaining current hours in the first month.

The difference comprises $7,200 of expected receipts not yet collected and the $3,000 equipment purchase. Those delayed receipts are not necessarily lost revenue, but their timing matters for payroll funding. Actual results could also differ if collections fall below expectations.

For the owner's full cash decision, add debt payments, owner distributions, tax payments and other excluded movements separately. Confirm equipment accounting with the CPA rather than treating this example as tax guidance.

Use the Results to Set an Operating Decision

Before approving a change, review four questions:

  1. Is demand genuinely incremental? Validate interest in the proposed sessions rather than assuming every available slot will fill.
  2. Does the configuration work operationally? Confirm provider availability, support coverage, room capacity and administrative workload.
  3. Is the downside affordable? Evaluate the cash needed to fund committed coverage while collections develop.
  4. Can the commitment be adjusted? Understand staffing and vendor terms before assuming a short pilot can be stopped without further cost.

Record the assumptions, decision owner and review point. Compare actual completed visits, collectible revenue estimates, receipts and paid coverage with the approved model. Investigate differences before expanding the schedule further.

For multi-location practices, show both location-level results and the consolidated effect. Apply shared-cost allocations consistently, but separately identify whether the decision actually changes total shared spending. For multi-entity owners, show which entity receives the collections and which funds payroll; a consolidated profit increase does not establish that every entity has adequate cash.

FAQs

Which inputs should a medical practice model first?

Start with additional completed visits, expected collectible revenue per visit, visit-dependent costs and committed staffing coverage. Then add administrative capacity, fixed overhead changes and collection timing. Prioritize assumptions that could change which operating option you choose.

Can I use bank deposits as the revenue assumption?

Use reconciled deposits to understand cash receipts, but do not assume they represent services delivered in the same period. Build a service-period revenue estimate and a separate receipt schedule. Label any management view that differs from the practice's reporting basis and reconcile it with the finance team.

What if existing staff can cover additional hours?

Confirm available paid capacity and the duties that would be displaced. If no additional payroll is required, model that configuration separately from paid expansion. Include any additional coverage costs or reduced output elsewhere rather than assuming existing staff capacity is unlimited.

How should I choose between a profitable option and a cash-conserving option?

Review incremental profit alongside payment commitments, collection delays and available cash. A modest profit improvement may not justify a substantial cash commitment or an inflexible coverage arrangement. Choose a configuration the practice can fund under plausible lower-attendance conditions, not only under the working assumption.
