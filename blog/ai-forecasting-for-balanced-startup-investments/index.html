AI Forecasting for Balanced Medical Practice Reserve Portfolios

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can have a substantial reserve portfolio and still struggle to fund payroll or an equipment payment. Portfolio values may fall just as payer receipts slow, and assets shown on a statement may not be available when an obligation comes due.

The owner's question is not simply, “How is the portfolio performing?” It is: “Do our existing holdings still support the practice's documented liquidity and risk objectives under different market and spending conditions?”

AI-assisted forecasting can help organize that review. It can structure cash requirements, apply explicit assumptions and draft comparisons for human review. It cannot reliably predict investment returns or decide which holdings the practice should buy or sell.

This article explains how to evaluate an existing reserve portfolio against practice obligations, identify potential shortfalls and prepare a decision memo for the owner, finance team and licensed investment adviser.

Define What a Balanced Reserve Portfolio Means

Here, balance means that existing reserves remain consistent with the owner's documented cash-access needs and tolerance for financial loss. It does not mean a particular mix of cash, bonds, equities or other investments.

Before running forecasts, document:

  • Which obligations the reserves must support, such as provider and administrative payroll, rent, debt service or committed equipment purchases.
  • The minimum reserve amount the owner wants to preserve after those obligations are funded.
  • How much loss the owner is willing and financially able to tolerate before reconsidering spending or investment decisions.
  • Which assets can be accessed, by which entity and by what date.
  • Which circumstances require a review rather than continued reliance on the existing plan.

These objectives are practice-specific. A practice opening a second location may have different commitments from an established office with no major capital project.

What this review is—and is not

Analysis Main question
Reserve-balance review Can existing holdings support documented obligations and reserve objectives under different assumptions?
Volatility measurement How much might investment values fluctuate?
Investment-horizon matching How do investment characteristics relate to when funds may be needed?
Diversification analysis How are investment exposures distributed?
General portfolio reporting What does the practice own, and how has it performed?

Those other analyses can inform the review, but none independently establishes whether the practice can fund its obligations. Investment selection, allocation changes and suitability decisions belong with a licensed investment adviser.

Build a Reliable Starting Position

Start with reconciled financial records and current account statements, not an AI-generated estimate of available wealth. The IRS's business recordkeeping guidance supports maintaining complete records, separating business and personal accounts, and reconciling bank records.

Create an inventory of reserve accounts showing the owning entity, statement date, current value, access conditions and any amounts already committed elsewhere. Distinguish operating cash from designated reserves so the forecast does not count the same dollars twice.

For each investment account, ask the adviser or custodian to confirm relevant withdrawal restrictions, settlement timing and potential transaction costs. A quoted market value is not necessarily the amount available to pay an obligation on a particular day.

Keep entity and location views distinct

A combined report can hide a funding problem. Reserves owned by one entity should not automatically be treated as available to another. Confirm ownership, transfer authority and any applicable restrictions with the appropriate advisers.

Within an entity, per-location P&Ls help identify which offices generate or consume cash. However, profit is not cash availability. Translate location results into expected receipts and payments, and apply a consistent method for shared administrative costs. Do not count shared payroll in both the location forecast and the central office forecast.

Forecast the Obligations Before Testing the Portfolio

Build a dated cash schedule that shows when the practice expects to receive and spend money. Use weekly detail around payroll and major commitments, with a longer monthly view for capital planning.

Include:

  • Payer receipts based on observed collection timing, not charges alone.
  • Patient-balance collections using supportable collection assumptions.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, benefits and related payments.
  • Rent, supplies, debt payments and other recurring obligations.
  • Equipment deposits, build-out payments and other committed capital spending.
  • Planned owner distributions, separately identified so their flexibility is visible.

Separate committed payments from deferrable spending. A signed equipment commitment is different from a proposed renovation that has not been approved.

The reserve draw is the funding gap after receipts, not total expenses. If unrestricted operating cash were available, it would also reduce the draw—but only once.

Timing still matters. A receipt arriving after payroll cannot fund that earlier payroll. Track the cumulative cash position at each payment date rather than relying only on the period total.

Use AI to Organize Scenarios, Not Predict Returns

With suitable tools and reviewed inputs, AI can help structure an assumptions table, flag inconsistent categories and draft explanations of scenario differences. Keep calculations in an inspectable model, and have a finance professional verify formulas and results.

A useful scenario set changes one driver at a time before combining stresses:

  1. Reference path: Collections and spending follow the approved plan; portfolio values are held flat as a modeling assumption.
  2. Collections-delay path: Receipts arrive later while committed payments remain due.
  3. Market-stress path: The adviser supplies hypothetical reductions in portfolio values and revised access assumptions.
  4. Combined-stress path: Portfolio losses occur alongside weaker collections or higher spending.

These are conditional tests, not predictions. Do not attach probabilities unless there is a defensible basis for doing so. A scenario labeled “reference” is not necessarily the most likely outcome.

Test simultaneous losses and funding pressure

A practice may need reserves most when investments have lost value. Multiple holdings may also decline together. Testing each holding independently can obscure that exposure.

Ask the adviser to provide a coherent portfolio-level stress assumption, including which holdings could fall together and what proceeds might be available when needed. Separately test business pressure, such as delayed payer receipts or an unavoidable facility expense. Combining them shows whether the practice would face a funding problem at an unfavorable time to sell.

AI can organize those assumptions; it should not invent correlations or portray historical relationships as guarantees.

Use aggregated financial information wherever possible. Patient names, clinical details and patient-level billing records are generally unnecessary for this review. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Compare the Portfolio With Its Obligations

A practical coverage measure is:

Portfolio-to-obligations coverage = scenario-adjusted accessible reserves ÷ (cumulative net reserve draw + documented remaining-reserve floor).

A result below 1.00 means the modeled resources do not cover both the required draw and the owner's reserve floor. A result above 1.00 does not prove every payment can be made on time; dated cash testing remains necessary.

Illustrative portfolio-to-obligations coverage table

Continue the invented example. Starting reserves are $600,000: $150,000 in cash and $450,000 in investments. The owner has documented a $200,000 remaining-reserve floor for this exercise—not as a general recommendation.

The market-stress assumption reduces the investment portion by 20%, leaving $510,000 in total reserves before withdrawals. This is a hypothetical shock, not a return forecast. All scenarios use the same starting holdings, with no purchases or rebalancing. Required withdrawals would reduce subsequent holdings.

The combined path still funds the modeled payments in aggregate, but it breaches the owner's remaining-reserve objective by $40,000. That distinction matters: a policy shortfall is not necessarily an unpaid obligation, though both require attention.

Turn the Results Into an Owner Review Memo

The review memo should compare the unchanged starting portfolio across assumptions before discussing possible investment changes. This separates problems caused by cash demands from those caused by market exposure.

Illustrative review memo

Decision: Does the existing reserve portfolio remain aligned with the practice's documented obligations and reserve floor?

Scope: Same starting holdings across all paths; no rebalancing assumed. Required withdrawals are modeled separately.

Findings: The reference, collections-delay and market-stress paths preserve the illustrative $200,000 floor at the period end. The combined path leaves $160,000, creating a $40,000 policy gap. These results do not establish whether every interim payment date is covered.

Required follow-up: The finance team will examine the lowest weekly cash balance and validate receipt timing. The practice manager will confirm which planned payments are committed and which can be deferred. The owner will decide whether discretionary spending or distributions should change. The licensed investment adviser will assess investment implications, access constraints and any proposed changes to holdings. The CPA will evaluate relevant accounting and tax consequences.

Decision record: Document whether holdings remain unchanged, which spending assumptions change, who owns each action and what event triggers another review.

Do not solve a shortfall merely by lowering the reserve floor in the spreadsheet. Any revised objective should reflect a deliberate owner decision and its consequences.

Establish a Repeatable Review Process

Refresh the analysis after reconciled financial results are available and whenever a material assumption changes. Useful triggers include a major capital commitment, sustained collection delays, unexpected portfolio losses or a change in account access.

Compare actual receipts, spending and reserve withdrawals with the previous forecast. Identify whether a variance came from timing, a recurring operating change, an investment assumption or a modeling error. Preserve dated assumptions so the next review explains what changed rather than simply replacing the old forecast.

FAQs

Can AI determine whether a medical practice reserve portfolio is balanced?

AI can help organize inputs and compare results against documented objectives. It cannot independently establish suitable investment risk or guarantee liquidity. The owner defines business requirements, the finance team validates the cash model, and a licensed investment adviser evaluates investment decisions.

Does coverage above 1.00 mean the practice has enough liquidity?

Not necessarily. Period-end coverage can hide an earlier shortfall. Test payment dates, asset access, settlement timing and entity ownership. Also confirm that receipts and operating cash have not been counted twice.

What should an owner do if a combined-stress scenario fails?

Identify whether the problem is an immediate payment gap, a breach of the reserve floor or both. Review discretionary spending and distributions, verify collection assumptions, and discuss investment implications with the adviser. Document decisions and rerun the forecast rather than treating the AI output as an instruction to trade.
