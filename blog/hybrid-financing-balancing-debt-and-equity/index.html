Hybrid Financing Choices for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A practice expansion can require several kinds of capital at once: funds for equipment and construction, cash for provider and administrative payroll, and a reserve while payer receipts catch up with the new operating costs. One funding source may not fit every need.

Hybrid financing combines debt and equity in a funding package. For a private medical practice, that might mean a loan for defined project costs alongside an ownership investment that supports the opening period. The package can use separate agreements; it does not have to involve a single instrument that converts from debt into equity.

The owner's decision is not simply whether a hybrid package raises enough money. It is whether the complete package creates acceptable cash obligations, ownership changes and decision rights compared with debt-only or owner-funded alternatives.

There is no universally appropriate mix. Before pursuing an equity component, have healthcare counsel assess the proposed ownership and entity structure. Have your CPA review the accounting and tax treatment of the actual documents rather than relying on labels such as “investment” or “hybrid.”

Start With a Defined Practice Project

Evaluate financing against a specific project, not an open-ended growth goal. A second location, an office expansion and an equipment purchase have different spending schedules and cash needs.

Create a project brief that identifies:

  • Scope: What will the project deliver, and which costs are outside the budget?
  • Spending schedule: When are deposits, construction payments, equipment invoices and hiring costs due?
  • Operating ramp: When do staffing and occupancy costs begin, and when are related collections expected?
  • Cash protection: What operating cash must remain available for the existing practice?
  • Accountability: Which entity signs each agreement, receives the funds and pays each cost?
  • Fallback: What happens if opening is delayed, costs rise or collections develop more slowly than expected?

Separate one-time project costs from ongoing operating expenses. Then distinguish expected revenue from actual receipts. A forecast that assumes services become cash immediately can make a funding package appear more affordable than it is.

For a multi-location practice, review the project's location-level outlook alongside the consolidated practice cash forecast. An established office may otherwise appear healthy while quietly funding the new site's payroll and shared administrative costs.

Assemble the Hybrid Package Around Uses and Timing

Assign a purpose to each funding source

For an illustrative project example, consider an established practice preparing to open another location. The owner might evaluate a loan for equipment and build-out costs together with an equity contribution intended to support pre-opening expenses and the collections ramp.

That division is only a proposal. Confirm which expenditures each source permits, when the funds become available and whether reserves must remain untouched. Do not assume that an equipment financing commitment can also cover payroll or that an investor commitment is available before closing.

Create a sources-and-uses schedule with a funding source beside every major cost. Identify any costs that remain unfunded and any expenses dependent on a later financing event.

Coordinate the funding sequence

Ask each funding party what must happen before its money is released. A lender may request evidence of contributed capital, while an investor may want confirmation that the loan will close. If those conditions depend on each other, resolve the sequence before making nonrefundable commitments.

Questions for the closing plan include:

  • Must owner or investor funds be contributed before the loan funds?
  • Are funds released at closing, against invoices or after completed work?
  • Who covers deposits and expenses incurred before funding?
  • What happens if only one component closes?
  • Can the project be reduced or paused without triggering additional obligations?

A signed term sheet and spendable cash are different milestones. The project schedule should recognize that distinction.

Reconcile overlapping restrictions

Review the package as a whole. For example, an investor's expected distributions may conflict with a lender's proposed distribution restrictions. A planned owner repayment may compete with the operating reserve. A future buyback provision may create a cash need after the expansion appears complete.

Ask counsel and the CPA to identify these conflicts before comparing headline rates or ownership percentages.

Put Cash Obligations and Ownership Changes on One Timeline

A funding comparison should extend beyond opening day. Include the operating ramp, regular repayment period and any later maturity, redemption or exit event.

The following is a planning template, not an assumed financing schedule. Replace each entry with the proposed terms.

This timeline exposes obligations that a monthly payment comparison can miss. A delayed payment start may help during opening but leave a larger obligation later. An ownership interest may begin at closing even when distributions are not expected until much later.

Turn the Term Sheet Into an Owner Decision Checklist

Ask for plain-language answers supported by the draft documents. Do not rely on verbal descriptions of “flexible capital” or “passive ownership.”

Required payments and distributions

For debt, ask when payments begin, whether rates or payments can change, what fees apply and whether a balance remains at maturity. For equity, ask what returns are expected and which payments, if any, the documents require.

Clarify how distributions would interact with payroll, operating reserves, debt restrictions and reinvestment. Keep provider compensation, owner distributions and returns on invested capital distinct in the financial review.

Dilution and future ownership

Request an ownership schedule showing the position before closing, immediately after closing and after any proposed future conversion or additional issuance.

Ask what determines the ownership allocation, what happens if more capital is needed, and whether an owner can be diluted by declining to contribute. Have counsel assess any proposed conversion mechanism and its compatibility with the practice's structure before treating it as an available option.

Guarantees and collateral

Ask who must sign guarantees, which obligations they cover and what assets secure the financing. Are there proposed limits or release conditions? What happens if an owner leaves or the project is discontinued?

A hybrid package should not be described as reducing personal exposure unless the actual guarantee and collateral terms support that conclusion.

Decision rights and exit expectations

Ask which decisions require consent: additional borrowing, hiring commitments, major capital spending, owner compensation, distributions, adding locations or selling the practice. Separate routine reporting rights from approval rights.

Also clarify transfer restrictions, valuation procedures, buyback provisions and dispute processes. An acceptable ownership percentage does not resolve an unacceptable decision-right or exit provision.

Compare the Complete Package With the Alternatives

Use one consistent forecast to compare owner-funded, debt-only and hybrid proposals. Evaluate more than the lowest initial payment.

A useful decision memo should answer:

  1. Is the whole project funded? Include the operating ramp and a clearly identified contingency.
  2. What cash must leave the practice, and when? Include scheduled payments, fees and documented investor obligations separately from discretionary distributions.
  3. What changes if receipts are delayed? Test whether payroll and essential operating expenses remain covered without an uncommitted owner rescue.
  4. What does the owner give up? Compare liquidity, guarantees, future economics and decision rights.
  5. What happens if the project stops? Identify remaining debt, committed spending, ownership rights and unwind questions for counsel.

If a package works only with immediate full collections or an assumed refinancing, flag that dependency. The appropriate response may be to renegotiate, stage the project, reduce scope or defer it—not automatically add equity.

Prepare the Records and Post-Closing Workflow

Provide the finance and legal teams with current financial statements, bank reconciliations, existing debt schedules, ownership documents, project bids and the cash forecast. For multiple locations or entities, identify shared-cost allocations and transfers so the same cash is not counted twice.

The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports complete records, separate business accounts and reconciliation. Those practices also help distinguish financing proceeds, project spending and ordinary operations.

After closing, assign responsibility for payment tracking, lender and investor reporting, ownership records and approval requests. Review project spending and collections against the plan, with clear escalation points when cash or timing moves off track.

FAQs

Does a hybrid package require a convertible loan?

No. In this article, hybrid financing means a coordinated debt-and-equity funding package, which can use separate agreements. A convertible instrument introduces additional ownership and documentation questions. Do not assume it is suitable for a medical practice without legal and CPA review.

Is hybrid financing better than debt-only funding for a medical practice?

Not necessarily. Compare the same project under both proposals. Hybrid funding may change the timing of cash needs, but it can also introduce dilution, distribution expectations and approval rights. Debt-only funding may preserve existing ownership while creating payment and guarantee obligations. The specific terms and the practice's cash capacity determine the trade-off.

What should be resolved before signing a term sheet?

Clarify the funding sequence, permitted uses, required cash payments, ownership changes, guarantees, decision rights and exit provisions. Ask counsel which provisions would be binding and have the CPA review the financial assumptions. Resolve any gap between when project bills become due and when committed funds become available.

How should an owner decide whether to proceed?

Proceed only after the project budget, funding commitments and cash-obligation timeline fit together, and the legal and CPA reviews are complete. The owner should understand the downside if collections lag, the personal exposure being accepted and the decisions that will require someone else's approval. A larger funding commitment is not enough if the resulting package is unworkable.
