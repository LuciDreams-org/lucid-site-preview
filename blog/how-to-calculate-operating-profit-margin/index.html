Operating Profit Margin for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Operating profit margin helps a private medical practice owner answer a practical question: After paying the costs of delivering care and running the business, how much operating profit remains from each dollar of net revenue?

The calculation is simple. Making it useful takes more care. Owner compensation, payer payment timing, shared administrative costs and inconsistent accounting periods can make two otherwise similar practices appear very different.

Use operating margin to evaluate your practice’s financial trends, investigate changes and test staffing or expansion plans. Do not treat it as a substitute for a cash forecast or compare it with an unsupported universal benchmark.

The Operating Profit Margin Formula

For an internal management report, use this clearly defined calculation:

Operating profit = Net operating revenue − Operating costs

Operating profit margin = (Operating profit ÷ Net operating revenue) × 100

Operating costs include the direct and overhead costs of running the practice. For this management view, include depreciation and amortization when recorded, but exclude financing interest and income taxes. Payroll taxes and other operating taxes are not excluded simply because they are taxes.

Document the definition with your finance team and use it consistently. If you remove depreciation or make other adjustments, label that result separately rather than calling every version “operating margin.”

Illustrative Example: A Quarterly Practice Calculation

The following figures are an illustrative example, not a benchmark or expected result. Assume the practice uses accrual-based reporting and includes owner-provider compensation in payroll expense.

Quarterly item Illustrative amount
Net operating revenue $600,000
Provider compensation, including recorded owner-provider compensation $210,000
Administrative and support staff payroll $90,000
Employer payroll taxes and benefits $45,000
Medical supplies and outside services $42,000
Rent, utilities and maintenance $36,000
Billing, technology, insurance and other administration $48,000
Depreciation and amortization $9,000
Total operating costs $480,000
Operating profit $120,000
Operating profit margin 20%

The calculation is $120,000 ÷ $600,000 × 100 = 20%. Under these assumptions, the practice retained 20 cents of operating profit per dollar of net revenue before financing interest and income taxes. That does not mean the same amount is available for owner distributions.

Step 1: Establish the Revenue Basis

Start with net operating revenue—not gross charges, all bank deposits or the total outstanding patient balance.

For an accrual-based management report, establish a consistent approach with your accountant for recognizing service revenue and related contractual adjustments, refunds and collectibility adjustments. Gross charges alone do not show what the practice expects to earn.

For a cash-basis report, revenue generally reflects receipts recognized under that basis. Payer and patient receipts may relate to services provided in earlier periods. That makes a cash-basis margin useful for some purposes, but different from a measure that matches service-period revenue with related expenses.

Revenue item Management reporting treatment
Payer and patient service revenue Include according to the report’s accounting basis
Contractual adjustments and applicable revenue reductions Apply consistently; do not subtract them twice
Uncollected patient balances Do not count the entire balance as collected cash or assume it is fully collectible revenue
Owner capital contributions Keep outside operating revenue
Loan proceeds Keep outside operating revenue
Transfers between practice accounts Do not count as new revenue
Equipment sale proceeds or investment income Separate from core service revenue and review the accounting treatment

Reconcile the revenue report to the general ledger. Explain differences between billing-system activity, payer receipts and bank deposits rather than forcing them to match without investigating timing and classification.

Step 2: Include the Full Cost of Running the Practice

A useful margin includes both patient-service costs and the infrastructure supporting them. Counting provider payroll but omitting benefits, administrative staff or billing fees overstates profitability.

Review these categories at each close:

  • People: Employed providers, administrative staff, contractors, employer payroll taxes and benefits.
  • Facilities: Rent, utilities, cleaning, repairs and other occupancy costs.
  • Service delivery: Medical supplies and purchased services used by the practice.
  • Administration: Billing services, accounting, business insurance, software and office expenses.
  • Recorded asset costs: Depreciation and amortization under the reporting policy.

Avoid counting an expense twice when it appears in both a payroll summary and a general-ledger account. Also check for vendor bills paid by an owner or another practice entity that have not reached the correct books.

An unusual expense is not automatically non-operating. A temporary staffing bill or equipment repair may still belong in operating costs. If you show an adjusted view excluding it, retain the unadjusted result and explain the adjustment.

Step 3: Make Owner Compensation Explicit

Owner compensation can be the largest obstacle to a meaningful comparison. An owner may receive payroll compensation, draws, distributions or some combination, depending on the entity and its arrangements. These payments are not interchangeable accounting categories.

A practice whose owner performs substantial clinical and administrative work without a corresponding expense can appear more profitable than one paying employed clinicians and a manager to do that work.

Use two views when needed:

  1. Book operating margin: Based on the revenue and expenses recorded in the financial statements.
  2. Management-adjusted operating margin: Includes a clearly documented adjustment for owner services when necessary for comparison or planning.

Do not simply reclassify owner draws or distributions as wages. Any management adjustment should be separate from the books, supported by stated assumptions and reviewed with the CPA.

Illustrative example: If book operating profit is $120,000 on $600,000 of net revenue, the book margin is 20%. If management applies an additional $60,000 owner-service cost assumption, adjusted operating profit becomes $60,000 and adjusted margin becomes 10%. Neither percentage is an industry benchmark; the difference shows why compensation assumptions matter.

Step 4: Allocate Shared Costs Across Locations and Entities

For a multi-location practice, calculate both a practice-wide margin and location-level results. A location report that excludes central administration may help evaluate direct operations, but it is not a fully allocated operating margin.

Keep the distinction visible:

Report layer What it shows Decision it supports
Net revenue less directly assigned costs Contribution before shared overhead How a location supports central costs
Contribution less allocated shared operating costs Fully allocated location operating profit Location economics after overhead
Consolidated results, with internal activity eliminated Overall practice performance Group-wide profitability and planning

Assign expenses directly when reliable information exists. Allocate the remaining shared costs using a reasonable, documented driver.

Shared cost Possible allocation basis
Central billing services Relevant billing activity or net revenue, depending on the arrangement
Shared administrative payroll Documented time or supported workload estimates
Shared space Space used and occupancy arrangements
Technology subscriptions Assigned users, devices or actual charges
Central management costs A documented approach reflecting how locations benefit

No single allocation method is right for every cost. Keep methods consistent across comparable periods, and explain changes rather than allowing an allocation revision to look like operational improvement.

For multiple entities, maintain entity-level accounts and identify internal charges and transfers. Consolidation should not count an internal management fee as new group revenue while retaining the same fee as a group expense. Review eliminations and the group’s reporting scope with your accountant.

Step 5: Compare Like Periods and Explain the Change

Monthly reports can be distorted by payment timing, payroll schedules, missing bills and delayed reconciliations. Before interpreting a margin movement, confirm that both periods are prepared on the same basis.

A practical monthly review should check:

  1. Revenue completeness: Have receipts, adjustments and relevant revenue entries been posted?
  2. Expense completeness: Are payroll, benefits, vendor bills and shared allocations included?
  3. Reconciliations: Do bank and credit-card balances agree with the supporting records?
  4. Comparability: Are the accounting basis, owner-compensation treatment and reporting scope unchanged?
  5. Context: Did provider leave, a new location or an unusual expense affect the period?

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals also make internal margin reports more dependable.

Review both the current period and a longer trend, such as quarter-to-date or year-to-date results. Where seasonality matters, compare with the same period in the prior year while noting changes in providers, locations or services.

Why Operating Profit Is Not Available Cash

A profitable practice can still experience a cash shortage. Operating margin and cash availability answer different questions.

Item Effect on the operating-margin view Cash consideration
Revenue recognized before collection Can increase accrual-based profit Cash may arrive later
Loan principal payment Not an operating expense Uses cash
Equipment purchase May be reflected through depreciation over time Payment can use substantial cash immediately
Owner distribution Not an operating expense Reduces cash
Depreciation Reduces operating profit when included Does not itself require a current-period payment
Loan proceeds Not operating revenue Increase cash while creating a liability

Pair the margin report with a cash forecast covering payroll, vendor payments, debt service, planned equipment purchases and owner distributions. A positive margin alone does not establish that a distribution or expansion is affordable.

Turn Margin Changes Into Management Decisions

A margin decline is a prompt to investigate, not an instruction to cut staff. Start by separating revenue changes from cost changes.

Finding Question to investigate Possible next step
Collections rose but accrual-based margin fell Were receipts for older balances while current service revenue weakened? Review revenue and collections separately
Payroll rose faster than net revenue Did staffing, overtime, provider availability or revenue timing change? Compare schedules, payroll detail and service activity
One location’s margin fell after allocation changes Did operations change, or only the reporting method? Reconcile the allocation impact
Supply spending increased Was the change caused by prices, purchasing timing or usage? Review purchasing and inventory practices
Margin improved but cash declined Did receivables, capital spending, principal payments or distributions increase? Update the cash forecast

For a proposed hire or new location, build a scenario with expected net revenue, provider and support payroll, benefits, occupancy and incremental shared costs. Show the assumptions explicitly and test a slower revenue ramp. Do not apply the existing practice’s margin percentage to new revenue and assume it will hold.

There is no universal “good” margin established here. A useful comparison requires similar specialties, staffing models, accounting methods, owner-compensation treatment and overhead definitions. Your own consistently prepared trend is often the best starting point.

Build a Repeatable Margin Review

Give each reporting cycle a clear owner. The bookkeeper prepares reconciled accounts, the CPA or finance lead reviews classifications and adjustments, and the practice owner or manager decides what action to take.

Use aggregate financial information for routine analysis. Avoid patient-identifiable details in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Where accounting work involves protected health information, review the appropriate arrangements and handling procedures; HHS explains covered entities and business associates, and its minimum necessary guidance describes the standard and its exceptions.

FAQs

Should owner compensation be included in a medical practice’s operating margin?

Include owner compensation recorded as an operating expense in the book calculation. Draws and distributions are not automatically compensation expenses. If recorded expenses do not reflect the owner’s clinical or management work, present a separate management-adjusted calculation with a documented owner-service cost assumption. Keep that adjustment distinct from the accounting records.

Can a practice calculate operating margin from bank deposits?

Not reliably from deposits alone. Deposits can include loans, owner contributions and transfers, while payer receipts may relate to earlier services. Use reconciled records and a stated accounting basis. A cash-basis margin can be useful, but it should not be presented as though it matches current-period services with their related costs.

What operating profit margin should a private medical practice target?

There is no single target established by this article. Define the calculation first, including owner compensation and shared overhead. Then evaluate your historical results, budget and carefully matched external comparisons. Set a target alongside cash requirements, staffing needs and planned investments rather than relying on a generic percentage.

Should one-time expenses be removed from operating profit?

Not automatically. An unusual cost can still be an operating expense. Keep it in the unadjusted result when appropriate, then show a separate adjusted view if that helps planning. Identify each adjustment and avoid repeatedly excluding costs that are part of running the practice.
