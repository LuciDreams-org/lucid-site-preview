Cap Table Transparency for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

Before discussing owner distributions, admitting a partner or approaching a lender, a private medical practice should be able to answer a basic question: Who owns what, as of which date, and what documents support that record?

A capitalization table, often called a cap table, summarizes recorded ownership interests. For a medical practice, it can serve as an ownership register that connects shares, units or other documented interests to the underlying agreements and transaction history.

The register is not a substitute for governing documents or professional interpretation. Its purpose is to make the ownership record understandable and supportable—not to determine ownership eligibility, value the practice or resolve legal disputes.

For owners and managers of staffed practices, transparency means having a controlled record that supports financial decisions without confusing ownership with compensation, capital balances or cash available for distribution.

Start With the Entity and the Decision

A useful ownership record identifies the exact legal entity it covers. A practice brand, office location and legal entity are not necessarily the same thing.

For example, several offices might operate within one entity. Alternatively, a practice owner might have interests in separate operating and property entities. A per-location profit and loss report helps assess operating performance, but it does not establish ownership of an entity.

Create a separate ownership register for each entity. Then identify the decision the register needs to support:

Decision Ownership information needed Financial information needed alongside it
Discussing a distribution Current interests, applicable economic rights and effective dates Reconciled cash, liabilities, payroll commitments and governing distribution provisions
Considering a partner admission Existing interests, proposed changes and unresolved commitments Proposed contribution, transaction costs and accounting treatment
Reviewing a partner departure Documented interests, transfer terms and relevant approvals Amounts paid or payable and entries requiring CPA review
Preparing financing materials Dated ownership snapshot and supporting documents requested by the lender Entity-level financial statements and relevant obligations

An ownership percentage is an input, not a complete payment instruction. A register alone cannot establish how much cash the practice can distribute or whether a proposed payment follows its agreements.

Build an Ownership Register That Can Be Supported

Use the terminology found in the entity’s documents rather than forcing every practice into a startup-style share table. The register should clearly state its entity, effective date, preparation date, version and review status.

Useful fields include:

  • Owner’s name as recorded in the relevant documents.
  • Interest class or category, where applicable.
  • Recorded shares, units or other measure of interest.
  • Percentage and the denominator used to calculate it.
  • Effective date of each change.
  • Links or references to governing documents and transaction records.
  • Separate references for contributions, transfers and unresolved questions.

Do not label a record “final” merely because its percentages add up. Arithmetic consistency does not establish that the underlying transactions were properly documented.

Illustrative Ownership Register

The following is an invented example for workflow illustration only. Assume a fictional practice entity has a single class of 1,000 recorded units. Its reviewed documents show that Dr. Avery transferred 100 existing units to Dr. Blake, effective September 1 of the illustrative year.

Total 1,000 0 1,000 100% Reconciled transaction schedule

This example records a transfer of existing units, not an issuance by the practice. It does not establish voting rights, distribution rights or tax treatment. Those questions require the applicable documents and professional review.

Maintain a supporting event schedule rather than overwriting the old percentages:

Event Effective date Cash or contribution evidence Document reference Review question
Prior recorded interests Before September 1 Historical contribution schedule, where relevant Prior agreements and register Does the opening register agree with the supporting records?
Transfer of existing units September 1 Identify any owner-to-owner payment separately from practice receipts Executed transfer agreement and applicable approval Did any payment belong in the practice’s books?
Updated ownership snapshot September 1 No new practice contribution assumed in this example Revised register and review record Are the ownership record and accounting treatment consistent?

The key distinction is between a change in ownership and cash received by the practice. Neither should be inferred from the other.

Keep Contributions, Economic Rights and Voting Rights Separate

One percentage column can conceal important differences. Use linked schedules when the documents distinguish among different rights or balances.

Capital Contributions and Accounting Balances

A contribution schedule should identify the contributor, recipient entity, date, amount or description, supporting evidence and accounting classification for review.

Do not calculate ownership by dividing each owner’s contributions by total contributions unless the governing documents and advisers establish that approach. An owner loan, expense reimbursement, contribution and purchase of another owner’s interest are different events.

Likewise, a book capital balance is not automatically an ownership percentage or the amount payable when an owner leaves. Ask the CPA which balances are being presented and how they relate to the legal ownership record.

Economic Interests

Economic rights can include rights to distributions or other financial participation described in the agreements. They may require a schedule separate from recorded units or shares.

Provider compensation also needs its own treatment. An owner’s clinical pay, administrative compensation and ownership distributions should not be collapsed into one ownership calculation. The practice manager should be able to explain which payment category is being reviewed before applying any allocation.

Voting and Approval Rights

Do not assume that the percentage of recorded interests answers every governance question. Have licensed counsel identify the applicable voting rights, approval provisions and any distinctions among interest classes.

A separate rights schedule can reference the relevant document sections without attempting to rewrite them in spreadsheet formulas.

Contingent or Proposed Awards

Keep proposed admissions, conditional awards and informal promises outside the current ownership total unless professional review establishes their status.

Track the recipient, proposal or agreement, conditions, relevant dates and unresolved questions. Clearly distinguish proposed, conditional and currently recorded interests. Do not silently include a future possibility as current ownership—or erase it because it has not yet become current ownership.

Reconcile the Register Before Using It

Reconciliation means connecting each recorded change to its supporting documents and financial consequences. It does not mean forcing every ownership event to match a practice bank deposit.

The IRS guidance on starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices support the financial side of this workflow; they do not resolve disputed ownership rights.

Use the following checklist during an ownership review:

Check Evidence to inspect What to flag
Confirm the entity Entity name on agreements, bank records and accounting files A transaction assigned to the wrong entity
Establish opening interests Prior reviewed register and supporting documents Unsupported opening balances or competing versions
Verify effective dates Executed agreements, amendments and applicable approvals Conflicting signing, payment and effective dates
Reconcile contributions Contribution schedule, bank evidence and ledger entries Receipts classified without adequate support
Reconcile transfers Transfer documents and relevant payment evidence Owner-to-owner payments treated as practice contributions
Check interest totals Opening interests plus documented changes Missing cancellations, duplicate entries or unexplained changes
Review separate rights Economic-rights, voting and contingent-interest schedules One percentage used for every purpose
Check accounting effects Equity, owner-loan, distribution and other relevant accounts Book entries that conflict with transaction substance
Resolve exceptions Issue log and written adviser conclusions Open issues hidden inside a “final” register

Preserve the discrepancy when evidence conflicts. Record what each source says, who is responsible for review and which decisions depend on resolution. Ask counsel and the CPA whether a pending distribution or transaction should wait; administrative cleanup alone cannot settle disputed rights.

Establish Version Control and Clear Responsibilities

A spreadsheet may be sufficient for a straightforward ownership record if it is maintained carefully. Dedicated software may help with more complex records, but no tool replaces executed documents and professional review.

Assign a primary recordkeeper and a separate reviewer. In a smaller practice, the manager may maintain the register while the owner coordinates legal and accounting review.

Use a practical control process:

  1. Preserve source documents. Keep executed versions distinct from drafts, with consistent document references.
  2. Maintain a change log. Record what changed, the effective date, the supporting document and the reviewer.
  3. Separate working and issued versions. Label unresolved drafts clearly and preserve dated snapshots used for decisions.
  4. Limit editing access. Give stakeholders the information appropriate to their role without allowing uncontrolled revisions.
  5. Review after ownership events. Connect relevant accounting checks to the financial close, and review the register before distributions, admissions, departures or financing discussions.

A useful file label includes the entity, ownership “as of” date, version and status. Avoid filenames such as “final-new-final,” which make competing copies difficult to distinguish.

Transparency does not require broad access to every owner’s personal financial information. Share a clear summary with the supporting detail appropriate to the recipient. Patient-level information is not needed for an ownership register.

Prepare Questions for Counsel and the CPA

When records disagree, send the advisers a concise issue packet: the disputed entry, relevant documents, financial evidence, competing interpretations and the pending decision. Do not ask either adviser to work from an unexplained spreadsheet total.

Questions for Licensed Counsel

  • Which documents govern the recorded interests, and how should conflicting versions be interpreted?
  • What is the supported effective date of the admission, transfer or departure?
  • Are any signatures, approvals or other transaction steps unresolved?
  • Do economic rights differ from voting rights or recorded ownership percentages?
  • How should conditional awards or informal ownership promises be classified?
  • What documents and approvals would the proposed transaction change?

Questions for the CPA

  • Do contribution, distribution and owner-loan accounts agree with supporting evidence?
  • Was a payment made to the practice or directly between owners, and how should it be recorded?
  • What accounting or tax reporting questions arise from the effective date?
  • Do prior reports or entries need correction after counsel resolves a discrepancy?
  • What financial information is needed before discussing distributions?
  • How should shared costs and inter-entity balances be presented without confusing them with ownership?

The aim is a coordinated answer: counsel interprets the governing rights and transaction documents, while the CPA evaluates the accounting and tax reporting implications.

Turn the Register Into a Decision Packet

Before an owner meeting, assemble a dated ownership snapshot, the relevant rights schedules, a summary of changes, unresolved issues and the financial information needed for the decision.

For a distribution discussion, that financial information includes cash needs for provider and administrative payroll, operating obligations and other commitments—not just the bank balance. For a proposed partner admission, keep the current register separate from the proposed post-transaction version.

FAQs

Does a single-owner medical practice need an ownership register?

A simple dated record is still useful. It identifies the entity and documented owner and provides a starting point for later transfers, partner admissions or financing requests. The supporting documents matter more than a complicated spreadsheet.

Can we use ownership percentages to calculate distributions?

Not without checking the governing provisions and financial circumstances. Recorded ownership, economic rights, compensation and available cash are separate inputs. Have counsel confirm the applicable rights and the CPA review the financial and accounting considerations.

What should we do when the cap table disagrees with an agreement?

Keep both records, log the discrepancy and avoid silently changing the register. Ask counsel which documents control and what corrections are appropriate. Then have the CPA evaluate related entries and reporting. Clearly label any affected ownership snapshot as unresolved.

Should each practice location have a separate cap table?

Maintain ownership records by legal entity, not automatically by office location. Multiple locations within one entity can have separate operating reports without separate ownership registers. If locations operate through different entities, maintain an entity-specific register and reconcile related financial records separately.
