Real-Time Financial Planning for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can show a profitable month and still face a cash squeeze before payroll. Payer receipts may arrive later than expected, patient balances may take longer to collect, and an equipment payment may fall in the same week as provider and administrative payroll.

Real-time financial planning means using current information to identify those conflicts early enough to act. It does not mean treating every dashboard number as final or rewriting the approved budget whenever collections fluctuate.

For a staffed private practice, the useful system has three parts: reliable closed books, a regularly updated forecast, and agreed triggers for decisions. AI-assisted analysis can support the process, but someone still needs to verify the inputs, explain changes and approve actions.

Keep the Budget, Forecast and Closed Books Separate

These records answer different questions. Combining them into one constantly changing spreadsheet makes it difficult to tell whether the practice is meeting its plan or simply revising the plan to match results.

Financial view What it answers How to use it
Approved budget What revenue, expenses and commitments did we authorize? Preserve it as the baseline for accountability
Closed monthly books What activity has been recorded and reconciled for the completed period? Evaluate actual profitability, balances and budget variances
Current-period estimates What appears to be happening since the last close? Identify emerging issues, with incomplete items clearly marked
Rolling cash forecast When do we expect cash to arrive and leave? Plan payroll, vendor payments, purchases and distributions

Closed books should reflect completed reconciliations and the practice's established accounting policies. They are not necessarily audited, and later corrections may still be needed. Current-period estimates are more provisional: payroll accruals, unpaid invoices, bank activity or other entries may still be missing.

The distinction also depends on the practice's accounting basis. A cash-basis P&L and an accrual-basis P&L recognize activity differently. Neither replaces a forecast of when money will actually be available.

Label every planning report with its reporting period, last refresh date, accounting basis and status—closed or estimated. A precise-looking number without those labels can create false confidence.

Build a Weekly Cash View From Known Obligations

Start with a weekly forecast covering the next several payroll cycles. Extend it far enough to include material commitments such as insurance renewals, equipment payments or planned hiring. A rolling 13-week view is one practical option, not a requirement.

The forecast should begin with available cash by bank account and entity. Identify funds already committed or unavailable for ordinary operations rather than treating the entire bank balance as spendable.

Then organize expected inflows and outflows by the week they are likely to occur.

Forecast input Useful source What needs review
Payer receipts Recent deposits and aggregate billing reports Whether expected payments are supported by collection history and current status
Patient collections Historical receipts and aggregate balance reports Whether payment-plan or balance assumptions are realistic
Provider and administrative payroll Payroll calendar and current staffing information Variable compensation, overtime, benefits and upcoming staffing changes
Operating payments Vendor bills, leases and payment schedules Due dates, recurring charges and one-time commitments
Owner distributions and capital spending Approved decisions and purchase commitments Whether payments remain appropriate under the latest forecast

Anchor the process in reconciled records. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. Those practices help establish a dependable starting point; they do not make future receipts certain.

Assign responsibility for each input. The practice manager might confirm staffing changes and purchase commitments, the billing lead might explain collection timing, and the finance team might reconcile cash and update the forecast. The owner approves decisions that exceed delegated authority.

Forecast Receipts Without Treating Receivables as Cash

Charges, expected reimbursement, accounts receivable and bank deposits are not interchangeable. A practice should not forecast next week's collections by adding up outstanding balances and assuming they will all arrive.

Build receipt estimates from the practice's own payment patterns, then adjust for known changes. Consider payer mix, recent deposit trends, claim-processing issues reported by the billing team, provider availability and seasonal changes in visit volume.

Separate two types of uncertainty:

  • Timing uncertainty: A receipt may still be expected, but its arrival date is unclear.
  • Amount uncertainty: The practice may ultimately collect less than the forecast assumes.

Moving a receipt into a later week addresses timing. Reducing the expected receipt addresses amount. Confusing the two can make a weak collection outlook look like a temporary delay—or make a temporary delay look like a permanent revenue loss.

Use a Base Case and a Downside Case

The base case should reflect the best supported current expectation, not the most optimistic collection outcome. A downside case should test a specific risk, such as a delayed payer batch or lower patient collections.

Illustrative example: A practice begins a planning period with $120,000 in available cash. It expects $80,000 in receipts, $95,000 in payroll-related payments and $35,000 in other payments.

Cash item Base case Delayed-receipt case
Beginning available cash $120,000 $120,000
Receipts during the period $80,000 $50,000
Payroll-related payments ($95,000) ($95,000)
Other payments ($35,000) ($35,000)
Ending available cash $70,000 $40,000

In this illustrative scenario, $30,000 arrives after the planning period rather than during it. If the owner has established a $50,000 minimum cash floor for this practice, the downside case triggers a review even though the base case remains above that floor.

The next step is to inspect the weekly timing. A positive ending balance does not prove the practice can meet every payment along the way. Payroll may be due before the expected receipts arrive.

Set Decision Triggers Before Cash Gets Tight

A useful trigger links a measurable condition to a specific review or action. “Watch collections” is not enough. “Review discretionary spending when the downside forecast falls below the approved cash floor” gives the team a clear responsibility.

Choose thresholds based on the practice's obligations, receipt volatility and access to liquidity. There is no single cash floor or variance percentage that suits every practice.

Trigger First review Possible owner decision
Forecast cash falls below the approved minimum Verify receipt assumptions and payment dates Defer an uncommitted purchase or discretionary distribution
Collections remain below the expected range across successive reviews Separate payment delays from lower volume or collection problems Revise the receipt forecast and assign follow-up
Payroll exceeds plan without a corresponding activity change Review staffing schedules, overtime and compensation inputs Adjust future scheduling or reassess a proposed hire
A location's operating results weaken persistently Check revenue attribution and shared-cost allocations Revise that location's operating plan
A material receipt or payment changes unexpectedly Confirm amount, timing and entity affected Refresh the cash forecast before making another commitment

Triggers should prompt investigation, not automatic cost cutting. A larger payroll may reflect a planned provider start. A weak collection week may reflect a timing shift. Confirm the cause before choosing the response.

Document who can act, what requires owner approval and when the decision will be revisited.

Use a Cadence That Matches the Decision

“Real time” should mean current enough for the decision—not constant intervention. Most practices benefit more from a dependable review schedule than from a stream of unfiltered alerts.

Weekly: Protect Near-Term Liquidity

Review available cash, upcoming payroll, expected receipts and material payments. Compare the previous forecast with what actually happened, and explain the largest differences.

End with a short decision log: what changed, what action is approved, who owns it and when the team will check again. Keep assumptions dated so an old estimate does not quietly carry forward.

Monthly: Use Closed Books to Evaluate Performance

After closing the month, compare actual results with both the approved budget and the prior forecast. These comparisons serve different purposes:

  • Actual versus budget shows whether the practice is performing against its approved plan.
  • Actual versus forecast shows whether recent expectations were reasonable.

Review collections, compensation, operating costs and profitability at the level needed for decisions. Replace estimated periods with closed actuals, then update the remaining forecast. Do not overwrite the original budget to erase unfavorable variances.

Periodically: Reassess Structural Commitments

Hiring a provider, adding a location or buying major equipment requires more than a short-term cash check. Evaluate the ongoing cost, expected contribution, ramp-up assumptions and downside exposure.

A temporary receipt delay may justify postponing a purchase. A sustained change in demand or staffing capacity may justify a formal budget revision. Keep revisions approved, dated and separate from the original baseline.

Preserve Location and Entity Visibility

A consolidated cash total can hide a shortage in one entity or weak performance at one location. Maintain separate views for legal entities and bank accounts, then build a consolidated planning view where useful.

For location reporting, use a consistent method for assigning direct revenue and expenses. Allocate shared costs—such as central administrative payroll—using a documented basis that fits the cost. Identify allocation changes so they do not appear to be operating improvements or declines.

Do not assume cash held in one entity is freely available to another. Have the appropriate advisers review proposed transfers or funding arrangements, and record approved transactions correctly. In consolidated reports, avoid counting intercompany transfers as new operating receipts.

Give AI a Defined Supporting Role

AI-assisted financial work can help organize information, draft variance explanations and compare scenarios. Its usefulness depends on the quality of the records and the review process around it.

A practical division of responsibility is:

  1. Prepare the inputs: Reconcile opening balances and identify missing or estimated information.
  2. Use assistance for analysis: Draft comparisons, summarize changes or calculate alternative assumptions.
  3. Validate the output: Check totals, payroll dates, receipt timing and whether the explanation matches the underlying records.
  4. Approve the decision: Have the responsible manager or owner authorize spending, staffing or distribution changes.

Do not let an AI-generated explanation substitute for evidence from the books or billing team. A pattern in deposits cannot, by itself, establish why a payer paid late.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary Slack messages and general-purpose AI tools. Aggregate financial reports are often sufficient for planning. Where accounting work involves protected health information, assess the applicable arrangements and responsibilities; HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities.

Start With One Repeatable Planning Cycle

For the first cycle, establish reconciled opening cash, list committed payments, build supported receipt estimates and test a downside case. Agree on the cash floor and the decisions that require escalation.

At the next review, compare the forecast with actual receipts and payments. Improve the assumptions where they were wrong. As each month closes, replace estimates with actuals while preserving the approved budget.

The goal is not a forecast that never changes. It is a process that makes uncertainty visible and gives the owner enough time to respond without continually resetting the practice's financial plan.

FAQs

Does real-time planning replace monthly bookkeeping?

No. Current estimates help with immediate decisions, while closed books provide the reconciled foundation for evaluating performance. A forecast built on unreliable opening balances can misstate available cash even when its receipt assumptions are reasonable.

How often should a medical practice update its forecast?

A weekly cash review is a practical starting point. Refresh sooner when a material payment changes, receipts are unexpectedly delayed or liquidity becomes tight. Review profitability after the monthly close, when the relevant records are complete enough to interpret.

Should the budget change whenever collections miss expectations?

No. Update the forecast as expectations change, but preserve the approved budget as the comparison baseline. Consider a formal budget revision when the underlying operating plan changes materially, not simply because one collection week was weak.

Can an owner approve a distribution from the bank balance alone?

The bank balance alone does not show upcoming payroll, unpaid bills or delayed receipts. Review the cash forecast, committed obligations, entity-level needs and downside case before deciding. Obtain appropriate accounting or legal advice for the specific distribution.

What should the owner ask the finance team each week?

Ask what changed since the last forecast, which estimates remain uncertain, whether either scenario breaches a trigger, and which decision needs approval now. Those questions turn current financial information into an actionable planning process.
