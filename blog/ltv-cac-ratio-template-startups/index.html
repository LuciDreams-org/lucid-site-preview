LTV:CAC Ratio Template for Startups

September 17, 2026 · 11 min read

If I want one spreadsheet to tell me whether growth makes sense, this is it. I track CAC, LTV, and CAC payback each month to see if I’m buying growth at a profit or burning cash too slowly to notice.

Here’s the short version:

  • CAC = Sales and marketing spend ÷ new customers
  • LTV = (ARPU × gross margin) ÷ monthly churn
  • LTV:CAC = LTV ÷ CAC
  • CAC payback = CAC ÷ monthly gross profit per customer

A few benchmark ranges make this easy to read:

  • Below 1.0x: losing money on customer acquisition
  • Around 3.0x: often a solid target
  • Above 5.0x: may mean I’m not spending enough to grow
  • Under 12 months payback: often a good sign
  • Over 24 months payback: cash risk gets hard to ignore

The article boils down to four jobs:

  • pull the right monthly inputs
  • build a simple two-tab spreadsheet
  • read the ratio with payback, not by itself
  • use channel and scenario views to decide where to spend next

One point stands out: the ratio alone is not enough. A startup can show 4.0x LTV:CAC and still have a cash problem if payback drags out to 18–24 months. And if CAC looks low because I left out salaries, tools, or founder time, the whole model breaks.

So my takeaway is simple: use fully loaded CAC, gross-margin-based LTV, one churn definition, and monthly updates after close. That gives me a clean way to spot whether the problem is high CAC, weak retention, low ARPU, or thin margin.

This article is less about spreadsheet setup and more about using a small set of numbers to make better growth calls.

Step 1: Gather the inputs for your LTV:CAC template

Before you build the sheet, pull together clean monthly inputs for LTV, CAC, and payback. Most of this will come from your billing platform, CRM, and accounting tools. Every field you collect here will turn into either a column or an assumption inside the model.

Customer, revenue, and churn data

For each month, pull these numbers:

  • Active paying customers on the first day
  • New paying accounts, not seat additions
  • Churned customers
  • Active paying customers on the last day
  • Month-end MRR
  • ARPU

These inputs power your CAC, LTV, and payback math.

Customer churn tracks lost accounts. Revenue churn tracks lost recurring revenue, net of expansion. That difference matters more than a lot of teams expect. A startup might post low customer churn and still lose far more MRR if bigger accounts leave.

If you're early stage, it's fine to start with customer churn. Once account sizes start to spread out enough to matter, add revenue churn too.

Sales and marketing costs to include in CAC

CAC should reflect the full cost to acquire a customer, not just ad spend. Put simply, fully loaded CAC includes everything it takes to win that customer, not only paid ads.

Include all sales and marketing spend: paid media, sales compensation, commissions, agency and contractor fees, software, and campaign costs.

If you count only ad spend, CAC can look lower than it is, which makes the ratio look better than it should. Founder sales time belongs here too. For shared costs, split them across channels with one consistent allocation method.

Gross margin and assumption choices

LTV only makes sense when margin is part of the math. Gross margin = (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue. COGS usually includes hosting, third-party API fees, payment processing, and customer support or onboarding tied to delivery.

If your data is thin, use a 3–6 month rolling average for churn and ARPU, and write down every assumption in a dedicated tab. Once you have enough customers and 6–12 months of data, add cohort tracking.

With the inputs set, you can map them into the template.

Step 2: Build the spreadsheet template

Now turn those inputs into a monthly model. Keep it lean: two tabs in Google Sheets or Excel are enough to get started.

Set up the input and calculation tabs

Use an Inputs tab for your raw monthly data. Stick to one row per month and format dates as MM/DD/YYYY like 01/31/2026. Your columns should include:

  • Month
  • Starting Customers
  • New Customers
  • Churned Customers
  • Ending Customers
  • MRR (USD)
  • Gross Margin %
  • Sales & Marketing Spend (USD)

Use month-end MRR and month-end customer count from the same close date. That keeps the model clean and avoids mismatched numbers.

Then create a Calculations tab that pulls from Inputs with direct cell references, such as =Inputs!F2. Build every metric from those linked cells: ARPU, Monthly Customer Churn Rate, CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC, and CAC Payback Period.

This setup helps for a few simple reasons. Raw data stays separate from formulas, errors are easier to catch, post-close updates are less messy, and finalized months can be locked down without touching the math.

Once the raw fields are set, connect them to the calculation layer.

Map each metric to a formula and source

Field Formula Data Source
ARPU Month-end MRR ÷ Month-end Customers Billing system (e.g., Stripe)
Monthly Customer Churn Rate Churned Customers ÷ Starting Customers CRM or billing export
Gross Margin % (Revenue − COGS) ÷ Revenue Accounting records
CAC Sales & Marketing Spend ÷ New Customers Accounting + CRM
LTV (ARPU × Gross Margin %) ÷ Monthly Churn Rate Calculated fields above
LTV:CAC LTV ÷ CAC Calculated fields above
CAC Payback (months) CAC ÷ (ARPU × Gross Margin %) Calculated fields above

It also helps to add a small formula dictionary on the Calculations tab. List each metric, the formula used, and the source cell. It sounds minor, but it saves time when you come back later and think, Wait, where is this number coming from?

After the core formulas are linked, tweak the sheet to match how you bill customers.

Adjust the template for your pricing model

Different pricing models need small changes, not a whole new spreadsheet.

For B2B SaaS, add ACV and convert it to MRR by dividing by 12. Leave out implementation fees.

For B2C subscriptions, track trial-to-paid conversion and failed-payment churn as separate lines. Lumping them together can blur what's going on.

For usage-based or hybrid models, split base MRR from usage revenue on the Inputs tab. Then use the steadier ARPU figure in LTV. If usage moves up and down a lot, a rolling 6–12 month average is better than using one month's ARPU.

Only add a Cohorts tab when retention or ARPU differs in a meaningful way by channel or acquisition month.

Step 3: Read the results and find what needs fixing

Once the Calculations tab is filled in, don't stop at the ratio itself. The number matters only when you know what it's saying about the business - and what action should follow.

How to interpret common LTV:CAC ranges

Treat the ratio like a signal. Then work backward to see whether the issue comes from CAC, churn, or gross margin. Around 3.0x is a common benchmark for B2B SaaS, and top-quartile companies often land in the 4x–6x range.

LTV:CAC Range What It Usually Means Typical Response
Below 1.0x Negative unit economics Stop scaling; fix pricing, churn, or CAC math first
1.0x–2.0x Weak economics Run small tests; improve retention, pricing, or acquisition efficiency
2.0x–3.0x Close, but not scale-ready Keep testing, but watch CAC payback and retention closely
3.0x–5.0x Healthy sweet spot for most growth-stage SaaS Maintain or gradually increase acquisition spend in proven channels
Above 5.0x Efficient, but may signal under-investment in growth Consider scaling acquisition if payback remains acceptable

Stage matters too. Early-stage PLG businesses may live with a lower ratio while they work on onboarding and virality. Later, when acquisition and retention start to feel more predictable, teams are usually expected to show 3x or more.

Once you know which range you're in, use payback plus the three input drivers to find the bottleneck.

Why CAC payback period matters too

LTV:CAC tells you about profit. CAC payback tells you how long it takes to earn that money back. And that's a big deal.

A strong LTV:CAC ratio can still hide a cash problem. If it takes 18 to 24 months to recover what you spent to acquire a customer, growth can eat through runway long before that cohort pays off.

Here's the math in plain English: at $1,200 CAC and $100 in monthly gross profit, payback is 12 months. At $2,400 CAC, that stretches to 24 months.

Investors and CFOs usually view:

  • Under 12 months as healthy
  • 12–24 months as acceptable, but riskier
  • Over 24 months as a red flag, especially for early-stage companies

If you're hiring fast or ramping spend after a fundraise, slow payback creates a stack of cohorts that still haven't broken even. That's where runway can disappear faster than founders think.

Spot whether the problem is CAC, churn, or margin

When LTV:CAC looks weak, the next move depends on which input is off. That's why blended ratios can be a little sneaky. They smooth everything together and can hide the main issue.

Start with the trends in your template.

If CAC is rising while customer growth stays flat, that's usually an acquisition problem. Maybe channels are getting saturated. Maybe ad costs are climbing. Maybe sales productivity is slipping.

If churn is rising, LTV drops fast. In most cases, the first place to look is onboarding, product engagement, and customer success before cutting acquisition spend.

If ARPU is low or gross margin is thin, LTV can stay weak even when retention looks solid. In that case, segment by plan, customer size, and channel before changing direction.

Step 4: Use the template for planning and monthly reporting

Once you've pinned down the problem, use the template every month to see if your changes are doing their job. This turns LTV:CAC from a one-time snapshot into a monthly check on what’s getting better, what’s slipping, and where to act next.

At the end of each month, pull updated essential financial metrics like MRR, customer counts, churn, and sales and marketing spend from your accounting system and CRM. Let the sheet recalculate LTV, CAC, LTV:CAC, and CAC payback on its own. Then sit down with your team and review what changed.

Run scenarios for pricing, churn, and acquisition spend

Test one lever at a time: pricing, churn, or acquisition spend. Change the same inputs already in the sheet - ARPU, churn rate, CAC, and spend - and keep each scenario in its own column or tab. That way, you can compare the base case next to each outcome without muddying the picture.

Tie each scenario to one specific move. For example, a price increase, a churn drop in one segment, or more spend on a single channel. That makes it much easier to see what’s worth doing and what only looks good on paper.

Use those scenarios to decide which channels or segments should get more budget.

Compare channels or segments with a reporting table

Blended LTV:CAC can hide a lot. One channel may be at 4.6x while another sits below target at 2.5x. If you don’t break it out, you won’t know where to cut back or where to lean in.

Build a simple reporting table that ranks each channel or segment by LTV:CAC and CAC payback period:

Update the table every month. Set a floor - say, LTV:CAC ≥ 3.0x and payback ≤ 12 months - and flag any channel that misses it. Then use that threshold to shift budget where it can do more work.

Keep the template current with clean financial data

Clean financial data is what makes monthly reporting worth trusting. If your numbers are messy, your unit economics will be too.

Keep the monthly process tight: categorize expenses at close, use one churn definition every time, and record revenue in the period it was earned. Conclusion: Build once, update monthly, use it to guide growth

Once each close is done, keep the template simple. Stick to one input tab, one calculation tab, and the same definitions every month.

Use fully loaded CAC and gross-margin-adjusted LTV. If you don’t, the ratio can overstate profitability. Then use payback as your second check. Read LTV:CAC alongside CAC payback, because a strong ratio can still hide slow cash recovery.

The output helps you spot what needs work fast. Low LTV usually points to pricing or retention issues. High CAC often signals channel or targeting problems. Thin margins usually trace back to your cost structure.

Update the model after each monthly close, review the results with your team, and add the summary to your board deck. If you use a single accounting and reporting system, those monthly updates are much easier to keep up. That kind of discipline turns a spreadsheet into a tool you can use to guide growth.

FAQs

How do I calculate fully loaded CAC?

Calculate fully loaded CAC by dividing total sales and marketing expenses by the number of new customers acquired.

Use all direct customer acquisition costs, including:

  • Salaries, payroll taxes, benefits, commissions, software, outside agency or contractor fees, trade shows, and a proportional share of overhead
  • Expenses tied to the sales and marketing team’s work to bring in new customers

Leave out costs that don’t belong to acquisition, such as retention, product engineering, and general administration.

If your sales cycle is long, line up expenses with the period when those customers were acquired. That way, the math reflects what it actually took to win those accounts.

Should I use customer churn or revenue churn?

Track both because they show different sides of business health. Customer churn tells you what share of users leave. Revenue churn shows how much recurring revenue you lose.

Looking at both gives you a clearer read on customer behavior by segment. For example, high churn among small accounts may hurt less than lower churn among high-value clients.

What if my LTV:CAC looks good but payback is slow?

A healthy LTV:CAC can still mask cash flow pressure if your payback period is slow.

Here’s the simple way to think about it: LTV:CAC tells you whether a customer is worth the cost over time. Payback period tells you how long it takes to earn that money back. Those are not the same thing, and that gap matters.

If you want to improve payback, focus on two levers:

  • Lower CAC through channel optimization, tighter targeting, or automation
  • Increase monthly revenue per customer through retention and upselling

If payback is above 12 to 18 months, it’s a good sign to revisit pricing or your sales strategy.
