Cost of Capital for Service- and Equipment-Heavy Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice adding providers faces a different investment decision from one purchasing a major piece of equipment. Both need capital, but they commit cash differently, offer different collateral and may wait different lengths of time for additional collections.

For an owner, the useful question is not simply, “What interest rate can we get?” It is: What does each funding proposal actually cost, when must we pay, and what restrictions or risks come with it?

Cost of capital includes the effective cost of borrowed money and the return owners require for committing their own funds. Leasing adds another comparison: the cost of using an asset, with ownership and end-of-term obligations determined by the contract.

This guide explains how to compare those costs for service-heavy and equipment-heavy private practice investments without relying on industry averages or assuming one funding source is best.

Start With the Investment Profile

“Service-heavy” and “equipment-heavy” describe the proposed investment, not necessarily the entire practice. A single practice can have both profiles at different times.

Decision factor Service-heavy investment Equipment-heavy investment
Typical use of funds Recruiting, provider and administrative payroll, training, and collection-lag funding Equipment purchase, installation, training, maintenance setup, and collection-lag funding
Cash commitment Often builds over successive payroll cycles Often concentrated around deposits, delivery and installation
Potential collateral Much of the spending does not create a separately saleable asset Equipment may provide collateral, subject to lender valuation and contract terms
Main timing uncertainty When new capacity produces collected payer receipts and patient payments When equipment becomes operational and generates collected receipts
Exit flexibility Hiring and operating commitments may continue even if demand disappoints Selling, returning or relocating equipment may involve costs and restrictions
Residual value Recruiting and training expenditures generally do not create a saleable asset Equipment may have resale value, but the amount and selling costs are uncertain

Neither profile automatically has a lower cost of capital. Equipment collateral does not guarantee favorable terms, and a service-heavy investment may be supported by an established practice’s existing cash flow. Compare actual proposals against the same project budget and collection forecast.

Separate the Project From Its Funding

Before requesting proposals, build two schedules.

The project schedule shows the investment’s cash requirements and expected operating results before financing. Include deposits, hiring costs, incremental payroll, installation, supplies, maintenance and the delay between providing services and receiving payment.

The funding schedule shows loan proceeds, lease payments, fees, owner contributions, principal repayments and any required end-of-term payment.

Keeping these schedules separate prevents double counting. For example, an equipment purchase belongs in the project’s cash investment. Loan proceeds fund that purchase; they are not operating revenue. Principal repayment is a financing outflow, not an additional equipment purchase.

Use expected collections, not charges, to assess payment capacity. A provider can be busy while the practice still needs cash for payroll because payer receipts and patient balances have not yet been collected.

For a multi-location practice, identify which location benefits from the investment and which entity signs the financing agreement. Record shared costs consistently so that a location’s apparent profitability is not distorted by expenses paid elsewhere.

Measure Borrowing Beyond the Stated Rate

Request a written proposal and a dated payment schedule. The headline rate alone does not capture the full cost or the cash burden.

Proposal item What to establish
Funds available Gross advance, fees withheld, and net amount actually available for the project
Payment timing First payment date, payment frequency, and whether payments start before the investment generates collections
Interest terms Fixed or variable rate, reset terms, and any introductory period
Additional charges Origination, documentation, closing, appraisal and other required financing costs
Final obligation Balloon payment, maturity balance or other amount not included in regular payments
Early repayment Prepayment charges and whether early payoff reduces the remaining financing charge
Collateral and guarantees Assets pledged, scope of any personal guarantee, and conditions for release
Operating restrictions Required cash balances, reporting, financial covenants and restrictions on other borrowing

For a straightforward loan, calculate the effective rate from the net proceeds received and every required financing payment on its actual date. A spreadsheet’s dated cash-flow return calculation can help; have the finance team check the cash-flow signs and assumptions. Do not label that result a legally defined APR without confirming the applicable methodology.

Also show total financing dollars paid and the lowest projected cash balance. A lower effective rate may still come with a large payment before collections arrive.

Keep collateral and guarantee exposure visible alongside the rate. Those constraints matter even when they cannot be reduced to a reliable dollar estimate.

Compare Leases on Equivalent Terms

A lease proposal needs more than a monthly payment comparison. Establish whether the practice is paying to use and return equipment, to purchase it at the end, or to choose between those outcomes.

Review:

  • Advance payments, deposits and documentation charges.
  • Payment commencement, including obligations before installation or acceptance.
  • Maintenance, insurance and service responsibilities.
  • End-of-term purchase prices or fair-market-value provisions.
  • Return, removal, shipping and restoration obligations.
  • Renewal requirements and notice provisions.
  • Early termination, relocation and equipment substitution restrictions.

Use the same comparison horizon and equipment configuration for each proposal. Separate service and maintenance costs where possible; otherwise, identify the bundled items rather than treating them as financing charges.

A return-only lease and an ownership transaction do not leave the practice in the same position. Compare their cash flows together with their different end states. Do not credit the practice with resale proceeds for equipment it will not own.

Side-by-Side Capital-Cost Worksheet

Illustrative example only: The following figures are invented to demonstrate a worksheet. They are not market rates, quotes, industry averages or recommendations. Both projects require $150,000, and all financing schedules below use 36 monthly payments. Fees are paid separately at the outset.

Worksheet item Service-heavy expansion Equipment-heavy expansion
Project budget $120,000 for recruiting, training and payroll support; $30,000 for equipment $120,000 for equipment; $30,000 for training and operating cash
Total project cash requirement $150,000 $150,000
Illustrative full-project borrowing proposal $150,000 advance; $3,000 upfront fee; 36 payments of $4,800 $150,000 advance; $1,500 upfront fee; 36 payments of $4,700
Total loan repayments plus upfront fee $175,800 $170,700
Financing dollars above the amount advanced $25,800 $20,700
Illustrative equipment-only lease with purchase at end $300 upfront fee; 36 payments of $925; $3,000 purchase payment $1,200 upfront fee; 36 payments of $3,700; $12,000 purchase payment
Total equipment lease cash paid, including purchase $36,600 $146,400
Project spending not covered by the equipment lease $120,000 $30,000
Combined lease-path cash outflow if uncovered spending is owner-funded $156,600 $176,400
Full owner-funded purchase path $150,000 project outflow; no external financing payments $150,000 project outflow; no external financing payments
Items still needed for a complete decision Dated cash flows, owner-capital opportunity cost, reserve impact, guarantees and restrictions The same items, plus supported equipment residual-value and disposal-cost estimates

The loan totals are simple arithmetic. For example, the service-heavy proposal requires $172,800 in scheduled payments plus the $3,000 upfront fee. Its $25,800 financing charge is not an annualized rate; the balance declines as payments are made.

The lease paths are not directly comparable with full-project borrowing. The service-heavy lease leaves $120,000 to be funded elsewhere, while the equipment-heavy lease leaves $30,000. Their combined cash totals exclude the opportunity cost of those owner funds.

In this example, both leases include purchase payments. If the actual proposal instead requires return of the equipment, remove the assumed purchase payment, add applicable return costs, and change the ending asset position. Do not assume the two arrangements are economically equivalent.

Owner Capital Has a Cost Even Without Interest

Using retained practice cash or an owner contribution avoids external financing payments, but it is not free capital.

The opportunity cost is the value of the next-best use of those funds. That could include preserving operating reserves, avoiding another borrowing need, making a different investment or retaining funds outside the practice. Owners also bear the risk that the project returns less than expected or that their money remains committed longer than planned.

Choose and document a required return rather than borrowing a public-company benchmark. It should reflect the owners’ alternatives, risk tolerance and expected commitment period. It is an economic assumption, not an invoice or an automatic accounting expense.

Use that required return to discount the project’s expected cash flows. Do not add an arbitrary opportunity-cost charge and also discount the same cash flows for the same purpose; that would count the cost twice.

If owner advances are intended to be loans rather than contributions, have the CPA and legal adviser address documentation and treatment. The comparison should not assume those arrangements are interchangeable.

Handle Residual Value Conservatively

Equipment resale value can materially affect an ownership comparison. Use supportable estimates for the expected disposal date, not the original purchase price or an optimistic listing price.

Deduct removal, transport, selling and other expected disposal costs. Show more than one residual-value scenario, including a low-value outcome. Confirm whether the financing agreement permits a sale and what payoff or release is required.

Do not subtract an assumed future resale value from the contractual borrowing cost and call the result the interest rate. Residual value belongs in the investment’s economic comparison; it does not change the lender’s required payment schedule.

Why P&L Expense and Financing Cash Flow Differ

A practice can report a profit while facing substantial financing outflows. It can also make a large equipment payment without recognizing that entire payment as an immediate accounting expense.

Item Cash-flow effect Accounting distinction
Loan proceeds Cash received Creates a liability rather than operating revenue
Principal repayment Cash paid Reduces the liability rather than becoming an operating expense
Interest and financing fees Cash paid according to the agreement Expense recognition and fee treatment require the appropriate accounting method
Equipment purchase Cash paid at purchase or through financing May create an asset whose cost is recognized over time
Lease payment Cash paid under the contract Presentation depends on lease terms and the accounting framework
Owner contribution Cash received by the practice Not patient-service revenue

Ask the CPA to confirm accounting and tax treatment separately. Do not assume that an accounting expense equals a current tax deduction, or that a potential tax benefit will arrive when a financing payment is due.

Turn the Comparison Into an Owner Decision

Prepare a short decision package containing the project budget, actual proposals, effective borrowing costs, payment schedules, owner-capital assumptions and contractual restrictions.

Then test delayed collections, slower volume growth, installation delays and lower residual value. Show the lowest cash balance under each scenario and whether the practice can still meet provider and administrative payroll without relying on uncertain resale proceeds.

Use reconciled financial records as the starting point. IRS Publication 583 provides guidance on business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation; those records support a credible funding analysis.

The goal is not to select a funding source from a generic ranking. It is to understand the price, timing, constraints and remaining risks of each proposal before committing the practice’s cash.

FAQs

Does equipment collateral always make borrowing cheaper?

No. Collateral is one consideration alongside the practice’s cash flow, existing obligations, lender valuation and contract terms. Compare written proposals rather than assuming equipment-heavy investments receive lower rates or fewer restrictions.

Is the lowest monthly payment the lowest-cost option?

Not necessarily. It may reflect a longer term, advance payment, balloon balance or end-of-term purchase obligation. Compare all dated payments, effective financing cost, cash-reserve impact and the asset position at the end of the comparison period.

How should a practice compare owner cash with a loan or lease?

Use the same project budget and comparison horizon. Include external financing payments where applicable, identify owner cash required by each option, and evaluate its opportunity cost and effect on reserves. Keep contractual costs separate from uncertain project returns and resale proceeds.
