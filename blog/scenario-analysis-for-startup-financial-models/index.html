Scenario Analysis in Medical Practice Financial Models

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Adding staffed capacity can increase a medical practice’s earnings while reducing its available cash. Payroll may begin before schedules fill, payer receipts arrive after services are delivered, and equipment purchases require cash before their full cost appears in earnings.

A useful scenario model connects those effects. Changing a provider’s start date should change available appointments, revenue, payroll, receivables and cash—not just one revenue cell.

For an owner considering another clinician, expanded hours or additional staffed rooms, the key question is: Does the model carry the operating change consistently through profit, cash and financing needs?

This article explains how to inspect that architecture. It is not a guide to setting departmental budgets, generating scenarios with AI, predicting external market changes or deciding when to refresh a forecast. The focus is whether the spreadsheet correctly translates a proposed operating decision into financial consequences.

Start With a Specific Operating Decision

Define the proposed change before opening the spreadsheet. For example: add a clinician and supporting administrative hours at an existing location, with equipment purchased before the first appointments.

Document:

  • The location and legal entity taking on the activity.
  • The provider and support-staff start dates.
  • Available appointment capacity and expected completed visits.
  • Expected net revenue per completed visit.
  • When payer and patient balances are expected to become cash.
  • Equipment, deposits and other setup payments.
  • Available cash and any proposed borrowing.

Compare the proposal with a no-change baseline, rather than comparing two versions that both assume expansion. Existing operations still need a forecast; the proposed change should be identifiable as an incremental layer.

Use monthly columns through the ramp-up period. Annual totals can hide a temporary cash shortage between the first payroll and the arrival of collections.

Map the Spreadsheet Before Testing Scenarios

An inspectable model separates inputs, operating schedules, financial statements and decision outputs.

Worksheet or section What belongs there Where the results flow
Historical actuals Reconciled financial statements, payroll totals and aggregate collections history Opening balances and assumption support
Assumptions Scenario selector, start dates, capacity, visit yield, receipt timing and cost inputs All operating schedules
Capacity and revenue Staffed sessions, completed visits and net revenue earned Income statement and receivables
Staffing Provider compensation, admin hours, payroll taxes and benefits Expenses, payroll liabilities and cash payments
Receivables and receipts Opening receivables, new revenue, collection timing and adjustments Cash receipts and ending receivables
Capital and debt Equipment purchases, depreciation, borrowing, principal and interest All three financial statements
Financial statements Income statement, balance sheet and cash flow Scenario comparison and funding assessment
Checks and dashboard Reconciliation tests, lowest cash balance and financing gap Owner review

Keep historical actuals separate from forecast formulas. The IRS guidance on business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those records provide a sound starting point; they do not validate future assumptions by themselves.

For multiple locations, retain a location view of operating results. For multiple entities, identify which entity receives collections, employs staff, owns equipment and owes debt. A consolidated cash total can conceal a shortage in the entity responsible for payroll.

Use One Assumptions Table

Avoid separate spreadsheets with independently edited formulas for every scenario. Use one calculation structure with a scenario selector drawing from a single assumptions table.

Assumption No change Planned capacity addition Slower ramp
Added staffed capacity None Defined provider and support schedule Same staffing commitment
Completed visits Existing operations only Planned monthly ramp Lower initial utilization
Net revenue per visit Existing assumptions Supported payer/service mix Same unless mix changes
Collections timing Existing pattern Supported receipt pattern Change only if timing also differs
Payroll start No incremental hire Contracted start Same unless hiring is delayed
Equipment payment None Purchase schedule Same if already committed

Each input should have a definition, unit, source, effective date and responsible reviewer. A collection assumption should specify whether it applies to revenue earned, billed charges or opening receivables. Those are not interchangeable bases.

Keep assumptions that should move together linked. Delaying a provider’s start should delay capacity and applicable compensation. Lower demand, however, may reduce completed visits without reducing guaranteed payroll. The model should reflect the actual operating commitment, not automatically cut costs whenever revenue falls.

Trace Capacity Into Revenue and Staffing

Build revenue from usable capacity

A practical revenue schedule follows this sequence:

Staffed sessions × appointment slots per session × completion rate = completed visits

Completed visits × expected net revenue per visit = revenue earned

Use expected net revenue after relevant contractual adjustments rather than gross charges. Keep receipt timing separate so the model does not treat earned revenue as immediate cash.

Check that provider availability, rooms and support coverage can accommodate the projected activity. Adding appointment slots does not necessarily create completed visits. Also consider whether the new clinician shifts visits away from existing providers; transferred activity is not wholly incremental practice revenue.

Make staffing reflect commitments

Separate provider compensation from support payroll, payroll taxes, benefits and any temporary overlap or training costs. Identify which costs are fixed, hourly, visit-based or triggered by a staffing threshold.

If provider compensation includes both a guarantee and production-based payments, model the actual arrangement rather than adding both indiscriminately. If current administrative staff can absorb the initial workload, avoid allocating existing salaries as new cash costs while also including them in the baseline.

For location reporting, distinguish allocated shared costs from additional spending. Allocating central administration to a new location can change its reported profit without increasing consolidated cash outflows.

Connect Receivables, Capital Spending and Debt

Receivables turn revenue into cash over time

Use an aggregate collection schedule grounded in the practice’s experience, with separate payer and patient-balance patterns where materially different.

The basic roll-forward is:

Opening receivables + net revenue earned − receipts − applicable adjustments = ending receivables

Define adjustments consistently so contractual reductions or expected noncollection are not deducted twice. Opening receivables also need a collection schedule; applying a new-service receipt pattern only to forecast revenue will omit cash from prior services.

Use aggregate financial inputs wherever possible. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools to build a scenario.

Equipment affects earnings and cash differently

A capitalized equipment purchase generally appears as a cash outflow when paid, while book depreciation spreads its cost through earnings. Model deposits, final payments and the in-service date separately when relevant. Have the CPA confirm accounting treatment rather than assuming every setup payment is capitalized.

Borrowing is not revenue

Loan proceeds increase cash and debt, not operating profit. Principal repayments reduce cash and debt; interest affects earnings and cash according to its payment timing.

The debt schedule should reconcile:

Opening debt + new borrowing − principal repayments = ending debt

Do not assume a funding gap automatically produces an approved loan. Show proposed or committed financing separately from additional funding still required.

Illustrative Example: Adding Staffed Capacity

The following is an invented illustrative example, not a case study or benchmark. It isolates the incremental effects of adding a clinician and support coverage over three months; the practice’s existing operations are excluded.

Assumptions for this example:

  • Completed visits are 300, 400 and 500 in the planned case, versus 100, 250 and 400 in the slower-ramp case.
  • Net revenue earned is $100 per completed visit.
  • Half of each month’s revenue is collected that month and half the following month, with no additional losses or adjustments.
  • Added payroll and benefits total $20,000 monthly; other added operating costs total $2,000 monthly; supplies cost $10 per visit. All are paid in the month incurred.
  • Equipment costs $30,000, paid in month one, with illustrative book depreciation of $1,000 monthly.
  • A $20,000 loan funds in month one. Scheduled principal payments are $1,000 monthly and assumed interest payments are $200 monthly.
  • There are no opening incremental receivables, other working-capital changes, taxes or owner distributions.

These are simplified modeling inputs, not recommended compensation, lending or depreciation terms.

Incremental three-month result No change Planned addition Slower ramp
Completed visits 0 1,200 750
Net revenue earned $0 $120,000 $75,000
Cash collected $0 $95,000 $55,000
Ending receivables $0 $25,000 $20,000
Payroll, supplies and other cash operating costs $0 $78,000 $73,500
Depreciation expense $0 $3,000 $3,000
Interest expense and payments $0 $600 $600
Pretax profit/(loss) $0 $38,400 −$2,100
Operating cash after interest $0 $16,400 −$19,100
Equipment payment $0 −$30,000 −$30,000
Loan proceeds $0 $20,000 $20,000
Principal payments $0 −$3,000 −$3,000
Net cash change $0 $3,400 −$32,100

The planned addition earns $38,400 before tax but produces only $3,400 of net cash after equipment and financing. The reconciliation is:

$38,400 profit + $3,000 depreciation − $25,000 receivables growth − $30,000 equipment + $20,000 borrowing − $3,000 principal = $3,400 cash increase.

The slower ramp retains the same staffing commitment, so costs do not fall in proportion to visits.

Inspect the lowest cash point—not just the ending total

In this illustrative example, cumulative incremental cash after the scheduled loan is:

Month-end Planned addition Slower ramp
Month one −$21,200 −$29,200
Month two −$13,400 −$37,400
Month three $3,400 −$32,100

Even the profitable planned case needs cash support early on. With an illustrative $10,000 reserve assigned to this project, the opening cash required would be $31,200 in the planned case and $47,400 in the slower case, after the modeled borrowing.

Those amounts are not the practice’s total funding requirement. Combine the project with existing operations, debt, distributions and other commitments. A weekly cash schedule may also reveal payroll or equipment-payment pressure hidden within monthly totals.

Test the Model Before Trusting the Comparison

Use deliberate input changes to test the links.

Test Expected result Possible defect if it fails
Set added visits to zero Added revenue and variable supplies disappear Hard-coded revenue or costs
Delay the provider start Capacity and applicable payroll shift together Disconnected schedules
Slow collections without changing visits Profit stays unchanged; receivables rise and cash falls Revenue and receipts conflated
Increase equipment cost Cash and fixed assets change; depreciation follows policy Purchase expensed and capitalized twice
Remove borrowing Cash and debt decline; funding gap becomes visible Automatic financing hides a shortfall
Change shared-cost allocation Location profit changes, but consolidated spending does not Allocated costs counted twice

Also verify that:

  • The balance sheet balances in every forecast month.
  • Receivables, fixed assets and debt reconcile to their schedules.
  • Ending cash on the cash flow statement matches the balance sheet.
  • Scenario outputs reference the active assumptions rather than copied values.
  • Formulas extend consistently across months and locations.
  • Payroll burdens, supplies, depreciation and loan payments appear only where intended.

Turn the Model Into an Approval Decision

An owner’s decision summary should show the proposed commitment, incremental profit, lowest cash point, ending receivables, financing status and assumptions with the greatest effect on the outcome.

Use that summary to distinguish financially attractive from currently fundable. A profitable addition may need more opening cash, staged equipment purchases or a different staffing start. A slower ramp may be affordable but fail to produce an acceptable return.
