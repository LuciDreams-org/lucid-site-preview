Burn Rate Scenarios for Medical Practice Cash Runway

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A staffed medical practice can consume cash while opening a location, adding a provider, or working through persistent operating losses. The owner's question is not simply, “What is our monthly burn rate?” It is: How long can available cash support our commitments, and when must we change course?

Burn rate scenarios help answer that question by connecting collections, payroll, expansion spending, debt payments, and owner distributions to a dated cash forecast. The goal is to identify a decision deadline before cash reaches an unsafe level—not merely predict when the bank account reaches zero.

This approach is most useful for practices with operating deficits or temporarily cash-consuming investments. For a mature, cash-generating practice, a burn-rate ratio may offer little insight. A schedule of receipts and payments is still useful, particularly when collections are uneven.

Define Burn Rate Before Calculating Runway

Accounting profit, operating cash flow, and changes in bank balances are different measures. Keep these distinctions visible in the model.

Measure Working definition What it helps the owner assess
Gross operating cash outflows Cash paid for practice operations during a period, before offsetting collections The scale and timing of operating commitments
Net operating burn Operating cash payments minus operating cash receipts, when payments exceed receipts Whether ordinary operations are consuming cash
Total net cash depletion All modeled cash outflows minus all modeled cash inflows How quickly the modeled cash pool is shrinking
Cash runway Time until forecast available cash reaches a stated floor or is exhausted How long current plans remain financially supportable

Gross cash outflows are not necessarily the same as expenses on a P&L. Depreciation is an expense without a current cash payment. Equipment purchases consume cash but generally do not appear immediately as a full operating expense. Debt principal and owner distributions also reduce cash without being operating expenses.

Define your conventions consistently. For example, if operating payments include debt interest, show only principal in the separate debt-payment row. Alternatively, put the entire loan payment in the debt row and exclude its interest from operating payments. Never count the same payment twice.

Likewise, distinguish compensation paid to an owner for services from owner distributions. Cutting distributions does not repair an underlying operating loss, even though it can slow cash depletion.

Why the simple runway formula can mislead

A common shortcut is:

Estimated runway = available cash ÷ average monthly net burn

That formula is only a rough screening tool when burn is positive and reasonably stable. It can mislead when:

  • Payer receipts arrive after payroll or rent is due.
  • A capital purchase creates a large, one-time outflow.
  • A new location incurs staffing costs before generating collections.
  • Owner contributions or borrowing temporarily mask operating losses.
  • A profitable practice distributes more cash than it generates.
  • An average includes a cash-positive period that obscures an earlier shortfall.

If operating cash flow is positive, do not describe runway as “infinite.” Test whether dated commitments can still cause a temporary cash shortage.

Establish How Much Cash Is Actually Available

Start with reconciled bank balances and adjust for outstanding payments and other reconciling items. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliations. Those records are the foundation for a credible starting balance.

Next, separate cash available for the modeled plan from balances that cannot appropriately support it. Review:

  • Amounts reserved for taxes, refunds, or other identified obligations.
  • Funds subject to contractual restrictions.
  • Cash held by another entity that is not available to the operating practice.
  • Pending payments not yet reflected in the displayed bank balance.

Do not treat accounts receivable as cash on hand. Schedule expected payer receipts and patient-balance collections separately, using collection history and current information rather than assuming every outstanding balance will be collected.

Avoid double counting reserved obligations. If a balance is excluded from starting available cash because it will pay a particular obligation, do not subtract that payment again from the available-cash forecast. Keep a separate reconciliation showing the reserve and its eventual payment.

Finally, establish an operating cash floor: the amount management wants to preserve to support critical commitments and uncertainty. This is a management decision, not a universal industry benchmark. A floor is different from restricted or earmarked cash; it remains available, but crossing it triggers action.

For multi-entity owners, prepare entity-level forecasts before consolidating them. One entity's surplus does not automatically make another entity's payroll supportable. Any planned transfer should have a confirmed amount, timing, authorization, and appropriate accounting treatment.

Build a Dated Cash-Depletion Model

Use weekly periods near the decision point, with a longer monthly view for the full expansion or recovery plan. The forecast should extend beyond the period when management expects cash consumption to stop, so that assumption can be tested.

For each period, calculate:

Ending available cash = beginning available cash + forecast receipts − forecast payments

Separate at least these components:

  1. Operating receipts: expected payer deposits, patient collections, and other operating receipts.
  2. Operating payments: provider and administrative payroll, employer payroll costs, rent, supplies, and other operating commitments.
  3. Capital purchases: equipment, build-out payments, and other investment outflows.
  4. Debt payments: principal and interest under the model's stated convention.
  5. Owner distributions: distinct from compensation included in payroll.
  6. Other cash movements: identified transfers or contributions, shown separately rather than treated as earned receipts.

For an expanding practice, use per-location P&Ls to understand operating performance, then translate that performance into cash timing. Allocate shared costs consistently, but also identify which entity actually pays them. A shared-cost allocation is not itself a bank payment.

Document each important assumption with its basis, expected date, responsible reviewer, and next review date. Use aggregate financial information; patient-identifiable detail is generally unnecessary for this analysis.

Compare Scenarios by Spending Type and Reversibility

Useful scenarios change specific assumptions. A downside case should explain what causes lower cash: slower payer receipts, a delayed provider start, weaker collections, or a larger committed payment. An action case should show only changes management can actually execute.

Illustrative example—not a benchmark or actual practice case: A practice has $420,000 in reconciled cash. It excludes $60,000 reserved for separately tracked obligations, leaving $360,000 available for this model. Management sets a $100,000 operating cash floor. Cash component Base plan Downside: lower receipts Action: phase expansion Commitment or reversibility
Monthly operating receipts $220,000 $190,000 $220,000 Uncertain inflow; validate expected collection dates
Monthly operating payments $250,000 $250,000 $250,000 Mixed; scheduled payroll and contractual payments are committed, while some future spending may be adjustable
Monthly operating deficit $30,000 $60,000 $30,000 Result of receipts and payments, not a separate payment
Capital purchases in Month 1 $80,000 $80,000 $20,000 Action case assumes $60,000 is not yet committed and can be deferred

The action case does not assume immediate payroll cuts or delayed contractual payments. It uses two identified choices: deferring uncommitted equipment spending and pausing distributions. Those choices preserve cash but leave the operating deficit unresolved.

Negative balances indicate an unsupported plan, not an ability to continue paying. The action case buys time, but it does not demonstrate that the expansion is sustainable.

These deliberately simplified monthly assumptions must be tested against actual payment dates. If payroll precedes a large payer deposit, cash may cross the floor before the month-end table suggests. Moving an expected receipt into a later week can change the decision deadline even when total projected collections stay unchanged.

Turn Runway into Action Deadlines

The most useful output is not a single runway number. It is a set of decisions with enough lead time to take effect.

For each proposed action, record the approval deadline, notice requirements, earliest cash impact, and potential effect on future receipts. Confirm contractual and staffing constraints before treating spending as reversible.

Forecast signal Decision to evaluate Lead time to verify
Cash floor will be crossed before expansion receipts are established Phase an uncommitted purchase or expansion stage Vendor cancellation deadline, deposit terms, and installation schedule
Planned distributions materially accelerate depletion Reduce or pause distributions if appropriate Owner approval, entity obligations, and scheduled payment dates
Collections remain below the base assumption Reassess expansion pace and receipt assumptions Time needed to validate collection issues and revise commitments
Recurring operating deficits persist Review staffing plans, overhead, and operating economics Contract notice periods and the time before changes affect cash
Entity-level cash cannot cover scheduled payments Review planned transfers and entity commitments Authorization, transfer timing, and accounting review

Work backward from the forecast floor crossing. If an action takes time to approve and implement, its decision deadline must fall earlier. Include uncertainty rather than waiting until the final possible day.

Do not assume staffing reductions produce immediate savings or leave collections unchanged. Provider and administrative capacity support operations; proposed changes need operational review as well as financial modeling.

Refresh the Model and Explain Variances

During active cash depletion, review the near-term forecast weekly and reconcile it to actual receipts, payroll, vendor payments, and capital spending. Refresh the longer view as new information changes the expected cash path.

Separate variances into:

  • Timing: a receipt or payment moved to another period.
  • Amount: the total expected receipt or payment changed.
  • Scope: management added or removed a commitment.

A delayed receipt is not automatically a permanent revenue loss, but it still matters if payroll comes first. Likewise, deferring a purchase improves near-term cash without necessarily reducing its eventual cost.

Assign an owner to each action and track whether its cash effect has occurred. Do not count planned savings as realized savings before implementation.

Make the Decision Before Cash Forces It

Burn rate scenarios are useful when they show which commitments consume cash, which choices remain reversible, and when management must act. Keep operating losses separate from capital purchases, debt payments, and distributions. Then test the cash path by date—not just by monthly average.

The owner's decision is whether the practice can support its plan while preserving essential operating capacity. A scenario model should make that decision clearer before available cash becomes the constraint.

FAQs

Is burn rate the same as a medical practice's accounting loss?

No. An accounting loss includes items that may not involve current cash payments, while cash depletion includes payments such as equipment purchases, debt principal, and owner distributions. Track operating cash burn separately from total cash depletion to understand what is driving the decline.

How much cash should count toward practice runway?

Use reconciled cash available to the modeled practice or entity after identifying restrictions, earmarked obligations, and outstanding payments. Do not include receivables as starting cash. Show expected collections on their forecast receipt dates, and keep the operating cash floor separate from excluded reserves.

Can a profitable medical practice still run short of cash?

Yes. Profit does not establish when cash arrives or leaves. Uneven collections, equipment payments, debt principal, and distributions can create a cash shortage despite reported profit. A dated cash forecast is more informative than a burn-rate ratio in that situation.

When should an owner act on a downside scenario?

Act early enough for the chosen change to affect cash before the forecast floor crossing. Base the deadline on approval steps, contractual terms, implementation time, and uncertainty. Do not wait for the account balance to reach the floor before starting a decision that takes time to execute.
