LTV/CAC for Medical Practices: Limits and Better Measures

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A marketing campaign can fill appointment slots without generating enough cash to support its cost. Another campaign may look weak simply because its payer receipts have not arrived yet. Neither problem is solved by assigning patients an assumed lifetime value.

For owners of staffed private medical practices, the useful question is narrower: Does this acquisition spending produce enough contribution, within a defined period, to justify the spending and support the practice’s cash needs?

LTV/CAC can prompt that question, but subscription-business formulas usually do not answer it. A better starting point is a bounded-period comparison of acquisition costs, completed new-patient visits, realized collections and attributable variable costs. Then test the result against collection lag, attribution uncertainty and available capacity.

Why conventional LTV/CAC can mislead a medical practice

Customer lifetime value, or LTV, estimates the economic value generated over a customer relationship. Customer acquisition cost, or CAC, measures the cost of acquiring a customer. Their ratio compares expected value with acquisition spending.

The difficulty is not the division. It is whether the underlying assumptions describe your practice.

Patients are not subscription accounts

A subscription model may have recurring contract revenue, a defined cancellation event and a measurable monthly churn rate. An encounter-based practice generally has none of those in the same form.

A patient who has not returned may have completed appropriate care, moved, changed insurance or simply not needed another visit. Treating that absence as subscription churn can produce a misleading estimate of relationship length.

Even a practice with membership revenue must distinguish the economics of that arrangement from payer-funded or patient-paid encounters. A subscription formula should not be applied indiscriminately across both.

Charges are not realized value

Billed charges do not establish what the practice will collect. Contractual adjustments, denials, patient balances, refunds and payment timing affect the result. Collections also are not profit: provider compensation, supplies and other costs still matter.

Multiplying average charges by an assumed number of future visits can therefore overstate both revenue and financial contribution.

A ratio does not establish growth capacity

There is no universal LTV/CAC target that proves a medical practice should expand its marketing. An attractive ratio may reflect omitted costs, uncertain attribution or unused capacity that will disappear as volume grows.

Acquisition analysis must not become a reason to recommend unnecessary services. The financial objective is sustainable access to appropriate care—not maximizing visits per patient.

Replace assumed lifetime value with a bounded-period measure

Use a defined group of newly acquired patients, called a cohort, and a fixed observation window. Measure what actually happened within that boundary rather than projecting an indefinite relationship.

A useful internal measure is observed contribution coverage of acquisition spending:

Observed contribution = Realized collections − Attributable variable service costs

Acquisition coverage = Observed contribution ÷ Attributable acquisition costs

Contribution after acquisition spending = Observed contribution − Attributable acquisition costs

These are management measures, not a standardized accounting ratio or a claim about patient lifetime value. Document their definitions in every report.

Establish the measurement rules first

Item Working definition Important boundary
Acquisition cohort New patients attributed to a specified campaign who complete their first visit during a defined intake period Exclude inquiries, cancellations and no-shows from the completed-patient count
Acquisition costs Campaign spending plus attributable agency, creative, tracking and staff costs Disclose allocation methods and avoid counting the same labor twice
Observation window A fixed period after each patient’s first completed visit Compare cohorts with equal follow-up time
Realized collections Payer and patient receipts, net of refunds, tied to eligible services Exclude outstanding balances and receipts for services outside the window
Variable service costs Costs attributable to delivering those services, such as supplies and volume-linked compensation Do not assume every payroll dollar varies with visits
Collection cutoff The date through which eligible receipts are included Show unresolved balances separately; they are not cash collected

Illustrative measurement design: Include services delivered during each patient’s first 90 days after the initial completed visit, and observe associated collections through day 150. Compare only cohorts old enough to have reached the same cutoff. These periods are examples, not recommended standards; choose windows that fit your practice’s service pattern and collection cycle.

Count attributable variable costs for all included services, even when the related payment has not arrived. Otherwise, delayed collections and inconsistent cost recognition can distort the comparison. Label this a contribution analysis, not a cash-flow statement.

Build the analysis from practice records

1. Reconcile acquisition spending

Start with vendor invoices, campaign charges and attributable internal work. Separate campaign-specific expenses from shared marketing costs. If a shared website or agency retainer supports several channels, document the allocation basis and show material unallocated costs separately.

Do not compare one campaign’s advertising bill with another campaign’s fully loaded cost.

Complete records and bank reconciliation support a reliable spending baseline. The IRS business recordkeeping guidance explains these underlying recordkeeping practices; it does not prescribe this campaign analysis.

2. Connect spending to completed first visits

Use a consistent definition of “new patient” and count each person once in the acquisition denominator. Follow-up visits may contribute collections within the observation window, but they are not additional acquisitions.

Source attribution is imperfect. A patient may see an advertisement, hear about the practice from someone else and later search for its name. Record the attribution method and maintain an unknown or mixed-source category rather than forcing every visit into a campaign.

Attributed visits are also not necessarily incremental visits: some patients might have chosen the practice without the campaign. Treat attribution as evidence for a spending decision, not proof of causation.

3. Match receipts and service costs

A bank deposit alone cannot tell you which campaign produced a receipt. Use authorized operational reports to associate collections with the relevant cohort and service window, then reconcile aggregate receipts to the books.

Keep patient-level matching within appropriate authorized systems. Use aggregate financial worksheets wherever possible, and do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum necessary guidance provides relevant privacy context, including exceptions; an aggregate worksheet does not by itself establish compliance.

4. Separate contribution from full practice profitability

Rent, baseline provider and administrative payroll, insurance and other fixed costs still need funding. Leaving them outside a variable-cost calculation does not make them disappear.

If growth requires another shift, provider guarantee or additional administrative coverage, include that added cost in the expansion decision. Review the affected location’s P&L and the practice cash forecast alongside the campaign worksheet.

Both campaigns have the same acquisition cost per completed new patient. Yet Campaign B produces more observed contribution after acquisition spending despite bringing in fewer new patients.

That does not automatically make B the right campaign to expand. Its results could depend on a small sample, an unusually favorable service mix or access to appointment slots that are no longer available. Collection maturity and attribution quality also need review.

A coverage value of 1.0× means observed contribution covers acquisition spending under the worksheet’s definitions. It does not mean the practice has covered all overhead, paid its financing obligations or achieved its required owner compensation.

Test sensitivity before changing the budget

A useful sensitivity analysis asks how the decision changes when a major input moves—not how to manufacture an attractive lifetime estimate.

The table below uses illustrative inputs for Campaign A. Acquisition spending remains $6,000 and attributable variable service costs remain $6,000. Only realized collections change.

Scenario Realized collections Contribution before acquisition Acquisition coverage Contribution after acquisition
Lower collections $12,000 $6,000 1.0× $0
Base case $15,000 $9,000 1.5× $3,000
Higher collections $18,000 $12,000 2.0× $6,000

The lower-collections result could reflect slower payment or lower ultimate receipts. Those require different responses. Review aging and subsequent receipts before concluding that the campaign itself is weaker. Do not silently add expected collections to an actual-results table; show any forecast separately.

Capacity can change the decision as well. In an additional illustrative scenario, if serving the base-case volume requires $3,500 of added staffing beyond the costs already included, the $3,000 residual becomes a $500 shortfall before other fixed costs. A campaign that works with existing capacity may not work once expansion costs begin.

Turn the worksheet into a spending decision

Use the analysis at a recurring owner-manager review, with more frequent cash monitoring when spending or staffing commitments are material.

Finding Practical response
Cohort is too new for comparable collections Maintain a provisional view and wait for a comparable cutoff before declaring a winner
Contribution does not cover acquisition spending Check attribution, missing costs and collection status; limit further exposure while investigating
Contribution is positive but requires added capacity Model staffing and scheduling costs before expanding
Results are favorable but attribution is weak Use a limited, controlled budget change and observe subsequent cohorts
Contribution is positive but cash arrives too slowly Reduce or stage spending to protect payroll and other operating commitments

For multi-location practices, show results by location before combining them. A campaign that fills open appointments at one office may strain another office’s provider schedule. For multi-entity owners, assign spending and receipts to the appropriate entity and document shared-cost allocations without counting expenses twice.

Before approving more spending, answer three questions:

  • Does the observed contribution justify the campaign under conservative assumptions?
  • Can the practice fund acquisition and service delivery while waiting for collections?
  • Will the next increment of volume require costs absent from the historical worksheet?

Conclusion

LTV/CAC is useful as a prompt to compare acquisition cost with economic contribution. It is less useful when it depends on assumed patient lifetimes, subscription churn or universal growth benchmarks.

Start with completed new-patient visits, bounded observation periods, realized collections and attributable costs. Then test collection timing, attribution uncertainty and the cost of serving additional volume. The best spending decision is one the practice can support operationally and financially—not simply the one with the highest ratio.

FAQs

Is there a good LTV/CAC ratio for a medical practice?

There is no universal target that establishes financial health. The result depends on collection timing, included costs, capacity and the practice’s fixed-cost requirements. Define a bounded-period contribution measure and evaluate it alongside location profitability and cash needs.

Should outstanding patient balances count as acquisition returns?

Not in a realized-collections measure. Report outstanding balances separately and distinguish them from expected collections in a forecast. Revisit the cohort after a consistent collection interval so a slow-paying cohort is not compared unfairly with a mature one.

Can a practice estimate value beyond the observation window?

Yes, as a separately labeled forecast supported by relevant historical cohorts. Explain assumptions about future services, collections and costs, and test downside scenarios. Do not present projected value as realized contribution or assume that future visits are clinically necessary.

Does a high acquisition coverage ratio mean we should spend more?

Not by itself. Verify attribution, cohort maturity and cost completeness first. Then assess available appointments, added staffing costs and the cash needed before receipts arrive. A limited budget increase with a defined review point is more informative than assuming past performance will continue at a larger scale.
