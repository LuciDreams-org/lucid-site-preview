Financial Benchmarks for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A financial benchmark is useful only when the comparison is fair. A private medical practice can appear more profitable than another because its owner’s clinical work is not recorded as compensation expense. A new location can look inefficient because it carries a full lease before its patient schedule matures. Strong payer receipts in one month can reflect earlier work rather than current performance.

For owners and managers of staffed practices, benchmarking should answer a specific question: Is this result explained by our business model, or is there an operating problem we should address?

Start with comparable practices, consistent accounting, and clearly defined metrics—not a universal overhead target. Then use the comparison to decide whether to investigate collections, adjust staffing coverage, review shared costs, or revise a location’s financial plan.

Start With the Decision, Not the Industry Average

Different decisions require different comparisons. A practice-wide profit margin may help assess overall performance, but it cannot tell you whether a particular office is covering its direct costs.

Owner’s decision Useful comparison Essential qualification
Is an established office underperforming? Location margin and revenue per provider FTE Similar specialty, provider availability, and location maturity
Can we support another administrative position? Administrative payroll burden and a forward cash forecast Include benefits, payroll taxes, and the proposed role’s responsibilities
Are collections weakening? Receipt trends, accounts receivable aging, and a consistently defined collection measure Separate service dates from payment dates and consider payer mix
Is a new office progressing as planned? Actual results against its opening budget and ramp assumptions Do not judge it solely against mature offices
Is owner income sustainable? Profit after a consistent allowance for owner labor, plus cash obligations Separate compensation for work from ownership distributions

Choose one decision first. A small set of well-defined measures is more actionable than a dashboard of ratios with unclear denominators.

What Makes a Medical Practice Benchmark Comparable?

Before adopting an external benchmark, request its methodology. If the source cannot explain who is included or how a metric is calculated, treat it as background information rather than a management target.

Specialty and service mix

Compare practices with similar economics. A procedure-heavy specialty may have a different supply, equipment, and staffing profile from an office-based specialty. Ancillary services can also change both revenue and expense ratios.

Even within one specialty, determine whether the comparison includes services your practice does not provide. If ancillary operations materially affect results, evaluate them separately before combining them into a practice-wide comparison.

Payer mix and local operating conditions

Practices with different payer arrangements or patient-payment exposure may have different reimbursement levels and collection timing. Local wages and occupancy costs also affect profitability.

These differences do not make comparison impossible. They do mean that a variance needs an explanation before it becomes a cost-cutting instruction.

Size, provider capacity, and location maturity

A practice with centralized administration spreads some costs differently from a single-office practice. Provider headcount alone can also mislead: several part-time clinicians do not necessarily represent the same capacity as the same number of full-time clinicians.

Use a documented full-time-equivalent, or FTE, convention. Identify whether the denominator includes physicians, advanced practice providers, owners, and clinicians who worked only part of the period.

Keep opening-stage, expanding, and mature locations in separate comparison groups. A new office should first be assessed against its approved ramp plan.

Reporting period and sample quality

Check the dates covered, sample size, and whether the figures are averages, medians, or percentiles. An annual survey and a single month of internal results are not directly equivalent.

Also determine whether the source represents independent private practices or includes organizations with materially different funding and cost structures. A large sample is not automatically a relevant sample.

Normalize Accounting Before Comparing Profitability

Two otherwise similar practices can report different margins because they classify transactions differently. Resolve these differences before interpreting a benchmark gap.

Cash versus accrual basis

Cash-basis reports generally recognize receipts and payments when cash moves. Accrual reports recognize revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, subject to the accounting policies used.

For practice management, that distinction matters when payer payments lag services, a payroll period crosses month-end, or annual expenses are paid at once. Compare like basis with like basis. If your management reports use adjustments that differ from your tax books, document the reconciliation with your finance team.

Do not divide cash receipts by billed charges from the same month and assume the result measures collection performance. Those amounts may relate to different services and payment periods.

Owner compensation

Owner compensation is one of the largest sources of misleading comparisons. An owner may receive payroll, distributions, or other payments depending on the entity and its arrangements. Those cash flows do not all have the same accounting treatment.

For management analysis, separate:

  • Compensation for the owner’s clinical work.
  • Compensation for administrative responsibilities.
  • Profit attributable to ownership after accounting for that work.

If the books do not include a comparable expense for owner labor, prepare a separate normalized management view using a documented compensation assumption. Keep that adjustment distinct from the recorded financial statements; it is not a tax instruction or a change to actual payroll.

Nonrecurring costs and financing

Show identifiable opening, relocation, or unusual project costs separately when evaluating ongoing operations. Keep an unadjusted result beside the adjusted view so that normalization does not hide the actual financial outcome.

Also distinguish profit from cash availability. Loan principal payments, equipment purchases, and owner distributions can reduce cash without appearing as ordinary operating expenses. A favorable margin alone does not establish that a practice can fund an expansion.

Build a Small, Defined Benchmark Scorecard

Use a metric dictionary so every reporting period follows the same rules. The following measures are a starting framework, not published industry targets.

Measure Working definition What to document
Operating margin Operating profit divided by revenue on the selected accounting basis Treatment of owner labor, depreciation, interest, and unusual items
Staff payroll burden Non-provider employee wages, employer payroll taxes, and benefits divided by revenue Included roles and treatment of temporary or outsourced staff
Provider compensation burden Included provider compensation and related employer costs divided by revenue Owner normalization and which clinician categories are included
Revenue per provider FTE Revenue divided by average provider FTE for the period FTE convention, partial-period providers, and ancillary revenue
Location contribution Location revenue minus directly attributable location operating costs Which expenses are direct and whether shared overhead is excluded
Fully allocated location margin Location profit after shared-cost allocations divided by location revenue Allocation drivers and consistent treatment across offices
Receivables aging Outstanding receivables grouped by a defined aging date Whether aging begins at service or billing date and which balances are included

Select only the measures relevant to the decision. For example, revenue per provider FTE can flag a difference, but it cannot by itself distinguish schedule availability, service mix, reimbursement, and collection timing.

Compare Locations Without Hiding Shared Costs

For multi-location owners, prepare two views of each office:

  1. Direct contribution: Does the office cover the costs directly associated with operating it?
  2. Fully allocated profitability: What remains after assigning a reasonable share of central support costs?

Both views matter. Direct contribution helps assess an office’s operating economics. Fully allocated profitability shows whether the combined business supports its administrative infrastructure.

Cost Possible treatment Comparison risk
Office rent and local utilities Assign directly to the location Shared premises may need a documented allocation
Staff working at multiple offices Allocate using recorded time or a reasonable documented schedule Assigning all payroll to a home office distorts results
Central administrative team Allocate using a driver connected to its work Revenue alone may not reflect support workload
Shared software Allocate by users, providers, or another relevant usage measure Equal allocation can overburden a smaller office
Owner administrative work Include consistently in the normalized management view Omitting it can overstate operating profitability

Choose allocation methods for a reason and apply them consistently. If a method changes, explain the effect rather than presenting the resulting margin change as an operational improvement.

For multi-entity practices, reconcile entity-level accounts before assembling a combined management view. Identify intercompany charges and transfers so they are not mistaken for additional practice revenue or counted twice as expenses. Preserve visibility into each entity’s obligations even when reviewing combined performance.

Illustrative Example: Why Normalization Changes the Decision

The following figures are an invented illustrative example, not industry benchmarks.

Two mature offices in the same specialty each report $1,000,000 of annual revenue. Office A reports $200,000 of operating profit; Office B reports $120,000. Initially, their margins appear to be 20% and 12%.

However, Office A’s reported expenses omit compensation for clinical work performed by its owner. For this management comparison, the practice assigns a hypothetical $100,000 owner-labor expense. Office B already includes comparable provider compensation.

Measure Office A Office B
Reported revenue $1,000,000 $1,000,000
Reported operating profit $200,000 $120,000
Additional owner-labor adjustment $100,000 $0
Normalized operating profit $100,000 $120,000
Normalized operating margin 10% 12%

The apparent ranking reverses. That does not prove Office B is better managed: provider capacity, shared costs, and revenue classification still need review. It does show why cutting Office B’s staff based only on the original margins would be premature.

Turn Benchmark Gaps Into a Monthly Review Workflow

1. Close and reconcile the underlying records

Reconcile bank activity, payroll, and relevant balance-sheet accounts before calculating ratios. Distinguish payer and patient receipts from borrowing, owner contributions, and transfers.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices also provide a sound foundation for management comparisons.

2. Compare against three reference points

Review the current result against:

  • The same practice or location’s historical performance.
  • Its current budget or operating plan.
  • A suitably matched external benchmark, if one is available.

These answer different questions. Historical results show change; the budget tests expectations; external data provides context. None should replace investigation.

3. Explain the variance before assigning an action

If payroll burden rises, check both payroll and revenue. The cause could be added coverage, overtime, a provider absence, or delayed receipts—not necessarily excess staffing.

If location margin falls, separate direct-cost changes from shared-cost allocation changes. If receipts weaken, compare them with service activity and receivables trends before concluding that demand has declined.

4. Record an owner, action, and review date

Turn each material unexplained gap into a specific task. Examples include reviewing overtime by role, checking whether a new office is meeting its ramp assumptions, or resolving an unreconciled receipt category. Record what evidence would show improvement.

Use aggregate financial data wherever possible. Do not place patient-identifiable details in ordinary Slack conversations or general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; appropriate handling depends on the information and the parties involved.

FAQs

What is a good profit margin for a private medical practice?

There is no single margin that is meaningful across all practices. Specialty, owner compensation, service mix, accounting basis, and shared-cost treatment can materially change the result. Establish a consistent internal definition before comparing it with a documented peer group.

Should owner distributions be included in overhead?

Do not treat distributions as a substitute for measuring the cost of owner labor. Ask your CPA how payments are classified in the books, then prepare a separate management adjustment if needed to compare clinical and administrative labor consistently. Avoid counting compensation twice.

Can I compare a new location with an established office?

Yes, but not as if they were at the same stage. Compare the new location with its opening plan, provider capacity, and expected cost structure first. A mature location can provide context without becoming an immediate target.

What if I cannot find a reliable external benchmark?

Use reconciled historical results, consistent location comparisons, and a documented budget. These are more useful than an external number with an unknown definition. Revisit external comparisons when you can verify the peer group and methodology.
