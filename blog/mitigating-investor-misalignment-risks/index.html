Preventing Owner-Investor Misalignment in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A medical practice owner may see cash in the bank as funding for additional administrative staff or a second location. A capital partner may see the same balance as money available for a distribution. Both can support the practice’s success while holding incompatible expectations about how it should operate.

The time to identify that difference is before approving the operating plan, promising a distribution or signing a new commitment. Once hiring begins or a lease is signed, the disagreement becomes harder to resolve without disrupting the practice.

Owner-investor alignment requires more than agreement on a growth target. It requires a shared understanding of which obligations come first, how much cash stays in the business, what information partners receive and who has authority to approve each decision. This guide focuses on those ongoing operating and capital expectations—not exit objectives or ownership waterfall mechanics.

Start With a Shared Financial Baseline

Before discussing priorities, make sure everyone is reviewing the same financial period, entities and assumptions. A profitable practice can still face a cash shortfall when payer receipts arrive later than expected or staffing costs begin before additional collections.

Prepare a baseline package containing:

  • A reconciled balance sheet and profit and loss statement for each relevant entity.
  • Location-level results where separate locations drive investment decisions.
  • A cash forecast showing expected payer receipts, patient collections and payment timing.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, benefits, vendor payments and other committed outflows.
  • Existing debt payments, planned capital spending and previously approved distributions.
  • A list of assumptions, including uncertain receipts and expenses not yet committed.

Distinguish billed charges, recorded revenue, expected collections and cash already received. They answer different questions and should not be used interchangeably when discussing distributions.

For multi-location practices, explain how shared administrative costs are allocated. For multi-entity owners, show where cash is held and which entity bears each obligation. Do not assume that a positive combined bank balance makes all funds available for any entity’s spending; have advisers review proposed transfers and applicable restrictions.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those basics help partners begin with a reliable financial baseline rather than competing versions of the numbers.

Use an Alignment Interview Before Approving the Plan

Ask owners and existing or prospective capital partners to answer the same questions separately. Then compare their responses in a structured meeting. Independent answers are more useful than a general discussion in which everyone agrees to “support responsible growth.”

For prospective partners, document what remains subject to diligence and formal agreement. For existing partners, compare stated expectations with current governing documents and prior approvals.

Topic Interview questions What to document
Distributions Are distributions expected regularly or only after review? What happens when profit rises but collections lag? Expected review process, cash assumptions and reasons to defer a payment
Reinvestment Which needs come first: staffing, equipment, debt reduction or another location? What evidence would change that order? Ranked priorities and the financial case for each
Cash reserves Which obligations must remain funded? How should uncertainty in payer receipts affect the reserve? Reserve methodology and circumstances requiring reassessment
Reporting access What reports does each party need, at what level of detail and how often? Recipients, delivery cadence, permitted access and question owner
Additional capital Does the plan assume further contributions? Is that funding committed or merely anticipated? Amount, timing, conditions and unresolved funding gaps
Staffing commitments Who supports hiring before collections increase? What happens if the expected receipts do not materialize? Full cost, funding source and hiring review points
Location commitments What must be confirmed before signing a lease or beginning buildout? Funding dependencies, approval process and downside plan
Disagreement Which matters can proceed while a dispute is unresolved? Who coordinates adviser review? Escalation route and commitments to hold pending resolution

Listen for differences hidden inside similar language. “Maintain a healthy reserve” is not an agreement until the parties define how that reserve is calculated. “We will provide more capital if needed” is not a dependable funding source without documented terms and confirmation.

Test Alignment With a Cash-Retention Scenario

A specific trade-off reveals more than broad statements about growth. Use the practice’s forecast to compare a distribution with an operational investment.

Illustrative example: administrative staffing versus a distribution

The following figures are invented solely to illustrate the discussion. They are not recommended reserve levels or financial benchmarks.

A staffed private practice has $300,000 in cash. Its owner proposes retaining funds for a phased administrative staffing project. A capital partner instead expects a $50,000 distribution because the practice reported a profit.

For the planning period, assume the practice has $140,000 of committed cash payments exceeding expected incoming receipts. The parties are also considering a $100,000 minimum ending reserve, separate from those forecast payments.

Cash bridge Illustrative amount
Starting cash $300,000
Net committed cash outflows during the planning period ($140,000)
Proposed minimum ending reserve ($100,000)
Cash available above those needs $60,000
Proposed staffing project ($60,000)
Remaining capacity for a distribution if the project proceeds in full $0

The practice cannot fund both proposals from this forecast while preserving the proposed reserve. Paying the $50,000 distribution would leave only $10,000 above the reserve for the project.

The useful discussion is not whether the owner or partner is “growth-oriented.” It is:

  • Does the staffing project address a documented operational need?
  • Does its budget include wages, benefits, recruitment and onboarding?
  • Can it be phased without undermining its purpose?
  • What happens if payer receipts arrive later than forecast?
  • Is additional funding actually available, and on what terms?
  • What evidence would justify reconsidering a distribution?

Compare alternatives: retain all available funds for the project, phase the project and consider a smaller distribution, or postpone the project. Show the cash consequences of each. Do not assume the project will pay for itself; any expected collection improvement should be identified as an assumption and tested against a downside case.

A workable outcome is a documented decision with review conditions—not a vague promise to distribute cash “soon.”

Clarify Decision Rights Without Inventing Them

An operating plan does not, by itself, establish who has authority to approve spending, distributions or additional capital. Use a discussion matrix to compare the proposed workflow with governing documents, financing arrangements and professional advice.

The matrix below is a planning tool, not a statement of anyone’s legal rights. Counsel should confirm applicable authority, required approvals and any proposed changes. Medical-practice ownership and control questions require qualified legal review rather than assumptions based on a generic investor arrangement.

Decision Financial preparation Questions about authority Evidence to retain
Routine distribution Cash bridge, upcoming obligations and reserve analysis Who may propose and approve it? What conditions or restrictions apply? Approval and forecast used
Unbudgeted hire Fully loaded cost and cash impact Who can authorize spending outside the approved plan? Approved role, budget and funding source
New location or lease Location forecast, startup costs and downside case Which commitments require separate approval? Approval, conditions and signed commitments
Additional capital request Funding gap, use of proceeds and alternatives Do documents permit a capital call? What procedures and consequences apply? Adviser-reviewed notice and applicable terms
Reporting access Report inventory and proposed recipients What access is required or permitted? Who authorizes changes? Access decisions and delivery record
Shared-cost allocation Allocation method and entity/location impact Who reviews and approves the method or changes? Methodology and supporting calculations

Separate the person preparing a recommendation from the person authorized to approve it. A practice manager may assemble a hiring budget without having authority to approve it; a finance adviser may explain distribution capacity without deciding whether to distribute.

Fund Commitments Before Treating Them as Approved

Hiring and location expansion can create recurring costs before the expected receipts arrive. Review both the initial expense and the continuing obligation.

For a staffing plan, show the start date, compensation assumptions, benefits, onboarding costs and the period the practice must fund before any expected financial benefit. For a location plan, include deposits, buildout, equipment, administrative coverage and the operating cash needed during ramp-up.

Then identify the source of funding: existing cash, forecast operating receipts, financing or additional partner contributions. Label each source as confirmed, conditional or uncommitted.

A possible future contribution should not silently become the assumption that makes a plan affordable. If a capital partner cannot or will not contribute, the practice needs an alternative before making the commitment.

Discuss capital calls separately from voluntary funding requests. Do not assume an owner must contribute, that another partner must cover a shortfall or that nonparticipation has a particular consequence. Those questions depend on applicable documents and legal advice.

Agree on Reporting That Supports Decisions

Reporting should explain what changed, what it means for cash and which decisions require attention. More frequent access to incomplete numbers is not a substitute for a reconciled reporting package.

Agree on a practical cadence and include:

  • Actual results against the approved plan, with material variances explained.
  • An updated cash forecast and changes to its assumptions.
  • Location and entity results where relevant, including shared-cost treatment.
  • Distributions made, retained cash and upcoming funding commitments.
  • Decisions requested, supporting analysis and unresolved issues.

Identify preliminary figures clearly. If a report is corrected, explain the change so partners are not debating different versions.

Provide financial information at the level needed for the decision. Aggregated payer receipts and patient-balance totals will often be more useful than patient-level detail. Review access and sharing arrangements with appropriate advisers when patient information may be involved; HHS guidance on minimum necessary information explains the standard and its exceptions. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Maintain an Unresolved-Issues Log for Adviser Review

Meeting notes should distinguish agreement from an open question. Assign each unresolved issue an owner, required evidence and decision point.

Unresolved issue Evidence or review needed Responsible coordinator Commitment affected
Distribution requested despite staffing needs Updated cash bridge and alternative scenarios Finance lead and CPA Distribution approval
Unclear authority for additional funding request Governing-document review by counsel Managing owner Reliance on partner contributions
Disputed location profitability Shared-cost methodology and reconciliation Finance team Expansion budget
Reporting access disagreement Document review and appropriate data-access assessment Owner, counsel and relevant advisers Expanded access

Give each item a status, target review date and written resolution. Where authority or funding remains unclear, identify the commitment that should wait for resolution. A log records the issue; it does not amend an agreement or create approval rights.

Make Alignment Part of the Operating Cycle

Revisit expectations during budget reviews and when circumstances change—such as a delayed payer receipt pattern, an unbudgeted hire, a proposed location or a request for additional capital. Record what was approved, what remains conditional and what would trigger another review.

FAQs

What should a medical practice do when a capital partner expects a distribution but the owner wants to retain cash?

Prepare a cash bridge showing expected receipts, committed payments, the proposed reserve and the investment cost. Compare alternatives and their downside effects. Review applicable approval requirements, then document the decision and conditions for reconsidering it. Accounting profit alone does not establish how much cash is available.

Does approving an operating plan authorize a capital call?

Do not assume it does. Whether additional contributions can be required, how a request must be made and what happens if someone does not participate require review of the applicable documents and legal advice. Keep uncommitted contributions separate from confirmed funding in the forecast.

What financial information should owners and capital partners review together?

Use reconciled financial statements, an updated cash forecast, meaningful budget variances and a schedule of distributions and commitments. Add location/entity detail where it affects decisions. Agree on recipients and access rather than assuming every partner needs unrestricted access to all underlying records.

How should disagreements be handled before hiring or opening another location?

List the disputed assumptions, identify who has approval authority and confirm the funding source. Assign financial questions to the finance team or CPA and document or authority questions to counsel. Specify which commitments will wait until the relevant issue is resolved.
