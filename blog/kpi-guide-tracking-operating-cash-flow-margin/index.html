Operating Cash Flow Margin for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can report a profitable month and still struggle to fund its next payroll. Payer receipts may arrive later than expected, patient balances may remain outstanding, or cash may already be committed to equipment and debt payments.

Operating cash flow margin measures cash generated by operations as a percentage of revenue for the same period. It helps an owner assess whether practice operations are producing cash to support provider and administrative payroll, reinvestment and other commitments.

The formula is:

Operating cash flow margin = Operating cash flow ÷ Revenue × 100%

The calculation is straightforward. The interpretation requires more care. A higher margin can reflect stronger operating performance, but it can also reflect collecting old receivables or postponing vendor payments. Neither necessarily represents a lasting improvement.

Use this metric alongside a cash forecast, not instead of one. It describes cash generation over a completed period; it does not establish whether the bank account can cover next week's obligations.

Define the Numbers Before Calculating the Margin

Operating cash flow is not the change in your bank balance

Use net cash provided by operating activities from the statement of cash flows. A negative amount may be labeled net cash used in operating activities.

Operating cash flow generally captures receipts and payments associated with running the practice. These include payer and patient collections, employee payroll, rent, supplies and other operating expenses. Some classifications, including interest and tax payments, require attention to the reporting framework and the practice's financial statements.

Do not calculate operating cash flow simply by subtracting beginning bank cash from ending bank cash. That change can include borrowing, equipment purchases, owner contributions and distributions.

Transaction Treatment when calculating operating cash flow margin
Payer receipts and patient payments Generally included in operating cash inflows
Provider and administrative payroll Generally included in operating cash outflows
Loan proceeds or owner capital contributions Financing inflows, not operating cash flow or revenue
Capitalized equipment purchases Investing outflows, not operating cash outflows
Loan principal payments Financing outflows, not operating cash outflows
Owner distributions Financing outflows, not operating expenses or operating cash flow
Transfers between the practice's own bank accounts Not new revenue or new cash generated by operations

An owner who also works as a physician may receive compensation and distributions. Keep those categories separate according to the books; do not treat every payment to an owner as the same type of transaction.

Use the same period, accounting basis and reporting scope

Take revenue from the income statement for the exact period covered by operating cash flow. Use the practice's recognized revenue, not unadjusted billed charges or gross deposits.

For an accrual-basis practice, revenue may be recognized before payment arrives. The cash flow reconciliation explains that timing difference through accounts receivable and other adjustments.

For a cash-basis practice, reported revenue is more closely tied to receipts. Its ratio may therefore tell a different story from an accrual-based ratio. Cash-basis operating profit may also be much closer to operating cash flow, depending on the accounting policies used. Do not compare the two bases as though they were interchangeable.

Document the basis and keep it consistent. If management wants an accrual-based view while the books use another basis, have the accountant prepare and explain the adjustments rather than mixing numbers informally.

Also match the entity scope. Revenue for several practice entities cannot be paired with operating cash flow from only one entity. For a combined report, eliminate internal transfers and intercompany activity appropriately.

Worked Example: Reconcile Operating Profit to Operating Cash Flow

Illustrative example: A staffed private medical practice reports $600,000 of accrual-basis revenue and $90,000 of operating profit for a quarter. Its operating profit margin is 15%, but collections and payment timing change how much cash operations generate.

The simplified reconciliation below assumes interest expense equals interest paid, no entity income tax payments, and no other nonoperating items or reconciliation adjustments during the quarter. These are example assumptions, not tax guidance.

Reconciliation item Cash-flow adjustment Running total
Operating profit Starting amount $90,000
Add back depreciation included in operating expenses +$15,000 $105,000
Increase in accounts receivable −$40,000 $65,000
Increase in prepaid operating expenses −$5,000 $60,000
Increase in operating accounts payable +$12,000 $72,000
Increase in accrued payroll and related operating liabilities +$8,000 $80,000
Interest paid, classified as operating in this example −$5,000 $75,000
Net cash provided by operating activities $75,000

Operating cash flow margin = $75,000 ÷ $600,000 × 100% = 12.5%

The practice generated 12.5 cents of operating cash per dollar of recognized revenue during the quarter.

Here is what the reconciliation explains:

  • Depreciation reduced profit without using cash in this period. Adding it back does not erase the separate cash cost of buying equipment.
  • Receivables increased. Some recognized revenue had not yet been collected, reducing cash relative to profit.
  • Prepaid expenses increased. The practice paid cash before recognizing all of the related expense.
  • Payables and payroll accruals increased. Some recognized expenses remained unpaid at quarter-end, temporarily supporting operating cash flow.

A formal indirect-method cash flow statement commonly begins with net income. This example starts with operating profit to make the bridge visible; an accountant must include applicable interest, taxes, nonoperating items and other adjustments in the actual reconciliation.

Why a Higher Margin May Not Mean Lasting Improvement

Collecting old receivables can temporarily lift the ratio

Illustrative example: A practice collects an additional $30,000 of previously recognized payer receivables during a quarter with $600,000 of revenue. With everything else unchanged, operating cash flow rises by $30,000 and the margin rises by five percentage points.

That is useful cash recovery. But collecting an old balance does not create new current-period revenue, and the same backlog cannot be collected repeatedly.

Ask whether the improvement came from a repeatable process change, such as resolving recurring claim-submission problems, or simply from receiving a delayed payment. Review receivable aging and collection trends before treating the higher margin as the new normal.

Delaying vendor payments can create a similar effect

Illustrative example: If the same practice postpones $30,000 of vendor payments into the following quarter, operating cash flow margin also rises by five percentage points, all else equal.

No operating cost has disappeared. The obligation remains and will consume cash when paid. Payment timing within agreed terms can help manage liquidity, but overdue bills may indicate strain rather than improvement.

Review accounts payable aging, upcoming payroll and scheduled payments alongside the ratio. A rising margin supported by growing overdue balances is different from one supported by sustainable collections and controlled costs.

Build a Rolling-Period KPI Worksheet

Review the ratio after the monthly close, when accounts are reconciled and material accruals are recorded. During a cash squeeze, monitor receipts, payments and the short-term cash forecast more frequently; an unfinished monthly ratio may be misleading.

Use both monthly and rolling-period results. A rolling three-month view can reduce the effect of an unusually large payer deposit or a payroll-date shift. A longer view can add context for seasonality.

Illustrative worksheet: The first three months below total the same $600,000 of revenue and $75,000 of operating cash flow used in the reconciliation.

Period Revenue Operating cash flow Monthly OCF margin Rolling three-month revenue Rolling three-month OCF Rolling three-month margin
January $180,000 $15,000 8.3% — — —
February $200,000 $25,000 12.5% — — —
March $220,000 $35,000 15.9% $600,000 $75,000 12.5%
April $240,000 $24,000 10.0% $660,000 $84,000 12.7%

Calculate the rolling margin by dividing total operating cash flow by total revenue for the rolling period. Do not average the monthly percentages.

Add these review fields to the worksheet:

Review field What to record
Reporting basis and scope Accounting basis, entities and locations included
Close status Bank reconciliation status and material unresolved adjustments
Collection timing Unusual payer receipts, patient-balance collections and receivable changes
Payment timing Payroll-date effects, prepaid expenses and unpaid vendor balances
Separate cash commitments Capital purchases, debt principal and planned owner distributions
Management action Specific next step, responsible person and review date

For multiple locations, location P&Ls can help explain differences in revenue and operating costs. However, shared bank accounts, centralized payroll and shared expenses can make location-level operating cash flow difficult to establish. Label any allocations clearly and reconcile the combined result to entity-level cash flow rather than presenting allocated figures as directly observed cash movements.

Use the Ratio for the Right Decisions

There is no universal operating cash flow margin target for private medical practices. Specialty, payer mix, collection patterns, staffing, accounting basis and payment timing all affect the result.

Compare the practice with its own consistent history and budget. Then ask what the cash must support.

Measure What it answers What it does not establish
Operating profit margin How much accounting operating profit was earned per revenue dollar? Whether the related revenue has been collected
Operating cash flow margin How much operating cash was generated relative to revenue? Whether all future obligations can be funded
Bank cash balance How much cash is held now? Whether operations generated it or borrowing supplied it
Operating cash flow less capital spending How much cash remains after capital investment? Whether debt principal and distributions are covered
Short-term cash forecast When are receipts and payments expected? Whether the underlying operating model is profitable

Before adding a provider or administrative position, test the expected payroll and benefit payments against a cash forecast, including the collection delay on added activity. A positive historical margin alone does not prove that the timing will work.

Before purchasing equipment or making a distribution, review cash remaining after capital spending, debt service and other commitments. Those outflows may not reduce operating cash flow margin, but they still reduce available cash.

If the margin weakens, separate collection delays from operating cost increases. Review receivables, payroll, supplies and occupancy costs before choosing a response. If it improves, identify how much came from stronger operations versus temporary timing benefits.

Keep the Supporting Records Reliable

A useful ratio starts with reconciled accounts and consistent classifications. The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations.

Keep the management worksheet focused on aggregate financial information. Patient-identifiable details generally are not needed to explain revenue, receivables and operating cash flow trends; do not place them in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

What is a good operating cash flow margin for a medical practice?

There is no universal target. Compare consistent periods within your own practice, explain unusual collections and payment timing, and assess whether cash generation supports payroll and other commitments. Generic business or SaaS benchmarks are not a substitute for that analysis.

Can a profitable practice have negative operating cash flow?

Yes. On accrual-basis reports, revenue may be recognized before collection. Growing receivables, prepaid expenses or payments of previously accrued bills can consume more cash than current-period profit generates. Reconcile profit to operating cash flow to identify the cause.

Do loans, equipment purchases or owner distributions change operating cash flow margin?

Loan proceeds and owner contributions are financing inflows, not operating cash flow. Capitalized equipment purchases are investing outflows, while loan principal payments and owner distributions are financing outflows. They affect available cash even though they do not directly enter the operating cash flow numerator. Interest and other related items require the appropriate accounting classification.

How often should the practice review the ratio?

Calculate it after each monthly close and review a rolling-period trend. Monitor bank balances, expected payer receipts, payroll and vendor obligations more frequently when liquidity is tight. Use the cash forecast for near-term payment decisions rather than relying on the margin alone.
