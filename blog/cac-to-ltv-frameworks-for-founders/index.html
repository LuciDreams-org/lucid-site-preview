5 Alternatives to CAC/LTV Frameworks for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A marketing campaign can bring in new patients without generating enough contribution or cash to justify its cost. For a staffed private medical practice, the useful question is not how much a patient might spend over a theoretical lifetime. It is whether a specific marketing investment produces financially sustainable access to services the practice can appropriately provide.

Subscription-style lifetime value (LTV) models can obscure that decision. Patients are not recurring subscription revenue, and future medically appropriate demand cannot be presumed. Payer reimbursement, patient balances, collection delays, staffing requirements and available appointment capacity all affect the result.

This article compares five decision frameworks—not a step-by-step CAC calculator, a unit-economics tool or a patient-retention strategy. Use them together to decide whether to continue, adjust, test or stop marketing spending.

Framework Main question Decision supported
Acquisition-cost attribution What did each source cost per attributed new patient? Review channel spending and measurement quality
First-service contribution What remained from the first completed service after attributable delivery costs? Assess whether initial economics justify acquisition spending
Aggregate cohort contribution What contribution has a defined group actually generated? Compare observed results across groups and periods
Cash-payback analysis When did collected cash recover acquisition and delivery outlays? Set an affordable spending pace
Incremental campaign testing What changed because of the campaign? Decide whether added spending deserves continuation

Why SaaS LTV/CAC Ratios Can Mislead Practice Owners

A subscription LTV model often assumes recurring revenue, a measurable cancellation rate and relatively stable service margins. Those assumptions do not transfer cleanly to medical practices.

An absence of visits is not necessarily churn. A patient may have completed appropriate care, moved, changed insurance or simply not needed another service. A higher visit count is not inherently a better outcome, and marketing analysis must not become a reason to encourage unnecessary care.

Revenue is also not the same as collections. Billed charges may bear little resemblance to eventual payer receipts and patient payments. Even expected reimbursement does not tell you when cash will arrive.

Finally, an attractive ratio does not establish affordability. A campaign may produce positive contribution while leaving too little cash for provider and administrative payroll. It may also fill scarce appointments that would otherwise have been booked without advertising.

There is no universal SaaS-style ratio that should authorize a practice's marketing budget. Use observed collections, explicit cost definitions and a separate assessment of capacity and cash.

1. Acquisition-Cost Attribution: Compare Sources Without Claiming Causation

Inputs and calculation

Gather campaign invoices, agency fees, creative costs, attributable marketing labor and an unduplicated count of new patients completing a first service. Booked appointments, inquiries and completed first services should be reported separately.

Attributed acquisition cost = assigned acquisition spending ÷ attributed new patients with a completed first service.

Choose a source-assignment rule, such as the first recorded discovery source, and apply it consistently. Keep unknown sources visible rather than forcing them into a channel. Allocate shared marketing costs using a documented basis, such as staff time or campaign work performed.

Match spending to a reasonable response window. Dividing this month's spending by this month's completed appointments can mislead when scheduling delays cross reporting periods.

Attribution limits and decision

A patient may encounter search advertising, a physician recommendation and the practice website before booking. Any single-source rule simplifies that journey. Attribution describes an accounting assignment; it does not prove that the campaign caused the appointment.

Use this framework to identify expensive channels, missing source data and inconsistent cost allocation. Do not automatically move all spending to the lowest-cost channel: its collections, delivery costs and capacity requirements may differ.

2. First-Service Contribution: Test the Initial Economics

Inputs and calculation

For the attributed first completed services, gather payer and patient receipts, refunds, provider compensation attributable to the services, applicable payroll burden, supplies, payment-processing costs and other direct delivery costs.

First-service contribution = collected revenue from first services − attributable delivery costs.

Then subtract acquisition spending to see whether those initial services have covered the marketing investment.

Separate direct delivery costs from fixed overhead. Existing rent and general administrative payroll may not change with a small campaign, but they still need funding from the practice's total contribution. If additional volume requires overtime, a new provider session or extra administrative coverage, show that added cost explicitly.

Attribution limits and decision

Recently completed services may have unresolved claims or patient balances. Label those results immature; do not treat outstanding balances as collected revenue or assume uncollected amounts will never arrive.

Provider labor also requires judgment. Allocated salary cost helps compare service economics, while incremental staffing cost helps assess the next spending decision. Show both views when the difference matters.

Use this framework to determine whether the campaign depends on later collections or later services to recover its cost. A positive result before overhead is contribution—not practice-wide profit.

3. Aggregate Cohort Contribution: Measure Observed Results Over Time

Inputs and calculation

Define a cohort by first completed service period and, where useful, acquisition source or location. Track its aggregate collections, refunds, attributable delivery costs and original acquisition spending through a stated cutoff.

Observed cohort result = cumulative collected revenue − cumulative attributable delivery costs − acquisition spending.

Compare cohorts at the same age, such as the same number of months after their first-service period. An older cohort has had more time for claims to settle and for any subsequent services to occur.

Keep the original cohort membership fixed. Do not remove patients who have no subsequent visits; doing so would inflate the apparent result.

Attribution limits and decision

This is an observed historical contribution measure, not lifetime value. Subsequent receipts may reflect delayed payment for the original service rather than another visit. Track service dates separately from receipt dates so the distinction remains visible.

Payer mix, seasonality, service mix and provider availability can explain differences between cohorts. Attribution also becomes less certain for later services: the original campaign cannot automatically receive causal credit for all future activity.

Use cohort results to assess whether campaign economics improve as collections mature. Do not extend the observed pattern into assumed lifetime visits or guaranteed future demand.

4. Cash-Payback Analysis: Protect Payroll and Operating Cash

Inputs and calculation

Build a dated schedule of acquisition payments, receipts from the attributed services, refunds and attributable delivery payments.

Cumulative campaign cash position = cumulative receipts − cumulative delivery cash outlays − cumulative acquisition cash outlays.

Cash payback occurs when that position becomes nonnegative. Avoid dividing acquisition cost by a flat monthly contribution estimate when receipts are uneven.

Include delivery obligations that remain unpaid in the accompanying forecast. A temporary positive balance is not reliable recovery if payroll or supplier payments are still due. Money received for services not yet delivered also cannot be treated as unrestricted contribution without considering the remaining service obligations.

Attribution limits and decision

Allocated expenses and actual cash payments are different. For example, assigning salaried provider time to a campaign does not create a separate payroll payment. Reconcile the campaign schedule to the whole-practice cash forecast so wages are neither omitted nor counted twice.

Use payback to set spending limits around payroll, rent and other commitments. A campaign can be economically promising but too slow to fund from current cash. The appropriate recovery period depends on the practice's resources and obligations, not an imported subscription benchmark.

5. Incremental Campaign Testing: Ask What the Spending Actually Added

Inputs and calculation

Where practical, compare a campaign group with a similar group not exposed to the campaign. Options may include comparable locations or staggered campaign periods. Keep access and clinical decisions separate from the marketing test.

Track spending, completed first services, collections, direct delivery costs, capacity and relevant changes in staffing or scheduling.

Estimated incremental result = contribution above a credible no-campaign baseline − added campaign spending − other added operating costs not already included.

Deduct each cost once. If extra staffing is already in delivery costs, do not subtract it again.

Attribution limits and decision

Before-and-after comparisons alone are weak evidence when seasonality, payer contracts, provider schedules or local competition also change. A comparison location may differ materially from the campaign location. Small samples can produce unstable results.

Test design should also consider substitution. Advertising may attract patients who would have booked anyway or shift appointments between practice locations without increasing practice-wide contribution.

Use this framework to decide whether an additional budget is likely to add value—not merely receive credit for existing demand. Treat uncertain findings as a reason for a limited follow-up test, rather than confident expansion.

Attributed acquisition cost is $112.50 per completed new patient: $4,500 divided by 40. First-service contribution is $100 per patient, leaving the initial services short of recovering acquisition spending.

The later observed services bring the cohort result to positive $600 before fixed overhead. This does not establish net profit, prove incremental demand or justify assuming more visits.

Turn the Comparison Into a Budget Decision

At each review, have the practice manager validate source counts and completed appointments, the bookkeeper reconcile spending and receipts, and the owner or finance adviser evaluate contribution, capacity and cash.

For multi-location or multi-entity practices, assign direct expenses to the location or entity that incurred them. Document shared campaign allocations and compare practice-wide results before celebrating a gain at one location that may have displaced another's activity.

Keep the worksheet connected to reliable accounting records. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation; it does not validate a particular marketing attribution model.

Use aggregate reporting wherever possible. Where patient information is involved, follow the practice's approved privacy processes and applicable requirements. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool for this analysis.

FAQs

Should a medical practice stop calculating acquisition cost?

No. Acquisition cost can help compare sources when spending, patient counts and attribution rules are consistent. It cannot, by itself, establish profitability, cash recovery or whether the campaign caused additional appointments.

What if a new practice has little collection history?

Start with completed first services and actual collections, clearly identifying unresolved balances. Use a limited spending budget supported by the cash forecast. Keep any collection scenarios separate from observed results, and do not fill the history gap with assumed lifetime visits.

What should we do when the frameworks disagree?

Identify the disagreement before changing the budget. Low acquisition cost with weak contribution suggests a service-mix or cost issue. Positive contribution with slow payback signals cash pressure. Strong attributed results with weak incremental evidence may mean the campaign received credit for existing demand. Limit expansion while resolving the uncertainty.
