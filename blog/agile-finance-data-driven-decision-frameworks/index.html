Agile Finance Decision Frameworks for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A private medical practice does not need to wait for its annual budget review to address rising administrative overtime or a mismatch between staffing hours and workload. But acting quickly should not mean making decisions from an unreconciled bank balance or an isolated complaint.

Agile finance is a short-cycle process for making, documenting and revisiting financial decisions. For a staffed practice, that means defining a specific question, assigning an accountable owner, reviewing dated evidence, authorizing a bounded action and checking the results.

This is not another forecasting method or dashboard project. It is a way to decide who can act, what evidence they need and when the practice will reconsider the decision. Annual budgets and monthly financial statements remain useful; the decision process connects those records to action between formal reviews.

The practical goal is to make reversible decisions without unnecessary delay while giving consequential commitments the scrutiny they deserve.

Build a Five-Step Financial Decision Cycle

Use the same basic cycle for questions such as adjusting administrative staffing hours, changing a recurring vendor service or reallocating shared support costs between locations.

Step Question to answer Required output
Define What specific decision must we make? A bounded question and intended result
Assign Who is responsible for moving it forward? One accountable decision owner
Review What evidence supports or contradicts the proposal? Dated records, assumptions and gaps
Authorize Who may approve the action, and within what limits? Written approval and operating limits
Revisit Did the action work, and should it continue? A review date and continue, modify or stop decision

A spreadsheet or shared document can hold this process. The discipline matters more than the software.

1. Define a Decision, Not a Broad Concern

“Administrative payroll is too high” is a concern, not a decision question. It does not identify an action, a time frame or an acceptable trade-off.

A more useful question is: “Should we shift administrative coverage away from a consistently quiet period and reduce unneeded overtime while maintaining required coverage?”

The decision statement should identify:

  • The location and employing entity affected.
  • The expense or workflow being changed.
  • The intended financial and operational result.
  • What must remain unchanged, such as approved coverage requirements.
  • Whether the change is temporary or a continuing commitment.

Avoid combining unrelated questions. An administrative scheduling trial should not quietly expand into a permanent headcount decision or a change to provider schedules.

2. Assign Accountability and Approval Separately

The person developing the recommendation does not necessarily have authority to approve it. A practice manager may collect workload information and propose a schedule change, while the owner authorizes changes to total paid hours.

Write down three roles:

  • Decision owner: gathers evidence, recommends action and reports results.
  • Approver: authorizes the commitment within the practice’s governance arrangements.
  • Implementer: makes the approved change and flags exceptions.

One person may hold more than one role, but the distinction should remain visible. A CPA or CFO adviser can evaluate financial evidence without becoming the practice’s employment decision-maker.

For multi-owner or multi-entity practices, confirm the actual approval arrangements before acting. A location manager’s operational responsibility does not automatically confer authority over another entity’s employees or accounts.

3. Review Evidence That Fits the Question

A staffing-hours decision needs both financial and workload evidence. Lower payroll expense is not a successful outcome if unfinished work accumulates or other employees absorb the same hours as overtime.

Evidence What it helps establish Important limitation
Administrative time records and payroll reports Regular hours, overtime and labor expense Confirm the period and distinguish administrative from provider payroll
Aggregate workload by time block Whether coverage matches incoming work A quiet period may still require minimum coverage
Unfinished-work and response-time summaries Whether work is being completed Counts alone may conceal differences in task complexity
Reconciled cash records and upcoming obligations Whether the action fits near-term cash constraints A bank balance alone does not show all commitments
Location P&L and shared-cost detail Where the expense is recorded Allocation changes can resemble operating improvements

Record both the period covered and the date each report was prepared. Payroll from a completed pay period and a current bank balance describe different time frames.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide a stronger foundation for financial decisions than disconnected account screenshots.

If important records are incomplete, state the uncertainty. The approver can then request more evidence, narrow the trial or defer an irreversible commitment.

4. Authorize a Bounded Action

An approval should say more than “go ahead.” Specify the scope, duration, spending limits and conditions that require escalation.

For an administrative scheduling change, document who may adjust coverage, whether total paid hours may change, and whether overtime or temporary help requires further approval. Have the appropriate HR, payroll or legal adviser assess applicable employment requirements before implementation when needed.

Reversible does not mean consequence-free. A schedule can be changed back, but disruption to employees and unfinished work still matter. Permanent hiring, termination, lease and financing decisions generally require more review than a limited scheduling adjustment.

5. Revisit Results Before Making the Change Permanent

Set the review date when approving the action. Otherwise, a temporary experiment can become the default without anyone evaluating it.

At review, compare the outcome with the original objective and operating limits. Decide explicitly to continue, modify or stop. If results are unclear, identify the missing evidence rather than extending the trial indefinitely.

Illustrative Example: Adjusting Administrative Staffing Hours

The following dates, hours and amounts are an illustrative example, not a client case study or a predicted result.

A single-location private practice faces a tight cash period after payer receipts arrive later than expected. Its manager proposes reducing administrative hours. Before approving a cut, the owner asks whether the problem is excess staffing, poorly timed coverage or simply delayed cash receipts.

The manager reviews four weeks of aggregate workload and payroll records. The illustrative records show quiet late-afternoon periods but heavy morning demand and recurring overtime to finish administrative work. The practice currently schedules 160 administrative hours per week, plus an average of six overtime hours.

Instead of immediately reducing regular paid hours, the manager proposes shifting eight scheduled hours from quiet periods to morning coverage. The trial aims to reduce overtime without increasing unfinished work. This is a scheduling decision, not a permanent staffing reduction.

Finance estimates the change in payroll expense using actual pay rates and applicable payroll costs. The owner also reviews upcoming payroll and other obligations. Expected payer receipts are treated as uncertain until received, not as permission to make new commitments.

Put the Decision in a Log

The log should preserve what was known when approval occurred—not just describe the eventual outcome.

Decision-log field Illustrative entry
Decision ID and question ADM-01: Can shifting administrative coverage reduce overtime without worsening unfinished work?
Accountable owner Practice manager
Evidence dates Workload and time records: September 7–October 4, 2026; reports prepared October 6; cash reconciled through October 5
Evidence limitations One employee absence affected workload; delayed payer receipts may not represent an ongoing trend
Proposed action Shift eight regular hours per week to morning coverage; keep total regular hours unchanged
Approval authority Practice owner approves the trial; manager may adjust schedules only within that approval
Authorization date October 8, 2026
Operating limits Maintain approved coverage; no pay-rate changes; escalate additional overtime requests
Reversibility Prior schedule can be restored, subject to scheduling and employment considerations
Review date October 26, 2026, using completed trial records
Stop or escalation trigger Coverage gap, worsening unfinished work or a need to exceed approved hours
Final disposition Record continue, modify or stop, with reasons and the next review date

During the trial, the manager tracks overtime, unfinished work and coverage exceptions. At review, the owner checks whether lower overtime reflects genuine improvement or merely work postponed to a later week.

If payroll expense falls but the backlog grows, the trial has not met its objective. If performance improves, the owner can approve continued use with another review. If cash remains tight, the practice must address that separately rather than assume the scheduling change solves the entire liquidity problem.

Match Review Speed to the Consequences

Agile finance does not require every decision to move at the same speed. Use shorter cycles for limited, reversible actions and deeper review for commitments that are expensive or difficult to unwind.

Decision type Governance approach
Reallocate existing administrative coverage Manager recommendation, defined authority and a short trial
Add recurring staff hours Owner approval supported by workload and recurring-cost evidence
Hire permanently or sign a long contract Broader financial review and appropriate professional advice
Move shared costs between locations or entities Finance review of allocation basis and affected reports before approval

For multiple locations, distinguish a real improvement from a cost transfer. Moving administrative work to another office may improve one location’s P&L without reducing total practice expense. Identify where the work goes, who employs the staff and how shared costs are recorded.

Keep the decision queue manageable. A brief recurring meeting should focus on decisions awaiting approval, evidence gaps, active trials and reviews due—not a recital of every financial metric.

Use AI to Prepare Evidence, Not Exercise Authority

AI can help summarize approved, non-patient-identifiable inputs, organize a draft decision log and highlight missing dates or conflicting totals. A reviewer can ask it to separate documented facts from assumptions or list questions the owner should resolve before approval.

These are preparation tasks. The practice still needs human verification of source records and calculations.

AI-assisted task Human responsibility
Summarize dated payroll and workload totals Verify completeness, classifications and source periods
Draft a comparison of proposed actions Assess feasibility and effects on staff and operations
Flag missing approval or review fields Confirm actual authority and obtain approval
Summarize trial results Judge trade-offs and decide whether to continue

Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool. Aggregate operational counts are usually more useful for this decision than patient-level detail. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; practices should use their established privacy processes when selecting and sharing information.

AI should not authorize spending, decide employment changes or turn uncertain receipts into assumed cash availability. Those judgments belong to the authorized people responsible for the practice.

Make Each Decision Improve the Next One

A useful review records more than whether costs went down. Capture what the team learned: Was the workload measure reliable? Did the approver receive the evidence in time? Did a shared-cost allocation obscure the result? Should a similar future decision require different limits?

FAQs

Does a medical practice need Scrum or SAFe to use agile finance?

No. A practice can use a simple decision log and a recurring review meeting. Formal frameworks are optional. The essential elements are a clear question, accountable owner, dated evidence, approval authority and a scheduled review.

How often should the practice review financial decisions?

Match the timing to the action and the availability of meaningful evidence. A scheduling trial may warrant review after a completed payroll period, while a longer commitment needs a different timetable. Set the review date before implementation and define conditions that require earlier escalation.

What if the books are behind but a decision is urgent?

Identify which records are current and which are incomplete. Verify the cash and obligations relevant to the decision, document uncertainty and limit the action where possible. Catch-up bookkeeping can improve the broader picture, but an urgent decision should not treat incomplete reports as reliable simply because they are available.

Who should make the final decision: the owner, manager or finance adviser?

The person with the practice’s actual approval authority should decide. Managers can develop recommendations and implement approved changes; finance advisers can validate financial evidence and explain trade-offs. Neither an AI summary nor an adviser’s analysis replaces the required authorization.
