AI Payment Fraud Detection for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A duplicate supply invoice, an unexpected equipment payment or a vendor bank-account change can put a medical practice’s cash at risk. The owner’s immediate decision is practical: which payments should wait for investigation before funds move?

AI-assisted anomaly detection can help prioritize that review by comparing proposed payments with transaction history and approved records. It can surface unusual combinations that deserve attention, but an alert is not proof of fraud. Legitimate purchases can look unusual, and fraudulent payments can resemble routine ones.

For a staffed private practice, the useful outcome is a payment-exception queue: a list of flagged transactions, each with an assigned reviewer, supporting evidence and a documented release or cancellation decision. Detection only helps if someone can stop an unresolved payment before it leaves the account.

What Transaction-Level Detection Should Examine

This workflow focuses on outgoing digital payments, including vendor ACH transfers, wires and electronic bill payments. It does not depend on employee behavior monitoring, device tracking or analyzing whether an email sounds fraudulent.

A transaction-level review asks whether the payment matches the practice’s approved financial records:

  • Does the same invoice already appear in a paid or pending batch?
  • Is the amount unusual for this vendor and purchase category?
  • Have the destination bank details changed since the last verified payment?
  • Is the payment coming from the correct legal entity’s account?
  • Does the proposed amount agree with the invoice, purchase authorization and any applicable credits?

Keep incoming payer receipts separate from this workflow. An unexpected payer deposit may require reconciliation, but it is not the same decision as authorizing money to leave the practice.

Rules and AI serve different purposes

A fixed rule can flag an exact duplicate invoice number or require verification whenever vendor banking details change. An anomaly model may identify less obvious relationships, such as a near-duplicate invoice with different punctuation or an amount that differs sharply from comparable payments.

Neither approach establishes intent. A duplicate could result from a clerical error, while an unusual amount could reflect an approved equipment purchase. Use detection to identify questions, not to accuse a vendor or employee.

Build a Reliable Payment Comparison File

Start with approved invoices, pending payment batches, payment history and the vendor master record—the maintained list of approved vendors and their payment details. Include canceled, failed and reversed payments with clear status labels so they are not mistaken for completed disbursements.

Useful fields include:

Field Review purpose
Vendor identifier and invoice number Find exact and near-duplicate submissions
Invoice amount, date and payment status Distinguish new obligations from already-paid items
Paying entity and location allocation Check the source account and expense assignment
Payment method and scheduled release Determine how and when an unresolved item must be stopped
Approved bank-detail reference and change status Identify a destination that requires independent verification
Purchase authorization and reviewer Connect the payment to supporting approval

Normalize vendor names and invoice formatting before comparing records. Otherwise, the same supplier under slightly different names can hide a duplicate or create unnecessary alerts.

For bank-detail comparisons, use a controlled vendor record and an appropriate reference rather than copying full account numbers into a general review spreadsheet. Keep sensitive payment instructions in the practice’s approved payment environment.

Clean bookkeeping supports this process. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping explains the importance of complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices support payment review; they do not establish that any particular detection tool works.

Which Exceptions Require Investigation Before Release?

Set written review criteria before introducing a model. Some conditions should require a hold regardless of whether an algorithm considers them unusual.

Exception Why it deserves attention Evidence needed before release
Possible duplicate disbursement The same obligation may be paid twice Invoice comparison, prior payment status and any separate purchase documentation
Unusual payment amount The amount may reflect an error, unauthorized purchase or legitimate change Invoice, contract or purchase approval, plus an explanation of the variance
Changed vendor bank details A valid invoice could be redirected to an unauthorized account Independent confirmation through a previously established contact channel
Wrong paying entity Funds may leave an account that should not bear the obligation Correct contracting entity, source account and any approved shared-cost treatment
Multiple similar payments in one batch A duplicate may be split across entries or mixed with legitimate installments Invoice schedule, payment history and approval for each obligation

Duplicate disbursements

Compare proposed payments against both completed payments and other pending batches. Checking only the bank register misses duplicates that have not yet cleared.

Matching should consider vendor, invoice number, amount and invoice date together. Two identical amounts are not necessarily duplicates: recurring rent or separate supply orders may legitimately match. Conversely, a changed invoice suffix does not necessarily create a new obligation.

Unusual amounts

Compare like with like. A routine supply invoice should not share the same baseline as an equipment purchase simply because both come from one vendor. New locations, annual renewals and approved bulk orders can also change normal spending patterns.

The reviewer should establish what was purchased, who authorized it and whether the amount agrees with the supporting records. “The model flagged it” is not a sufficient explanation for withholding payment indefinitely.

Changed bank details

Treat a new destination as a separate verification issue, even when the invoice amount is ordinary. A familiar vendor name does not validate a changed bank account.

Use a previously verified phone number or established vendor contact—not a number supplied only in the change request. Confirm the change through the practice’s approved process, record the verification and require an independent approval before releasing payment. If verification is incomplete, keep the payment on hold.

A Hypothetical Payment-Exception Queue

Illustrative example: All vendors, identifiers, amounts and circumstances below are synthetic. No patient information is included. A practice manager reviews a proposed payment batch before release.

Exception Proposed payment Assigned reviewer Initial decision and required resolution
Duplicate candidate $2,460 to Supply Vendor A; invoice resembles a paid item Accounts-payable preparer investigates; practice manager approves resolution Hold candidate; compare invoices and confirm whether the earlier payment settled
Amount anomaly $8,900 to Equipment Vendor B, above its routine service charges Operations manager verifies purchase; owner approves payment Hold pending equipment authorization and invoice match
Bank-detail change $1,750 to Cleaning Vendor C using a new destination Manager not involved in the change verifies; owner approves Hold until independent confirmation and vendor-record approval
Entity mismatch $3,200 for Location B scheduled from Entity A’s account Finance lead checks obligation; authorized entity approver decides Hold pending correction or documented approval of the appropriate payment treatment

Every queue entry should also show its payment deadline, current status, evidence location, final approver and resolution date. Assign a backup reviewer so an absence does not leave urgent exceptions unattended.

An alert alone does not stop a scheduled transfer. Confirm whether the payment platform can place an item on hold. If it cannot, remove the unresolved item from the release batch and follow the provider’s process for stopping any already-scheduled instruction.

Separate Preparation, Verification and Release

The person who enters payment details should not be the only person who verifies and releases a flagged payment. In particular, avoid allowing one person to change vendor banking details, approve that change and release the resulting payment without independent review.

A workable division for a staffed practice is:

  • Payment preparer: Assembles the batch and attaches supporting invoices.
  • Exception reviewer: Investigates the alert and records the evidence.
  • Authorized approver: Accepts the documented resolution and authorizes release.
  • Reconciler: Compares completed payments with bank activity and accounting records.

Smaller teams may need overlapping responsibilities. Where full separation is impractical, add an independent owner review of sensitive changes and payment releases. Reconciliation afterward remains important, but it does not replace verification before funds move.

For provider and administrative payroll, use a separate, restricted review process. Investigate unexplained differences from the approved payroll register and verify sensitive payment changes through the established payroll workflow. Avoid distributing employee bank or compensation details through a broad vendor-exception queue.

Document the Resolution, Not Just the Alert

Close each exception with a clear outcome: approved as valid, corrected, duplicate canceled, unresolved and held, or escalated as suspected fraud.

The record should explain:

  1. What triggered the exception.
  2. Which records the reviewer compared.
  3. How any changed payment instructions were independently verified.
  4. What correction or explanation resolved the issue.
  5. Who authorized release or cancellation, and when.

For a legitimate unusual purchase, retain the purchase authorization and explanation. For a duplicate, connect the canceled entry to the valid invoice and payment. For an entity error, preserve the correction and expense-allocation support.

If a suspicious payment has already been released, contact the bank or payment provider promptly through an established channel to ask about available stop or recall options. Preserve the relevant records and follow the practice’s escalation process. Recovery is not guaranteed.

Test Detection Without Giving It Payment Authority

Begin with a limited review of historical transactions and synthetic test records. Then consider a shadow review of proposed payments: the tool produces alerts, while existing approval controls remain in place.

Ask practical questions before relying on a system:

  • Does it check pending payments as well as completed ones?
  • Can reviewers see the transaction details behind an alert?
  • Can it distinguish entities and purchase categories?
  • Who owns each exception, and what happens when that person is unavailable?
  • Can unresolved items actually be withheld from release?
  • How are corrected records and reviewer outcomes incorporated into later reviews?

Track alert volume, review time, confirmed duplicate or entry errors, legitimate exceptions and issues discovered outside the queue. A low alert count does not prove that payments are safe. Excessive alerts can also overwhelm reviewers.

New practices and newly opened locations may have too little history for meaningful comparisons. Keep explicit controls for duplicates and bank changes rather than relying entirely on a learned baseline. Do not remove mandatory verification merely to reduce alerts.

Keep Patient Information Out of Testing

Use synthetic financial records to test the workflow. Vendor-payment checks generally do not need patient names, diagnoses, dates of service or patient account details. Review invoice attachments and free-text descriptions as well as spreadsheet columns for unnecessary information.

Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool. If a proposed service would handle protected health information, assess the applicable requirements and arrangements before use. The HHS explanation of covered entities and business associates provides relevant background; it is not a certification of any vendor.

The Owner’s Priority: A Controlled Release Decision

The strongest workflow connects a transaction signal to a named reviewer, independent evidence and an authorized release decision. AI can assist with prioritization, but it cannot replace that accountability.

FAQs

Does an AI payment alert mean someone committed fraud?

No. It means a transaction meets a rule or differs from the comparison data. Duplicate entry, an approved equipment purchase or a legitimate bank change can all produce alerts. Investigate the underlying obligation and payment instructions before deciding how to resolve it.

Which exceptions should a medical practice hold before payment?

Require investigation for unresolved duplicate candidates, unverified bank-detail changes, unsupported unusual amounts and incorrect paying entities. Establish written criteria and an authorized release process. A low model score should not bypass a mandatory verification control.

Can a small practice use this process without AI?

Yes. A pending-payment register, duplicate checks, independent bank-change verification and separated approval responsibilities provide a useful starting point. AI-assisted comparison may help prioritize complex records, but it does not replace these controls.

What should a reviewer record before closing an exception?

Record the trigger, evidence examined, verification method, correction or explanation, final decision and authorized approver. Keep unresolved items visibly on hold rather than marking them complete because someone has started investigating.
