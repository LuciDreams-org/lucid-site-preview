Checklist: Align Medical Practice Exit Plans With Investor Returns

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A proposed sale price is not the same as cash available to a medical practice’s owners. Debt, transaction expenses, retained operating cash, holdbacks and contingent payments can change both the amount and timing of proceeds. Ownership documents may also require an allocation that differs from a simple percentage split.

For owners and managers of staffed US private medical practices, the immediate task is to build a documented picture of exit economics before discussing a sale, partner buyout or recapitalization with existing equity holders and licensed advisers.

Use this checklist to produce four working documents:

  • An ownership and invested-capital schedule.
  • A reconciled distribution and owner-loan history.
  • A gross-to-net proceeds bridge with a holder-level allocation.
  • An unresolved-questions register identifying who must review each assumption.

These documents support informed discussions; they do not establish a valuation, select a transaction structure or promise an investor return. Legal rights, ownership eligibility and tax outcomes require review by qualified professionals familiar with the practice and relevant jurisdictions.

1. Define Exactly What the Exit Discussion Covers

Before calculating returns, write down which entities, interests and assets are in scope. A practice with several locations may have one operating entity, multiple professional entities, a separate management entity or separately owned real estate. A consolidated financial report does not establish that all of those interests belong to the same owners or are included in a proposed transaction.

Checklist item What to document Question to resolve
Transaction scope Entities, ownership interests and assets under discussion Is the discussion about the entire practice or only a particular holder’s interest?
Locations Locations operated by each entity Are all locations included, and where are shared expenses recorded?
Property and equipment Ownership, financing and any separately held assets Are these included, excluded or subject to separate arrangements?
Financial cutoff Reporting date and expected closing assumptions Which balances are actual, estimated or still unreconciled?
Consideration Cash, deferred payments, contingent proceeds and retained or rollover equity Which amounts could actually become spendable cash, and when?

Keep a dated assumptions sheet. If transaction terms are preliminary, label them as preliminary rather than treating them as agreed economics.

2. Verify Ownership Against Executed Documents

Create an ownership schedule for each relevant entity. Collect executed operating or shareholder agreements, amendments, buy-sell agreements, subscription or purchase documents, transfer records and applicable approvals.

For each holder, record:

  • Legal name and the entity in which the interest is held.
  • Units, shares or other documented ownership interests.
  • Economic rights and voting rights, recorded separately if they differ.
  • Acquisition dates and supporting contribution or purchase records.
  • Any documented redemption, transfer, payment-priority or distribution provisions.
  • Unresolved differences between legal documents, accounting records and internal ownership spreadsheets.

Do not assume a capital-account balance equals ownership percentage, tax basis or an entitlement to sale proceeds. Likewise, a historical distribution percentage does not necessarily establish the allocation required at exit.

Have counsel confirm which documents control, what approvals are needed and whether ownership or transfer restrictions affect the discussion. Do not assume that any prospective holder is eligible to own a medical practice interest.

3. Reconcile Invested Capital, Distributions and Owner Loans

A holder’s recollection of “what I put in” may combine equity contributions, loans, personally paid expenses and amounts paid directly to a departing partner. Separate those categories before calculating returns.

Record category Supporting records Treatment in the working schedule
Contributions to the entity Executed documents, bank deposits and ledger entries Record by holder, entity and date
Purchases from another holder Purchase agreement and payment evidence Distinguish from cash contributed to the practice
Owner loans Loan documents, advances, repayments and interest records Track separately from equity
Distributions Bank activity, general ledger and distribution approvals Record recipient, date, amount and stated classification
Compensation Provider payroll, bonuses and related records Keep separate from investment returns
Expense reimbursements Expense support and payment records Do not automatically classify as distributions

Reconcile these schedules to entity-level books and bank records. The IRS guidance on starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those records provide a foundation for the analysis; they do not determine a holder’s legal payout rights.

If historical classifications are uncertain, flag them for CPA review. Do not silently reclassify payments to make a return calculation fit an expectation.

4. Establish the Practice’s Financial Starting Point

Use a reconciled balance sheet and current financial statements as the starting point for the proceeds model. Ask the CPA and transaction advisers which accounting basis and additional schedules are appropriate; do not assume every private practice transaction requires the same financial reporting package.

For a staffed practice, review:

  • Bank balances, restricted amounts and outstanding payments.
  • Payer receipts and patient balances in aggregate, including questions about collectibility and post-closing collections.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, accrued obligations and other operating liabilities.
  • Equipment debt, credit lines, owner loans and intercompany balances.
  • Per-location results and the allocation of shared administrative expenses where relevant.
  • Transaction expenses already paid and amounts still expected.

Document who receives collections attributable to pre-closing services and who bears related obligations under the proposed terms. Do not assume all receivables convert to cash at closing.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. Avoid placing patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Any necessary sharing of patient information requires an appropriately reviewed workflow; financial due diligence does not justify indiscriminate disclosure.

5. Build a Gross-to-Net Proceeds Bridge

A proceeds bridge explains how a proposed headline amount becomes estimated cash available for distribution. It should identify each adjustment, its source and whether it is confirmed or assumed.

Illustrative example: practice-level proceeds

The following is an illustrative example only, not a valuation, recommended reserve or expected transaction outcome. It assumes fixed consideration of $4,000,000, including a holdback, plus a separate potential contingent payment. All deductions are assumed to be separate and not already reflected elsewhere.

Bridge item Illustrative amount Explanation
Fixed gross consideration $4,000,000 Assumed fixed amount before the adjustments below
Less debt repayment ($600,000) Assumed lender payoff; actual payoff statements are needed
Less seller transaction costs ($150,000) Assumed legal, accounting and other seller expenses
Less closing adjustment ($100,000) Assumed reduction under the proposed closing calculation
Less operating cash retained ($200,000) Cash assumed to remain in the entity rather than be distributed
Less holdback ($300,000) Included in fixed consideration but unavailable at closing
Estimated cash available for distribution at closing $2,650,000 Before holder-specific adjustments and taxes
Potential later holdback release Up to $300,000 Subject to claims, deductions and release terms
Separate contingent consideration Up to $500,000 Not included in fixed consideration; conditions may not be met

The retained operating cash is not necessarily an expense or a permanent loss. It is excluded from immediate distributions in this example, and its ultimate treatment must be documented. Similarly, a holdback is not cash available today simply because it appears in the headline consideration.

Check for double counting. A liability should not reduce proceeds once through a closing adjustment and again through a separate deduction unless the documents require distinct amounts. Review entity-level taxes with the CPA and show any resulting adjustments explicitly; this example includes no tax estimate.

6. Reconcile Each Holder’s Allocation to Governing Documents

The practice-level bridge is only the first calculation. Next, prepare a distribution waterfall: the sequence in which available proceeds are allocated under the applicable documents and transaction terms.

Use this checklist with counsel and the CPA:

  • Confirm the ownership schedule agrees with executed documents.
  • Identify any required repayment, priority or other economic provision before allocating residual proceeds.
  • Separate owner-loan repayment from equity proceeds.
  • Confirm how transaction expenses, reserves and closing adjustments affect each holder.
  • Document who bears holdback claims and who receives later releases.
  • Identify who is entitled to contingent payments and under what conditions.
  • Record retained or rollover equity separately from cash proceeds.
  • Confirm that all holder allocations reconcile to the practice-level bridge.
  • Mark unreviewed provisions as unresolved rather than assuming equal or pro rata treatment.

Continuing the illustrative example: if the reviewed documents require a simple 60%/40% allocation of the $2,650,000 closing distribution, the holders would receive $1,590,000 and $1,060,000 before their individual taxes. This assumes no priorities, separate loan repayments or holder-specific deductions. It does not establish how a real practice should allocate proceeds.

7. Put Return Expectations and Payment Timing on the Same Page

Ask each holder to state the return measure and timing assumption they are using. One may be discussing total cash received since investing; another may mean closing proceeds alone. Neither should be compared without a consistent definition.

A simple cash multiple compares included cash receipts with documented cash invested. A simple cash return percentage compares those receipts, less invested cash, with invested cash. Both depend on what is included and neither measures annualized performance. An annualized analysis requires dated cash flows and professional review.

Keep salary, clinical compensation and reimbursements outside investment-return calculations unless advisers identify a specific reason to include them. Do not treat unpaid contingent proceeds or retained equity as realized cash.

Holder-level field What to record
Investment history Amounts, dates and whether funds went to the entity or another holder
Historical distributions Reconciled receipts and agreed classification
Closing cash Allocation tied to the proceeds bridge
Deferred or contingent receipts Conditions, deductions, payment assumptions and uncertainty
Retained equity Noncash interest shown separately, without assumed future liquidity
Expected liquidity date Holder’s stated expectation compared with documented payment terms
Tax treatment CPA-reviewed assumptions, clearly separated from pretax proceeds

A mismatch between expected and documented timing is an issue to resolve, not a reason to change the spreadsheet until expectations appear satisfied. A payment schedule also does not guarantee payment.

8. Assign Every Unresolved Question

Circulate a version-dated review packet containing the ownership schedule, financial reconciliation, proceeds bridge, holder allocations and supporting assumptions. Keep a separate issue register so uncertain items do not disappear into formulas.

Open question Review lead Required evidence or decision
Ownership records disagree Legal counsel Confirm controlling documents and corrections
Historical payment classification is unclear CPA Reconcile support and determine appropriate accounting treatment
Debt payoff is estimated Finance lead and lender Obtain current payoff information
Holdback allocation is unclear Legal counsel Confirm entitlement and responsibility for claims
After-tax proceeds are unknown Tax adviser Review entity and holder-specific facts
Expected payment date lacks support Owner and transaction advisers Identify the relevant provision or label the date as an assumption

Refresh the packet when terms, debt balances, distributions or expected costs change. Preserve a short change log so reviewers can distinguish new assumptions from previously reviewed figures.

FAQs

Does an ownership percentage determine a medical practice owner’s exit proceeds?

Not by itself. Governing documents, debt, owner loans, transaction expenses, closing adjustments and payment provisions can affect the result. Counsel should confirm legal rights before the finance team applies a percentage to distributable proceeds.

Should historical distributions count toward an investor’s return?

They can be included in a clearly defined cash-return analysis after reconciliation and classification review. Keep them separate from compensation, reimbursements and loan repayments. Historical distributions do not automatically reduce a holder’s legal entitlement to exit proceeds; that requires document review.

How should a practice show an earnout or other contingent payment?

Show it separately from fixed consideration and closing cash. Record the conditions, measurement period, payment assumptions, potential deductions and eligible recipients. Include a scenario in which no contingent payment is received, and do not present the maximum amount as assured proceeds.

Can this checklist establish after-tax proceeds?

No. It organizes the financial facts needed for tax analysis. Entity-level and holder-level outcomes depend on the actual transaction and individual circumstances. Have a qualified tax adviser review those facts before treating an estimate as available personal cash.
