How Inflation Affects Medical Practice Cash Flow

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can maintain its visit volume and collections yet have less cash available for the next payroll. When wages, rent, supplies and other operating costs rise before receipts increase, the practice retains less of every dollar collected.

For owners of staffed private practices, the response is not simply to raise prices. Contracted reimbursement may not change when expenses do, and a higher charge does not necessarily produce a higher payment. Patient collections also have their own timing and uncertainty.

The practical task is to identify which costs will change, when the higher payments will leave the bank and what decisions remain available before then. An inflation exposure register, paired with a rolling cash forecast, makes those decisions visible.

Separate Expense Inflation From Collection Delays

Several pressures can reduce cash at the same time, but they require different responses.

Pressure What changes What the practice should investigate
Expense inflation The price paid for the same staffing, space or supplies increases Rate changes, renewal terms and the first affected payment
Payment timing Expected receipts arrive later, or vendors require payment sooner Payer receipt patterns, patient balance collections and supplier terms
Revenue constraints Collections cannot immediately increase to match expenses Contracted reimbursement, payer mix and realistic effective dates for any changes
Seasonal variation Receipts or spending follow a recurring calendar pattern Prior-period patterns, provider schedules and recurring annual bills
Operational change Spending rises because the practice uses more resources Staffing hours, purchase quantities, service mix and new locations

A larger supply bill, for example, might reflect higher unit prices, more purchases or both. Comparing total spending alone will not tell the owner which response is appropriate.

Likewise, a delayed payer deposit is not proof of inflation. Put the delay in the cash forecast, but investigate it separately. Otherwise, the practice may cut recurring costs to solve a temporary timing gap—or borrow to cover a recurring operating deficit.

Energy costs can contribute to the problem, but an inflation review should cover the practice’s full expense base rather than utilities alone.

Build an Inflation Exposure Register

Start with payroll reports, vendor agreements, lease documents, renewal notices and recent invoices. List the costs that could change over the planning period, including changes already approved but not yet paid.

The register should connect each exposure to an accountable reviewer. A generic “expenses may rise” assumption is less useful than a named person confirming a specific renewal before the notice deadline.

The following is an illustrative planning template, not a statement of any practice’s contractual terms.

Cost category Renewal or effective date to record Contractual terms to check Cash timing to model Accountable reviewer
Provider and administrative payroll Approved compensation changes, hiring dates and benefit renewals Employment agreements, compensation formulas and benefit notices First affected payroll, related employer costs and benefit payments Owner and payroll lead
Rent and occupancy Lease escalation and renewal dates Fixed increases, adjustment clauses, shared building charges and notice provisions First higher rent payment and any reconciliation bill Practice administrator
Medical and office supplies Quote expiration and contract review dates Unit prices, minimum orders, freight and payment terms Order date, invoice due date and required deposits Purchasing lead
Software and outsourced services Subscription and service renewal dates Price changes, user counts, cancellation notice and annual billing Renewal payment or first revised monthly invoice Administrator and finance lead
Insurance Policy renewal dates Premium quote, coverage changes and payment options Deposit, installment schedule or annual payment Owner and insurance contact
Equipment and financing Service renewals and loan reset dates, if applicable Maintenance terms, rate provisions and payment schedules Revised service payments, installments or required purchases Finance lead

For each item, add the current cost, proposed cost, supporting document, confidence level and next action. Distinguish a signed increase from a preliminary quote or an internal estimate.

Separate Price Changes From Volume Changes

For supplies, compare unit prices and quantities. For payroll, separate wage changes from added hours, overtime, new positions and changes in provider compensation. Include associated employer costs rather than forecasting only take-home pay.

This distinction matters when choosing an action. Reducing waste may address excess purchasing, but it does not remove a contractual unit-price increase. Delaying a hire may conserve cash, but it is a staffing decision with operational consequences—not a correction to an inaccurate inflation assumption.

Translate Each Increase Into the Cash Forecast

Use a rolling weekly forecast for near-term payment decisions, with a monthly view extending through important renewals. A 13-week view is a practical starting point, but it will not capture a lease increase or benefit renewal that occurs later unless the longer view includes it.

Start With Reconciled Cash and Expected Receipts

Use bank balances reconciled to the books, then identify cash already committed to outstanding payments or other obligations. Do not treat all money visible in an account as available for discretionary spending.

The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Those practices also provide a more dependable starting point for cash planning.

Forecast payer receipts and patient collections separately where their timing differs. Use expected cash receipts, not billed charges or uncollected balances. Keep unconfirmed reimbursement improvements outside the base case.

Apply Changes When the Money Leaves the Account

A compensation increase may take effect before the first higher payroll clears. A supplier’s higher price may affect cash only after existing inventory is used and a new invoice becomes due. An annual subscription may create one large payment rather than twelve equal monthly outflows.

Record both the effective date and payment date. Also avoid counting the same increase twice—for example, once in an updated payroll schedule and again as a general expense inflation adjustment.

Illustrative Example: Staggered Increases With Unchanged Receipts

Assume a practice starts with $90,000 in cash. Monthly receipts remain $250,000, and baseline monthly cash outflows are $235,000. A payroll increase adds $4,000 per month beginning in Month 1, rent adds $1,500 beginning in Month 2, and higher supply costs add $1,000 beginning in Month 3.

These are illustrative figures, not benchmarks or a case study. The table summarizes monthly totals; an operating forecast should also show the payment weeks.

Cash forecast item Month 1 Month 2 Month 3
Opening cash $90,000 $101,000 $110,500
Cash receipts $250,000 $250,000 $250,000
Baseline cash outflows $235,000 $235,000 $235,000
Additional payroll outflow $4,000 $4,000 $4,000
Additional rent outflow $0 $1,500 $1,500
Additional supply outflow $0 $0 $1,000
Net cash generated $11,000 $9,500 $8,500
Closing cash $101,000 $110,500 $119,000

Without the increases, closing cash after three months would be $135,000. With them, it is $119,000: $16,000 less cash accumulated despite unchanged receipts.

The practice remains cash-positive in this example, but its capacity to fund equipment, distributions or unexpected costs has narrowed. A monthly closing balance can also conceal a low point before a large payer deposit arrives, which is why weekly timing still matters.

Do Not Assume Revenue Can Catch Up Immediately

Review revenue options separately from expense assumptions. A higher fee schedule does not necessarily change contracted payments, and a proposed reimbursement change is not cash until its terms, effective date and collection timing are understood.

For each potential revenue improvement, document:

  • What can change under the applicable arrangement.
  • Who must approve or confirm it.
  • When the change could take effect.
  • When additional cash could reasonably arrive.
  • What remains uncertain.

Administrative collection improvements may help, but distinguish collecting existing receivables sooner from generating additional revenue. Moving a receipt forward can relieve a temporary cash gap without fixing an ongoing mismatch between collections and expenses.

For patient balances, use aggregate collection assumptions appropriate to the practice’s experience. Do not assume every outstanding balance will be collected in full or immediately.

Use the Forecast to Guide Vendor Discussions

Approach vendors before renewal or purchase commitments, with specific questions rather than a general request for a discount.

Ask whether the proposed increase changes unit prices, service scope, order minimums or payment terms. Request a written effective date. Where appropriate, discuss phased increases, different order schedules, alternative service levels or revised payment arrangements.

Evaluate the full cash trade-off:

  • A lower unit price may require a larger upfront order.
  • An annual prepayment may reduce total expense while worsening near-term liquidity.
  • Longer terms may help timing without reducing the underlying cost.
  • A cheaper substitute may require operational review before use.

Do not assume payment terms can be extended unilaterally. Model agreed changes, and keep unconfirmed concessions in a separate scenario. Record the decision and update both the exposure register and forecast.

Set Reserve and Financing Decisions Around Actual Obligations

There is no single reserve balance suitable for every private practice. Consider payroll commitments, receipt variability, upcoming renewals, equipment needs, debt payments and access to financing.

Test at least three views: confirmed cost changes, plausible additional increases supported by quotes or renewal information, and a combined case that includes collection delays. Keep the delay assumptions separate so the owner can see which pressure causes the shortfall.

Use these results to establish practice-specific review triggers, such as a projected balance insufficient for the next payroll and committed bills, or a reserve draw that does not recover under the operating plan. Assign an action owner and decision date rather than prescribing a universal cash threshold.

If financing is being considered, distinguish a temporary timing need from a persistent operating shortfall. Review actual loan terms, repayment capacity and the effect of new payments. The Federal Reserve explains how monetary policy can influence borrowing costs, but that does not establish how a particular practice loan will change. Use the agreement and lender information for that forecast.

Keep Location and Entity Decisions Visible

For multi-location practices, compare cost changes by location before allocating shared expenses. A rent increase at one office should not disappear inside a consolidated total. Use a consistent, documented approach for shared administrative costs so comparisons remain useful.

For multi-entity owners, show where cash is held and which entity owes each payment. Consolidated cash is not automatically available to every entity; review proposed transfers with the appropriate advisers.

A workable review rhythm is to update near-term cash weekly, reconcile actual results monthly and revisit the exposure register when quotes, contracts or staffing plans change. Investigate differences between forecast and actual results instead of repeatedly carrying forward stale estimates.

Conclusion

Inflation planning is about the sequence of cash commitments, not just an annual expense assumption. Identify each increase, confirm its terms, assign a reviewer and place the payment in the forecast.

Then decide what the practice can change before the cash leaves: vendor terms, purchase timing, discretionary spending, distributions or financing plans. Keeping expense inflation separate from collection delays and seasonal patterns helps the owner choose a response that addresses the actual problem.

FAQs

Can inflation hurt medical practice cash flow even when collections are stable?

Yes. If payroll, rent or supplier payments rise while collections remain unchanged, the practice generates less cash from operations. The effect depends on when each increase begins and when the higher payments are due—not just the annual expense total.

Should a medical practice raise prices to offset inflation?

Not automatically. Contracted reimbursement and other applicable arrangements may limit what a price change produces in collections. Review the relevant terms and expected payment timing before including additional receipts in the base forecast. A higher listed charge alone is not evidence of higher cash receipts.

How can an owner tell whether a cash gap is inflation-driven or seasonal?

Compare unit prices, compensation rates and contractual increases with prior periods, then examine receipt timing and recurring calendar patterns separately. Inflation raises the cost of comparable resources; seasonality changes when activity, receipts or payments occur. Both can affect the same forecast, but they should have separate assumptions.

How much cash should a practice reserve for inflation?

Set the reserve decision around the practice’s obligations and forecast rather than a universal percentage. Test confirmed increases, uncertain renewals and collection delays. Consider payroll, essential vendors, debt service and committed purchases, then agree on review triggers with the finance team.
