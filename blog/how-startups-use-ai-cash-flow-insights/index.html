Using AI to Explain Cash Flow Drivers in Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A profitable month does not always leave a medical practice with more money in the bank. Payer receipts may arrive after revenue is recorded, payroll liabilities may be paid down, equipment purchases may consume cash, and owner distributions may reduce balances without appearing as operating expenses.

The owner's question is not simply, “Where did we spend more?” It is: “What explains the change in cash, which explanations can we verify, and what should we do about them?”

AI can help organize reconciled financial data and draft explanations. It cannot independently establish why a shortfall occurred. A useful month-end review connects every material explanation to verified records, distinguishes timing from underlying performance, and leaves unresolved questions visible.

Define the Review: Explain What Already Happened

This workflow starts after the books are reconciled. Its purpose is to explain the movement from beginning cash to ending cash for a completed period.

It differs from other cash-management activities:

Activity Main question Primary output
Current-transaction monitoring What transactions are appearing now? Recent activity and items needing attention
Cash dashboard What do the selected measures show? Balances, trends, and summary metrics
Cash forecasting What might happen next under stated assumptions? Projected receipts, payments, and balances
Month-end cash-driver analysis Why did cash change during the closed period? Reconciled profit-to-cash bridge and supported explanations

A dashboard can show that cash declined. A forecast can help assess whether the practice can fund upcoming payroll. Neither replaces an explanation of the completed month's cash movement.

The diagnostic review should inform decisions: whether to investigate collections, adjust purchasing, review distributions, or update a forecast. It should not turn every cash decline into a recommendation to cut staff or reduce operating expenses.

Prepare a Reliable Evidence Package

Before asking AI to explain anything, establish the reporting period, accounting basis, and accounts included in the review.

For a practice using several entities, start with each entity's bank accounts and books. For a multi-location practice within one entity, distinguish location-level operating results from centrally held cash. A location P&L does not necessarily show how much cash that location generated or can spend.

The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, separating business accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those practices provide the foundation for this review—not proof that an AI-generated explanation is correct.

Assemble the records behind the numbers

Evidence What it helps establish
Reconciled bank balances and cash general ledger Beginning cash, ending cash, and recorded movement
P&L and comparative balance sheets Profit, noncash charges, and changes in operating assets and liabilities
Aggregate receivables rollforward and collection reports Revenue-to-receipt differences, adjustments, and outstanding balances
Payroll registers, liability accounts, and payment confirmations Provider/admin payroll expense versus cash payment timing
Vendor invoices, accounts payable detail, and payment records Current expense versus payment of earlier obligations
Loan statements and equipment invoices Principal, interest, and capital purchases
Equity and intercompany account detail Contributions, distributions, and transfers between entities

Resolve missing accounts, duplicate entries, and material uncategorized transactions first. Reconcile payment-processor settlements and outstanding checks as applicable; a bank download alone is not a completed reconciliation.

Keep the cash definition consistent. Transfers between included bank accounts do not change total cash. For a combined view of several entities, identify and eliminate internal transfers rather than treating them as outside receipts or expenses.

Confirm the accounting basis

The bridge below assumes accrual-basis financial statements. If the practice reports on a cash or modified-cash basis, have the accountant adapt it. Do not add accrual-style receivable and payable adjustments to cash-basis profit without checking how those items are recorded.

Build a Profit-to-Cash Bridge Before Drafting the Story

For an accrual-basis practice, the bridge generally starts with net income, adjusts for noncash items and operating balance-sheet changes, then accounts for investing and financing cash flows.

Key distinctions include:

  • Receivables: An increase generally means recognized revenue exceeded collections, after accounting for applicable adjustments.
  • Payables and accrued payroll: A decrease can mean the practice paid obligations expensed in an earlier period.
  • Equipment: A capital purchase uses cash but is not necessarily expensed in full that month.
  • Debt: Principal repayment reduces cash without reducing net income; interest is generally already included in profit.
  • Owner distributions: Distributions reduce cash but are not operating expenses. Confirm their classification rather than grouping all owner payments together.

The bridge must include all relevant accounts. If it does not tie to reconciled cash, investigate the difference instead of asking AI to invent an “other” explanation.

Illustrative example: profitable month, declining cash

All amounts and circumstances in this example are illustrative, not a case study or benchmark. Assume an accrual-basis practice has no cash movements beyond those listed.

Bridge item Cash effect Running amount
Reported net income $60,000 $60,000
Add back depreciation +$10,000 $70,000
Increase in net accounts receivable −$35,000 $35,000
Decrease in accounts payable −$25,000 $10,000
Decrease in accrued payroll liabilities −$20,000 −$10,000
Equipment purchased with cash −$30,000 −$40,000
Debt principal repaid −$8,000 −$48,000
Owner distributions −$22,000 −$70,000

Beginning cash of $200,000, less the $70,000 decline, produces ending cash of $130,000. Both balances must agree to the reconciled records.

This bridge explains why positive profit did not produce positive cash flow. It does not yet establish why receivables increased or whether the practice's spending decisions were appropriate.

Use AI to Group Records and Draft Testable Explanations

AI assistance is most useful after a finance professional has prepared the reconciled inputs and bridge.

A controlled workflow can use AI to:

  1. Propose groupings for transactions, such as payer receipts, patient collections, payroll, vendors, debt, capital purchases, and distributions.
  2. Identify records that may need review, such as unusual payment timing or inconsistent classifications.
  3. Draft plain-language commentary from verified bridge amounts.
  4. List missing evidence and questions for the practice manager or accountant.

Treat the proposed groupings as suggestions. A bank description may not reliably distinguish a loan payment from an equipment purchase, or an owner reimbursement from a distribution. Human review must resolve those classifications.

Require evidence links for every explanation

Maintain a source register with the statement being made, the amount, the underlying record links, and the reviewer. Use links to actual records in the practice's approved accounting or document environment—not fabricated citations or AI-generated record references.

For example, “vendor payments reduced cash” needs payment records. “Those payments cleared prior-month liabilities” also needs invoice dates, payable detail, and the relevant ledger entries.

A useful drafting instruction is:

Use only the supplied reconciled amounts and verified record references. For each explanation, state the amount, accounting mechanism, supporting record links, and unresolved questions. Separate observed movement from possible causes. Do not infer payer behavior, staffing efficiency, or spending necessity from transaction timing alone.

The accountant should verify both the arithmetic and the meaning of the narrative before it reaches the owner.

Worked Review: An Apparent Expense Problem May Be Payment Timing

Continue the illustrative example above. The owner sees payroll and vendor cash outflows and asks whether operating expenses have become excessive.

The review finds that the $25,000 decrease in accounts payable represents payments against invoices already expensed in the prior month. The $20,000 decrease in accrued payroll liabilities represents settlement of previously recorded payroll obligations.

Together, these items used $45,000 of cash without creating $45,000 of additional current-month expense.

Initial concern Evidence to inspect Supported conclusion Decision implication
“Vendor costs suddenly increased.” Invoice periods, expense ledger, payable rollforward, and payment confirmations Part of the cash outflow settled earlier expenses Compare current-period costs before reducing purchasing
“Payroll is consuming more than the P&L shows.” Payroll registers, accrued liabilities, and bank settlement dates Earlier accruals were paid during this month Review payroll timing separately from staffing costs
“Collections are failing.” Receivables rollforward, aging, aggregate payer receipts, and subsequent deposits Receivables increased; the reason requires more evidence Investigate the change before attributing it to payer delays or denials

The owner should not conclude that everything is fine. Paying old obligations still consumes cash, and a timing effect may recur. But cutting staff would not directly address a cash decline caused by clearing prior-period liabilities.

The $35,000 receivables increase requires a separate review. It could reflect collection timing, higher recognized revenue, changes in adjustments, or unresolved collection issues. Confirm the rollforward and relevant records before selecting an explanation.

Separate correlation from causation

“Receivables increased during the same month a provider joined” is an observation. It does not establish that the provider caused a collection slowdown.

A stronger explanation identifies the mechanism and its evidence. For example, subsequent settlement records may support a conclusion that specific receipts crossed the month-end boundary. They do not prove that all outstanding balances will be collected.

Label conclusions as verified, supported but incomplete, or unresolved. Do not allow polished AI prose to erase uncertainty.

Turn the Review Into an Owner Decision Memo

The final deliverable can be concise even when the supporting work is detailed. Include:

  • Beginning cash, ending cash, and the reconciled change.
  • The profit-to-cash bridge.
  • Material drivers, each linked to verified records.
  • Whether each driver reflects operations, payment timing, investment, financing, or owner activity.
  • Open questions, responsible reviewers, and follow-up actions.

For multiple locations, explain shared-cost allocations consistently. A change in allocated administrative expense can alter a location's reported profit without changing practice-wide cash. For multiple entities, show entity-level liquidity before relying on combined totals; cash held in one entity should not simply be assumed available to another.

Actions should follow the evidence. A collections issue calls for operational follow-up. A large equipment payment calls for review of capital funding. Distribution-related pressure calls for an owner discussion. Only after explaining the completed period should the team update the cash forecast using appropriate assumptions.

Protect Financial and Patient Information

Use aggregate financial data wherever possible. Payer-level totals and summarized patient balances may be sufficient without names, account details, or clinical information.

Do not place patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool. Have the appropriate privacy, legal, or security reviewer assess any workflow that could involve protected health information. HHS guidance on business associates explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities when PHI is involved; it does not establish any provider's compliance status.

Build the Explanation on Clean Books

The value of AI-assisted cash analysis is a clearer, reviewable explanation—not an independent verdict on why cash fell.

FAQs

Can AI determine why my medical practice has less cash despite a profit?

AI can help group records and draft possible explanations, but it cannot independently establish the cause. A finance professional must reconcile the bridge, verify supporting records, and distinguish timing effects from operating problems.

How much historical data does this review require?

Start with the completed month's records, beginning and ending balance sheets, and supporting details for material movements. Earlier periods or subsequent receipts may be needed to test explanations. This diagnostic review does not depend on a universal minimum number of months for AI training.

Does a reconciled bridge prove there is no cash problem?

No. It explains the historical movement. The practice may still face a liquidity constraint, recurring collection delays, or distributions that leave insufficient operating cash. Use the verified findings to inform a separate forward-looking cash forecast.

Should owner distributions and loan payments appear as expenses?

Owner distributions and debt principal generally do not reduce operating profit, but they do reduce cash. Loan interest is generally recorded as expense. Have the accountant verify how each payment is classified, particularly when transactions combine principal, interest, reimbursements, or compensation.
