Data-Driven Scenario Model Design for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Before a private medical practice uses a financial model to approve another administrative position, the owner needs to know whether its calculations deserve trust. A detailed spreadsheet can still produce a misleading answer if it mixes cash receipts with earned revenue, treats missing hours as zero, or assumes a new hire will automatically increase collections.

Model design comes before scenario comparison. It establishes where inputs originate, how variables depend on one another, and whether those relationships explain historical results well enough for the proposed decision. This is different from choosing optimistic and conservative budgets or refreshing forecasts after new information arrives.

Consider a staffed, two-location practice deciding whether to add a shared administrative employee. The initial task is not to generate three financial outcomes. It is to build and test a model that connects documented administrative workload, staffing capacity, employment costs, and payment timing—without inventing revenue benefits.

1. Define the Decision Before Selecting Data

Write a short decision specification before building formulas:

  • Decision: Add shared administrative capacity, retain the current staffing arrangement, or postpone the commitment.
  • Scope: Both locations, with costs recorded in the employing entity and allocated to locations for management reporting.
  • Outputs: Incremental employment expense, remaining overtime or temporary coverage, monthly cash requirements, and location-level cost allocation.
  • Constraints: Minimum operating cash, coverage requirements, and management-approved staffing arrangements.
  • Excluded effects: Additional patient demand, provider productivity gains, or higher collections unless separately supported.

This boundary prevents a staffing-cost model from quietly becoming a revenue-growth model.

Keep two views distinct. The practice-wide incremental view measures whether total costs change. The location view shows where those costs are assigned. Moving an existing shared expense between locations changes their reported results but does not create a practice-wide saving.

2. Build an Input-Lineage Table

Input lineage means being able to trace a model value back to its source, definition, period, and transformation. Another reviewer should be able to reproduce the value without asking the model builder what it means.

For the administrative expansion decision, use a table such as this:

Input Source and accountable reviewer Definition and period Transformation or control Role in the model
Administrative workload units Dated operational export; practice manager Consistently defined completed administrative tasks by month and location Remove duplicates; separate task categories with materially different effort Explains variable administrative hours
Administrative hours Time records; payroll administrator Regular, overtime, and temporary hours by work period Separate leave, training, and one-time projects Calibrates workload-to-hours relationship
Employment expense Payroll reports and closed general ledger; finance reviewer Wages and applicable employer costs for the same work period Reconcile payroll totals and accounting adjustments Establishes existing and proposed staffing costs
Proposed position cost Approved hiring assumptions; owner and payroll reviewer Hours, compensation, benefits, and start date Document each component rather than applying an unexplained multiplier Calculates incremental expense
Cash receipts Bank reconciliation and receipt records; finance reviewer Payer and patient collections deposited during the period Explain transfers, refunds, and deposit timing differences Establishes baseline cash resources, not a hiring benefit
Shared-cost allocation Management allocation schedule; owner Documented basis for assigning shared staff costs Confirm allocated amounts equal the shared-cost total Produces location-level reporting
Opening cash and liabilities Reconciled entity accounts; finance reviewer Balances at the model start date Exclude interaccount transfers from operating receipts Connects expense estimates to cash requirements

For every input, also record the extraction date and source-file version. Avoid silently replacing historical files while the model is being tested.

Use aggregate operational data where possible. Patient names and clinical details are generally unnecessary for this analysis. Any workflow involving patient information should be reviewed for applicable privacy obligations; HHS provides guidance on the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions. Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

3. Choose a Historical Window That Represents the Decision

The longest available history is not automatically the best calibration window. Older data may reflect different locations, staffing responsibilities, systems, or task definitions.

Choose a window that captures relevant operating variation while remaining comparable to the proposed arrangement. Document:

  • Whether both locations operated throughout the period.
  • Whether administrative duties moved between staff groups.
  • Whether task-count definitions changed.
  • Whether holidays, absences, or unusually large projects affected hours.
  • Whether the data includes both ordinary and heavier-workload periods.

Illustrative window design: A practice might use 18 comparable months to estimate relationships and reserve the following three months for testing. Those durations are examples, not minimum requirements. If only a short comparable history exists, disclose that limitation rather than blending incompatible periods to create a larger dataset.

Do not discard inconvenient months merely because they weaken the model. Investigate them. A documented system conversion may warrant separate treatment; an ordinary busy month may be exactly the condition the model needs to represent.

Handle Missing Data Explicitly

A missing task count is not evidence that no work occurred. Classify each gap as a missing record, a confirmed zero, or a period that cannot be compared reliably.

Recover missing values from source records where possible. If an estimate is necessary, identify the method and flag the affected observations. Test whether excluding those observations materially changes the staffing conclusion. If it does, the model is not ready to support a firm commitment without better evidence.

4. Reconcile the Baseline to Closed Accounts

Before estimating future costs, prove that the baseline agrees with the practice's completed accounting records.

Reconcile payroll reports to general-ledger employment expense, explaining differences such as accruals, benefits, reimbursements, or posting timing. Reconcile opening cash to bank records. Tie location-level reports back to entity totals, and eliminate internal transfers when presenting a combined cash view.

Keep accounting periods and cash dates separate:

  • Hours worked explain staffing activity in the work period.
  • Employment expense belongs in the applicable accounting period under the practice's accounting method.
  • Payroll payments affect cash when paid.
  • Payer and patient receipts affect cash when collected, not necessarily when services occurred.

The IRS's business recordkeeping guidance supports complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. It does not validate a particular forecasting formula; that requires separate testing.

If the model cannot reproduce its starting balances and historical expenses, resolve the accounting differences before using its expansion estimates.

5. Draw the Driver-Dependency Map

A dependency map identifies which inputs drive which outputs. For this decision, a transparent structure might look like this:

Starting input Relationship Result Important limitation
Defined administrative task volume Fixed coverage hours plus variable hours per task Required administrative hours Task complexity and location coverage may matter
Required hours and usable scheduled capacity Compare requirements with available coverage Uncovered hours Total hours alone may hide scheduling conflicts
Uncovered hours Apply documented overtime or temporary-coverage arrangements Existing coverage cost Do not assume every hour is interchangeable
Proposed hire's schedule and start date Add usable capacity after onboarding Revised uncovered hours Paid hours are not all immediately available for routine work
Compensation and employer-cost assumptions Calculate recurring and one-time costs Incremental employment expense Include documented onboarding costs separately
Expense and payment schedules Assign cash outflows to payment periods Monthly cash requirement Expense timing and payment timing can differ
Shared employee cost and allocation basis Assign costs to locations Location-level expense Allocation does not reduce total cost

A candidate relationship is:

Required administrative hours = fixed coverage hours + task volume × hours per task

Treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a fact. Where task categories differ substantially, use separate categories rather than an opaque combined score. If location coverage imposes a staffing minimum, incorporate that requirement explicitly instead of assuming workload alone determines staffing.

Prevent Correlated Assumptions and Double Counting

Related assumptions should not move independently without justification. More staff capacity does not necessarily mean less overtime if the new employee cannot cover the relevant shifts or responsibilities.

Likewise, do not count both the new hire's full capacity and a separate unexplained productivity improvement. Do not add higher collections merely because administrative hours increase.

Hold payer and patient receipts independent of the hiring decision unless evidence supports a specific collection-timing relationship. Accelerating an existing receipt, if substantiated, changes cash timing; it is not automatically additional revenue.

6. Back-Test the Relationships Before Using Scenarios

Back-testing asks whether a model built from earlier information can explain a later period it was not fitted to. Freeze the formulas and assumptions before examining the reserved test period.

For a clean test, use only information that would have been available at each forecast date. Feeding actual later workload into an earlier forecast tests the hours relationship, but it does not test the ability to forecast workload.

Illustrative Back-Testing Worksheet

The following invented example demonstrates the worksheet structure; it is not practice performance evidence. Suppose the frozen relationship is 100 fixed hours plus 0.08 hours per workload unit. Workload estimates are recorded before each test month.

Reserved test month Workload estimate available at forecast date Predicted administrative hours Recorded administrative hours Error: predicted minus recorded Follow-up investigation
A 900 172 180 -8 Check workload estimate and task mix
B 1,000 180 176 +4 Review coverage and hour classifications
C 1,100 188 204 -16 Investigate missing tasks, unusual work, or capacity constraints

In this illustrative example, the mean absolute error is approximately 9.3 hours: the average of the absolute errors of 8, 4, and 16. That figure is not an accuracy guarantee or a universal acceptance threshold.

Ask whether errors are large enough to change the decision. An error that crosses an overtime or temporary-staffing boundary may matter more than a similar error during a well-covered month.

Separate Three Types of Error

  1. Input error: The workload estimate or source record was wrong.
  2. Relationship error: Workload was measured correctly, but the formula did not explain hours.
  3. Accounting or timing error: Costs were assigned to the wrong period, entity, or payment date.

After the original test, using actual workload can help diagnose the second category. Label that calculation as a diagnostic, not a successful advance forecast.

Compare the model with a simple benchmark, such as the prior comparable period's hours adjusted for known staffing changes. If a more complicated model does not improve decision-relevant performance or explain cost behavior better, its extra complexity is difficult to justify.

Back-testing the existing operation cannot prove how a new employee will perform. Onboarding speed, shift coverage, and task transfer remain assumptions requiring management review or a limited operational test.

7. Prefer Explainable Relationships to Unsupported AI Complexity

A small, inconsistent dataset rarely justifies a complicated model merely because AI can generate one. A transparent formula lets the owner ask why costs changed and identify which source or assumption needs review.

AI assistance may help organize a draft worksheet or suggest checks. Its output still needs validation against source records and accounting logic. It should not invent missing observations, supply unverified probabilities, or infer clinical demand from administrative data.

Use a more complex method only when it addresses a demonstrated weakness, has sufficient relevant data, and can be tested against a simpler alternative. Additional complexity should earn its place through useful evidence—not a more polished chart.

8. Set a Model-Readiness Gate

Before using the architecture to create financial scenarios, require a short review:

  • Inputs have documented definitions, owners, and source versions.
  • Missing and noncomparable periods are visible.
  • Baseline cash, payroll, and shared costs reconcile to closed accounts.
  • Formulas use consistent units and distinguish expense from cash timing.
  • Dependencies prevent double-counted savings or unsupported revenue gains.
  • Reserved-period testing is complete, with unresolved errors documented.
  • Important untested assumptions are visible to the decision-maker.

There are three useful outcomes: ready for the defined decision, ready only with explicit limitations, or not ready until specific evidence is obtained. A model can be fit for estimating payroll cash needs while remaining unfit for predicting collection improvements.

Establish the Financial Foundation First

A defensible medical practice scenario model does not begin with best-case and worst-case labels. It begins with traceable inputs, reconciled accounts, understandable relationships, and evidence about where those relationships fail.
