Cross-Border Payment Review Checklist for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An overseas vendor payment should not begin and end with entering bank instructions. A private medical practice needs a clear record of what it is buying, which entity owes the money, who verified the recipient and who authorized the transfer. It also needs a plan for resolving questions before funds leave the account.

That matters whether you are paying an overseas administrative contractor, purchasing equipment from a foreign supplier or renewing a service billed in another currency. Missing details can lead to payment delays, duplicate transfers, unexpected fees or expenses posted to the wrong location.

This checklist is an owner-side records and escalation workflow, not a universal compliance checklist. The jurisdictions involved, recipient, service or goods, payment route and transaction details determine what professional review is appropriate. Your bank or payment provider, CPA and legal counsel have different roles; approval from one does not resolve every question for the others.

1. Establish the business purpose and paying entity

Before requesting a transfer, have the practice manager assemble a short explanation of the purchase. A useful description identifies the vendor, deliverable, covered period and practice location or entity benefiting from it.

“Administrative support for the downtown office for the current service month” is more useful than “consulting.” For equipment, identify the item and the related purchase agreement without including patient information.

Confirm these points:

  • Business purpose: What is the practice receiving, and why is the expense necessary?
  • Contracting entity: Which legal entity signed the agreement or placed the order?
  • Paying account: Does the proposed bank account belong to that entity?
  • Benefiting location: Is the cost attributable to one office or shared across locations?
  • Payment obligation: Is this a deposit, milestone payment, recurring charge or final balance?
  • Timing: When is payment contractually due, and when must the recipient receive it?

For a multi-entity practice, do not select whichever account has the most cash without review. If one entity will pay an obligation belonging to another, ask the CPA how to document and record that arrangement. Seek legal advice if the contract or authority to pay is unclear.

Also consider the cash impact alongside provider and administrative payroll, rent and other scheduled obligations. Expected payer receipts are not the same as available funds.

2. Match the invoice to the agreement and work received

The payment packet should let the approver understand the obligation without reconstructing an email thread.

Record What the reviewer should confirm
Vendor invoice Vendor identity, invoice number, date, currency, amount and service or goods description
Contract, purchase order or engagement letter Payment terms, contracting parties, deliverables and any deposit or milestone conditions
Evidence of delivery or acceptance The responsible staff member confirms the service period, completed work or goods received, as applicable
Prior payment history Deposits, credits and previous installments have been considered; the invoice is not already paid
Location or entity coding The proposed expense assignment matches the business purpose and accounting policy
Correspondence resolving discrepancies Differences in names, amounts or terms have an explanation and an identified reviewer

A deposit may be payable before delivery. In that case, the approver should confirm that the agreement authorizes the deposit rather than treating a missing receipt as an unexplained exception.

Keep the original invoice and any corrected version. If the vendor changes the amount or currency, document which version supersedes the earlier request.

3. Verify beneficiary instructions independently

An invoice can be genuine while the attached payment instructions are not. Treat new beneficiaries and changes to bank details as separate verification events.

Use a previously established contact method to confirm instructions with an authorized vendor representative. Do not rely only on the phone number or reply address contained in the message requesting the change. If there is no established contact, resolve how to authenticate the vendor before releasing payment.

Record who performed the verification, when it occurred, the contact method and the result. Keep full account details in the practice’s designated restricted records rather than copying them into broadly shared status messages.

The reviewer should check:

  • The beneficiary’s legal name against the invoice and agreement.
  • The destination country and bank information requested by the payment provider.
  • Whether the beneficiary is the contracting vendor or a different party.
  • Whether instructions have changed since the previous payment.
  • Whether an intermediary bank or additional payment reference is required.

Pause unexplained mismatches. A request to pay an individual instead of the contracted company, send funds to an unrelated country or use a newly substituted account needs resolution. Ask the bank about payment-routing requirements and involve counsel when the proposed recipient or contractual arrangement is unclear.

Independent verification is a practical fraud-control step, not a substitute for the bank’s review or a legal determination about whether a payment is permitted.

4. Confirm currency, fees and timing before approval

The invoice amount, account debit and beneficiary receipt may differ. Make those differences visible before the owner authorizes the payment.

Ask the bank or payment provider:

  • Which currencies can be sent and received for this route?
  • What exchange rate applies, and when is it fixed?
  • Which transfer or conversion charges are included in the quote?
  • Could intermediary or receiving-bank charges reduce the amount delivered?
  • Can the provider confirm the expected recipient amount, or is it an estimate?
  • What processing window, cutoff or additional documentation could affect timing?
  • What happens if the transfer is rejected, delayed or returned?
  • What options exist to request cancellation or recovery, and what limitations apply?

Illustrative example: A practice receives an invoice for €4,000. Its review packet should show the invoice currency and amount, the provider’s quoted dollar debit, separately identified charges where available and any uncertainty about deductions at the receiving end. The owner should not assume that authorizing the quoted debit guarantees the vendor will receive the full invoice amount.

If the agreement requires the vendor to receive a specific net amount, resolve fee responsibility before sending. Ask the CPA how to record foreign-currency balances and exchange differences when relevant; do not force the bank debit to match the invoice by changing the underlying expense without explanation.

5. Assign approval and escalation responsibility

A staffed practice does not need a payment-compliance department to establish clear responsibilities. It does need named people for preparation, review, release and reconciliation.

Role Practical responsibility
Practice manager or accounts payable preparer Collect records, check for duplicates and document open questions
Service owner or purchasing lead Confirm the business purpose and applicable delivery or milestone
Owner or authorized approver Approve the obligation, paying entity, cash impact and documented exceptions
Authorized payment releaser Enter or release the transfer using the approved instructions
Bookkeeper or finance team Match the payment to the invoice, bank activity and accounting records

Where staffing permits, separate preparation from approval and release. If the same person must perform several steps, use an additional owner review and a documented post-payment check. Set internal approval limits and exception rules appropriate to the practice; there is no single amount that makes every cross-border payment routine or safe.

Use explicit statuses such as draft, awaiting professional response, approved for release, sent and reconciled. A packet with a material unresolved question should not be marked approved merely because the invoice is due.

6. Use a reusable payment-review packet

Keep one packet for each proposed payment, with links to supporting records rather than multiple disconnected copies. This template can serve as the cover sheet.

Packet field Information to capture
Payment identifier and status Internal reference, preparer, preparation date and current status
Practice entity and location Contracting entity, paying account reference and benefiting location or shared-cost basis
Vendor and purpose Legal name, country, goods or services and covered period
Invoice and agreement Invoice number, amount, currency, due date and links to supporting documents
Acceptance and prior payments Delivery or milestone confirmation, deposits, credits and duplicate-payment check
Beneficiary verification Verification date, verifier, method and restricted record containing confirmed instructions
Payment quote Provider, quote date, rate validity, expected debit, known fees and recipient-amount uncertainty
Open questions Question, assigned professional, date sent, response and whether it blocks release
Authorization Approver, approved amount and currency, conditions and approval date
Completion Transfer reference, status, bank debit, vendor acknowledgment if obtained and reconciliation date

Route unresolved questions to the right professional

Question or uncertainty Primary reviewer What to request
Required bank fields, destination support, intermediary routing or payment status Bank or payment provider Required instructions, documentation, processing expectations and next steps
Potential payment restriction or a provider request for additional review Bank or payment provider; legal counsel as needed Clarification of the provider’s requirements and legal advice where necessary
Foreign vendor documentation, possible withholding or reporting questions CPA, with specialist input if needed Transaction-specific advice based on the recipient, work performed and relevant jurisdictions
Expense classification, foreign-currency accounting or payment across entities CPA or finance team Accounting treatment and supporting records needed
Different contracting and receiving parties, unclear terms or disputed delivery Legal counsel Whether the arrangement and payment obligation require clarification or amendment
Vendor access to patient information or unclear data-handling terms Legal or privacy counsel and the practice’s responsible privacy lead Review of the actual services, information involved and appropriate arrangements

Do not treat a bank’s acceptance of a transfer as confirmation of tax treatment, contract enforceability or every legal issue. Likewise, a CPA’s accounting instructions do not establish that the bank can process the payment route.

7. Keep payment review separate from messaging and card standards

Bank-message formats, including ISO 20022, concern how payment information is structured and exchanged. If the provider requests particular fields or references, obtain its instructions. Completing those fields does not replace approval of the business purpose or resolution of recipient questions.

PCI DSS concerns payment-card data and applicable card-processing arrangements. It is not a general checklist for an overseas bank transfer. If the practice proposes paying by card, route card-specific questions to its processor or appropriate adviser rather than assuming this transfer workflow answers them.

The same distinction applies to patient information. A vendor invoice generally should not need patient names, diagnoses or patient-level billing detail to explain an administrative expense. Use aggregate descriptions where sufficient, and keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary Slack messages and general-purpose AI tools.

If a vendor’s services involve protected health information, assess that relationship separately. HHS explains covered entities and business associates, while its minimum necessary guidance describes the standard and its exceptions. A completed payment packet does not resolve those responsibilities.

8. Reconcile the payment and close exceptions

Sending the transfer is not the final control. The finance team should connect the approved obligation to what actually happened.

  1. Save the transfer confirmation. Record the provider reference and distinguish submitted, pending, completed and returned statuses.
  2. Match the bank debit. Compare the actual debit and charges with the approved quote; investigate unexplained differences.
  3. Apply the payment correctly. Match it to the vendor invoice, deposit or other applicable accounting treatment.
  4. Confirm allocation. Assign the cost to the correct entity and location, including any documented shared-cost treatment.
  5. Resolve shortfalls or returns. Before resending, establish whether the original payment failed, remains pending or was returned. Avoid creating a duplicate payment.
  6. Close the packet. Record remaining differences, responsible follow-up staff and the reconciliation date.

The IRS business recordkeeping guidance in Publication 583 supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Ask your CPA and counsel which retention requirements apply to the transaction and supporting agreements rather than adopting one universal destruction date.

Make the next payment easier to review

The goal is a repeatable decision: pay, hold for missing information or escalate a specific question. A complete packet makes that decision clearer without asking the practice manager to act as a bank, tax specialist or lawyer.
