5 Anti-Fraud Tool Types for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A staffed medical practice needs more than a warning that a transaction looks unusual. It needs someone to verify the supporting records, decide whether payment should proceed and document what happened.

The most useful anti-fraud tool is therefore not necessarily the one with the longest feature list. It is the one that addresses a specific gap in your practice’s financial workflow without leaving responsibility unclear.

This guide compares five complementary control types:

  1. Payment approval controls
  2. Vendor-change verification
  3. Duplicate-payment screening
  4. Payroll exception review
  5. Financial-access monitoring

These are selection categories, not vendor rankings. Evaluate whether your existing systems and documented manual procedures can meet each need before buying another product. An alert is a reason to investigate—not proof of fraud. Tools also do not replace segregation of duties: the person preparing a transaction should not be its only approver and reviewer.

Start With the Control Gap, Not the Software Demo

Map how money leaves the practice: vendor payments, provider and administrative payroll, reimbursements, refunds and transfers between practice entities. Identify who can create a payee, change payment details, approve a payment, release funds and reconcile the account.

Look especially closely at overlapping responsibilities. If the same administrator can add a vendor, change its bank details and release payment, a dashboard alone will not resolve the underlying control gap.

Use this starting map to choose your first priority:

Practice workflow concern Control type to evaluate first Decision it should support
Bills are paid without documented review Payment approval controls Is this expense authorized for this entity and location?
A supplier requests new bank details Vendor-change verification Was the change independently confirmed?
Invoices arrive through several locations or inboxes Duplicate-payment screening Has this obligation already been entered or paid?
Payroll changes are difficult to explain Payroll exception review Does the payroll match approved staffing, hours and compensation?
Former staff or outside users retain financial access Financial-access monitoring Does each person still need their current permissions?

Reliable source records matter across all five categories. IRS Publication 583 explains business recordkeeping, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices support financial review, but reconciliation by itself does not establish that every payment was authorized.

1. Payment Approval Controls

Gap addressed: Money can leave the practice without someone independently confirming the business purpose, amount and paying entity.

Evaluate whether the proposed workflow separates bill preparation from approval and payment release. Ask what happens when the usual approver is absent, an invoice changes after approval or someone labels a payment urgent. Do not assume these controls exist simply because a system has an approval button.

Evidence and investigation ownership

The reviewer needs the invoice, evidence that goods or services were received, the correct entity and location, and the relevant authorization. A location manager may confirm delivery, while the owner or designated finance reviewer approves the expense. The payment preparer supplies records but should not resolve their own exception alone.

For shared costs, distinguish payment authorization from accounting allocation. An approved technology invoice might be paid by one entity and allocated across locations under a documented method. An allocation does not substitute for approval of the underlying purchase.

Selection test and limitation

Ask the provider to demonstrate a changed invoice, an absent approver and a rejected payment. Confirm which payment channels are actually covered; checks, cards and transfers may follow different workflows.

Approval controls cannot establish that a convincing invoice is genuine. A manual fallback is an approval register linked to supporting documents, followed by independent comparison of the approved list with the payments being released.

2. Vendor-Change Verification

Gap addressed: A legitimate supplier’s payment destination is changed without independent confirmation.

Treat vendor setup and changes to banking instructions as separate decisions from invoice approval. An invoice may be valid while its payment instructions are not.

Evidence and investigation ownership

The reviewer needs the change request, previous vendor details, a trusted contact established independently of the request and a record of verification. Do not rely solely on a phone number or link supplied in the change email.

The employee entering the change should not be its only verifier. An owner or designated reviewer should confirm the request through a previously established contact channel before authorizing use of the new details. Record the verification without unnecessarily copying sensitive banking information into email or chat.

Selection test and limitation

Ask how the proposed process distinguishes receiving a request from verifying it. Test what happens when a request arrives shortly before a payment run and when the usual vendor contact cannot be reached.

A tool cannot make an untrusted contact trustworthy. The manual fallback is to leave the change unapproved, contact the vendor through an established channel and document the independent decision. Any urgent exception needs a named approver and a clear rationale—not an informal bypass.

3. Duplicate-Payment Screening

Gap addressed: The same obligation is entered or paid more than once, potentially across different locations or entities.

Evaluate screening against both open bills and prior payments. Ask which fields are compared, how invoice-number variations are handled and whether the review covers all relevant accounts. These are requirements to test, not capabilities to assume.

Evidence and investigation ownership

The reviewer needs vendor identifiers, invoice numbers, dates, amounts, payment status, credits and entity or location details. The accounts-payable preparer can gather the matching records; a finance reviewer should decide whether an apparent duplicate is a separate obligation, a recurring charge or an error.

Illustrative example: Two practice locations each receive a $1,250 equipment-service invoice from the same supplier. Matching amounts alone do not establish a duplicate. Review the service address, service period and invoice details before releasing or withholding payment.

Selection test and limitation

Test an exact duplicate, a differently formatted invoice number and two legitimate recurring invoices. Confirm whether the process merely flags records or can prevent payment, and identify who can override it.

Screening may miss duplicates when source records are incomplete and may flag valid repeat charges. A manual fallback is to sort open invoices and payment history by vendor, amount and invoice reference, then review possible matches before the payment run.

4. Payroll Exception Review

Gap addressed: Unexplained changes to employee records, compensation, hours or payroll totals reach payment without independent review.

Provider and administrative payroll require context. A higher payroll total may reflect an approved hire, extra shifts, leave or a compensation change. An exception report should direct attention to supporting evidence rather than label the increase fraudulent.

Evidence and investigation ownership

Review the current payroll register against the previous period, approved employee roster, compensation authorizations, time records where applicable, and employee payment-detail changes. For staff working across locations, also check whether location allocations reflect the approved work arrangement.

The payroll preparer gathers explanations. The practice manager or responsible supervisor validates staffing and hours; the owner or another authorized reviewer approves compensation exceptions. A change affecting the reviewer’s own pay requires separate approval.

Selection test and limitation

Ask for demonstrations using an approved new hire, an unexpected pay-rate change, a terminated employee and a direct-deposit change. Confirm what is visible before payroll release and what is available only afterward.

A comparison cannot determine whether reported hours were actually worked. The manual fallback is a documented payroll-to-prior-period comparison, supported by employee-level change approvals before submission. Resolve exceptions promptly with the payroll provider and appropriate advisers without assuming an alert justifies withholding earned wages.

5. Financial-Access Monitoring

Gap addressed: Users retain unnecessary authority over bank accounts, accounting records, payroll or payment settings.

Build a list of financial systems and their users, including outside bookkeepers and temporary personnel. Identify who can view information, change records, approve transactions, release payments and manage other users.

Evidence and investigation ownership

The reviewer needs current user lists, assigned permissions, employment or engagement status, approved role changes and available activity records. Include each practice entity rather than assuming one account list covers the whole group.

The owner approves business access needs. The system administrator implements approved changes, and a separate reviewer confirms the resulting permissions. If an alert concerns the administrator, escalate it outside that person’s control.

Selection test and limitation

Ask how access information is obtained, which systems are excluded and whether the review can identify conflicting permissions. Monitoring may reveal excessive access without preventing its use.

The manual fallback is a system-by-system permission review, with documented removal requests and confirmation that changes were completed. Shared credentials weaken accountability; alerts cannot reliably identify an individual when several people use the same login.

Use this scorecard for each candidate workflow, whether it relies on existing software, a new product or manual review. Assign 0 for absent, 1 for partly demonstrated and 2 for demonstrated with your practice’s test records. These scores describe your evaluation, not a prediction of fraud prevention.

Put Alert Handling Into the Workflow

Before rollout, assign a primary reviewer and backup for every alert type. Define which items need resolution before payment, which can be reviewed afterward and who may authorize an exception.

Keep a restricted exception register containing the transaction reference, alert reason, reviewer, supporting evidence, decision and resolution status. Document legitimate explanations as well as errors. If potential misconduct emerges, preserve relevant records and involve the owner and appropriate advisers rather than making an accusation from the alert alone.

Use financial identifiers instead of patient details wherever possible. Patient-refund reviews may require access to underlying records, but that does not mean those records belong in every reporting tool. HHS minimum-necessary guidance describes the standard and its exceptions. Assess the applicable privacy workflow before sharing information; do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool.

After implementation, review unresolved exceptions, overrides, missed payment channels and recurring false matches. The goal is a control process people can operate consistently—not simply more alerts.

Choose Complementary Controls, With Clear Owners

These five categories address different points in the payment lifecycle. Approval controls address authorization; vendor verification addresses payment destination; duplicate screening addresses repeated obligations; payroll review addresses compensation exceptions; access monitoring addresses authority over the systems themselves.

Start with your most consequential uncovered gap, test with representative records and document the fallback before relying on a new workflow.

FAQs

Does a medical practice need five separate anti-fraud products?

No. Existing systems and documented manual procedures may cover several categories. Evaluate each control separately, including its evidence, reviewer and fallback, rather than assuming one product covers the entire workflow.

Who should investigate alerts in a small staffed practice?

Assign someone with access to the necessary records who did not solely prepare or authorize the questioned transaction. When staffing limits separation, the owner can review underlying records directly or arrange independent financial review. Exceptions involving the owner require another appropriate independent reviewer.

Does an unusual payment or payroll change establish fraud?

No. It may reflect a valid recurring expense, approved staffing change, data-entry error or incomplete records. Verify the evidence, document the explanation and escalate unresolved concerns appropriately. Tools identify items for review; they do not establish intent or replace investigation.
