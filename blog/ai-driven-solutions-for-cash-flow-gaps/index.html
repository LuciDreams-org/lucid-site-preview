Managing Cash Flow Gaps in Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A private medical practice can have a full schedule and still struggle to cover its next payroll. Payer deposits may arrive later than expected, patient balances may take time to collect, and rent, provider compensation, administrative payroll and approved vendor bills keep coming due.

The owner's first decision is whether the shortage is a temporary timing gap or a sustained operating problem. A delayed receipt may require a short-term funding plan. Recurring losses require changes to the economics of the practice—not just another transfer from the owner.

A practical cash-management process connects expected receipts to dated obligations, identifies the lowest projected cash balance and assigns action before that balance becomes a problem. Here is how to build that process for a staffed private practice.

Start With Cash You Can Actually Use

A bank balance is a starting point, not a spending limit. It may include money needed for pending payments, upcoming payroll, taxes or other commitments. A combined balance across several entities can also hide a shortage in the entity that employs staff or pays the rent.

Before approving discretionary spending or an owner distribution:

  • Reconcile operating accounts and identify outstanding payments.
  • Confirm pending payroll withdrawals and scheduled electronic payments.
  • Separate operating cash from amounts designated or restricted for other purposes.
  • List balances by legal entity, rather than assuming every account is interchangeable.
  • Identify credit availability separately from cash already on hand.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, keeping business accounts separate from personal accounts and reconciling bank records. Those basics make a cash forecast more dependable.

Next, choose a minimum operating cash floor. Base it on the practice's payment schedule, receipt volatility and access to funding—not a generic percentage. The floor is a management trigger: falling below it prompts action even if the account is not yet empty.

Build a Rolling Weekly Cash Forecast

A rolling 13-week forecast is a useful planning format because it brings several payroll cycles and recurring bills into view. Update it weekly, and review the next few days more closely when cash is tight.

Use this structure:

Opening available cash + expected receipts − scheduled payments = closing available cash.

Carry each closing balance into the next week's opening balance. Keep borrowing, owner contributions and transfers separate from operating receipts so they do not disguise a weak collection pattern.

Forecast receipts, not charges

Charges posted and accounts receivable are not cash available for payroll. Estimate payer receipts using actual deposit history, current billing-team updates and known interruptions. Keep uncertain receipts visible rather than placing them in the forecast as if they were confirmed.

For patient balances, use observed payment behavior. Do not assume the full outstanding balance will arrive next week. Separate routine collections from unusual recoveries or one-time payments.

Ask the billing team for aggregate financial information: expected receipt ranges, delayed payment batches and material changes in collection patterns. A cash forecast generally does not need patient names, diagnoses or detailed clinical records.

Put obligations in the week cash leaves

A monthly expense total can conceal a midmonth shortage. Put payroll in the week the account will be debited, not simply the month the expense belongs to. Apply the same discipline to rent, insurance, equipment payments and approved vendor bills.

Include known irregular payments. Annual renewals, provider compensation adjustments and equipment deposits can create a gap even when routine expenses are predictable.

First confirm whether the second week's receipts are reasonably supported and whether the daily payment sequence creates a deeper shortage than the weekly totals reveal.

If the expected recovery depends on collecting old, disputed balances, the gap is less certain—and potentially more serious—than the table suggests.

Separate Timing Gaps From Operating Losses

Cash movement and profit answer different questions. Loan proceeds can raise cash without improving operating performance. Equipment purchases and debt principal payments can reduce cash without appearing as ordinary operating expenses in the same way. Accrual-basis revenue may be recorded before the cash arrives.

Review the forecast alongside reconciled financial statements and collection trends.

For multi-location practices, compare location-level performance while recognizing that cash may be collected centrally. Allocate shared administrative costs consistently and explain the method. For multi-entity owners, maintain entity-level cash forecasts; a healthy consolidated total does not establish that each entity can meet its obligations.

Choose Actions That Match the Cause

For a temporary timing gap

Start with actions that improve visibility or change genuinely flexible spending:

  1. Verify expected receipts. Have the billing team confirm the status of material delayed payments and update the forecast when timing changes.
  2. Pause discretionary commitments. Reconsider optional purchases, expansion spending and owner distributions before committing cash.
  3. Review approved bills. Identify payments that can be rescheduled by agreement with the vendor. Do not assume a due date can be ignored.
  4. Evaluate short-term funding if needed. Compare available cash, owner funding and credit options with a finance adviser. Include financing costs and repayment in the forecast.
  5. Document the recovery point. State which receipts are expected to close the gap and what happens if they arrive late.

Do not treat delayed payroll, tax payments or other required obligations as routine working-capital tools. If the practice may miss an obligation, seek qualified advice promptly rather than relying on a spreadsheet adjustment.

Before moving funds between entities, confirm the appropriate authorization, documentation and accounting treatment with the practice's advisers. A transfer is not new operating revenue and should remain visible as a transfer.

For recurring operating shortfalls

Repeated borrowing can postpone a decision without fixing the underlying problem. Review:

  • Collections: Are deposits falling because of unresolved billing issues, slower payment or changes in payer mix?
  • Staffing and compensation: Do scheduled hours, provider compensation and administrative costs fit current activity and sustainable collections?
  • Recurring commitments: Are unused services, duplicate subscriptions or unfavorable purchasing arrangements consuming cash?
  • Location performance: Is one office generating enough contribution to support its direct and shared costs?
  • Owner withdrawals: Are distributions based on available surplus or on the bank balance before upcoming obligations?

Financial analysis should inform staffing and purchasing decisions without overriding patient-care needs or applicable obligations. Assign each corrective action an owner, a review date and an expected financial effect. Then compare the result with the forecast.

Run a Short Weekly Cash Review

The owner, practice manager and finance lead should use one agreed forecast rather than separate versions of the numbers. Bring billing-team input when receipt assumptions need explanation.

Each weekly review should answer:

  • What changed in available cash since the last review?
  • Which receipts or payments differed from forecast, and why?
  • What is the lowest projected balance, and when does it occur?
  • Which decisions must be made before the next payroll or major bill run?
  • Who will complete each action, and by when?

Compare actual receipts and payments with the prior forecast. Distinguish timing errors from amount errors: a deposit arriving one week late requires a different response from a permanent reduction in expected collections.

Keep an action log with the decision, responsible person and revised assumption. This helps prevent an uncertain receipt from staying in the forecast week after week without challenge.

Use AI Assistance Without Outsourcing Judgment

AI-assisted financial work can help organize information or draft variance explanations, but it cannot guarantee payer deposits or make an unreliable forecast dependable. A human reviewer should verify opening balances, receipt assumptions, payment dates and proposed actions.

Use aggregated financial information where possible. Do not place patient-identifiable information into an ordinary Slack conversation or a general-purpose AI tool. HHS explains that accounting services involving protected health information can raise business-associate responsibilities. Its minimum necessary guidance also describes limits and exceptions that require context-specific consideration. Confirm appropriate arrangements before sharing sensitive information.

FAQs

Can a profitable medical practice still have a cash flow gap?

Yes. Receipts can arrive after payroll and bills are due. Debt payments, equipment purchases and owner distributions can also affect cash differently from reported profit. Review both financial statements and a dated cash forecast.

How much cash should a private practice keep available?

There is no single appropriate amount for every practice. Set an operating floor using upcoming payroll, essential bills, receipt variability and dependable access to funding. Revisit it when staffing, locations or payer patterns change.

Should an owner use a line of credit to cover payroll?

It may be an option for a supported temporary gap, but availability, cost, terms and repayment capacity matter. Forecast repayment before borrowing. Repeated credit use for ordinary expenses calls for a review of operating performance, not just a larger credit limit.

Should cash be pooled across practice entities?

Do not assume balances are freely interchangeable. Forecast each entity's obligations separately and consult advisers before intercompany transfers. A consolidated report is useful, but it should not hide which entity holds the cash and which owes the bills.

What should the practice do first if cash is already tight?

Reconcile available cash, confirm the next payroll withdrawal and list payments due before expected receipts arrive. Validate those receipts, pause discretionary commitments and assign immediate follow-up. If required payments may be missed, seek qualified financial or legal advice promptly.
