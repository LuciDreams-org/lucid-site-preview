ISO vs. NSO Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

An associate physician asks about an ownership path. A practice administrator proposes equity instead of a larger cash bonus. Or an owner receives a compensation proposal labeled “ISO” or “NSO.” Before comparing potential tax outcomes, answer a more basic question: Can the proposed entity and recipient support this particular stock-option arrangement?

For a staffed private medical practice, the answer requires more than a compensation spreadsheet. Advisers need to identify the issuing entity, review its governing documents, establish the recipient’s employment relationship, examine ownership restrictions and understand what happens when the option is exercised.

Neither incentive stock options nor nonqualified stock options are universally better. A potentially favorable tax label does not establish that an arrangement is available, appropriate or affordable.

This article is an instrument-specific review checklist—not a guide to designing vesting schedules or a recommendation to offer equity. Use it to prepare a focused discussion with your CPA, tax counsel and healthcare counsel before promising an award.

What ISO and NSO Actually Mean

A stock option generally gives its holder a contractual right to purchase specified shares at an agreed exercise price, subject to the award’s terms. Receiving an option is not the same as immediately receiving shares.

An incentive stock option (ISO) is a stock option intended to meet the requirements of Internal Revenue Code Section 422. Those requirements include an employee relationship and other conditions affecting the plan, issuer and award. Calling an award an ISO does not establish that it qualifies.

A nonqualified stock option (NSO) is a stock option outside that statutory ISO treatment. NSOs can be considered in a broader range of service relationships, but that does not mean any practice can grant one to any person. Entity authority, tax treatment and healthcare ownership questions still need separate review.

These terms describe stock-option instruments—not every arrangement that rewards business growth. An interest in an LLC, a direct purchase of ownership, a cash bonus and a phantom-equity promise should not be treated as interchangeable with an ISO or NSO.

Start With the Entity, Not the Tax Label

A practice’s public name may cover several legal entities. One entity may employ providers, another may employ administrative staff, and another may own property. A location can also be a department of one entity rather than a separate business.

A location-level P&L is not evidence that the location has its own shares to issue. Likewise, an employee working across several offices is not necessarily employed by every entity associated with those offices.

Give advisers a simple entity map showing:

  • Each legal entity and its organizational form.
  • Its tax classification, supported by the relevant records.
  • Current owners and their interests.
  • Which entity employs each proposed recipient.
  • Which entity receives practice revenue and pays major expenses.
  • Any parent, subsidiary or management relationships.
  • The specific entity proposed as the option issuer.

Do not assume that a tax election changes the legal interests available under an entity’s governing documents. Do not assume an LLC proposal becomes workable merely by changing “ISO” to “NSO.” Ask counsel to identify the actual instrument and explain whether the proposed entity can issue it.

For medical practices, healthcare counsel should separately assess whether the proposed ownership and control arrangement is permissible. A tax classification does not answer that question.

Collect the records before asking which option is better. Missing documents often reveal that the proposal is not ready for a tax comparison.

Record existing informal promises as well as signed awards. An offer letter mentioning “future equity” should be brought to counsel’s attention rather than silently replaced with a new proposal.

The IRS’s business recordkeeping guidance in Publication 583 supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Those practices help advisers evaluate the financial background; they do not establish stock-option eligibility.

Ask the CPA to explain potential employer deductions and recipient tax consequences separately. Neither should be presented as an assured benefit before the facts and proposed terms have been reviewed.

Model Cash Needs Separately From Potential Value

An option can have potential economic value without providing spendable cash. The recipient may need money to exercise, and tax obligations may arise without a convenient opportunity to sell shares. The practice may also face administration costs, payroll-related funding needs or a contractual repurchase obligation.

Keep three cash views separate:

  1. Recipient cash: exercise payments, adviser-estimated taxes and any funding needed before a sale.
  2. Practice operating cash: professional fees, administration and any applicable withholding or payment obligations.
  3. Owner and transaction cash: dilution, purchase proceeds, distributions and potential buyback funding.

Do not count an employee’s possible future exercise payment as dependable cash for provider or administrative payroll. Similarly, a possible employer tax deduction is not a substitute for cash available to meet expenses.

Illustrative Practice Scenario: Different Employers, One Proposal

An owner operates a clinical practice and a separate management entity. An employed associate physician and a practice administrator are both offered “options in the business,” but they have different employers.

Before comparing ISO and NSO outcomes, advisers need to identify which business each award covers, whether the relevant employment relationships support the intended treatment, and whether each recipient could hold the resulting ownership interest. Healthcare counsel must review the contemplated ownership arrangement rather than relying on the compensation label.

The finance team should also separate each entity’s earnings and explain shared-cost allocations. A proposal based on combined results may otherwise obscure the economics of the specific issuer.

This is an illustrative workflow, not an eligibility conclusion or a recommendation to create a management entity.

Turn the Review Into a Written Decision

End the review with a short decision memo that records:

  • Business purpose: Why use a stock option rather than cash compensation or another ownership arrangement?
  • Issuer and recipients: Which entity and named participants are being evaluated?
  • Feasibility: Can the proposed entity, participants and ownership structure support the instrument?
  • Conditions: What approvals, valuation work or document changes remain outstanding?
  • Economics: What are the exercise cost, dilution, modeled cash needs and exit limitations?
  • Administration: Who maintains the award ledger, coordinates reporting and tracks relevant events?
  • Decision: Proceed to documentation, revise the proposal or stop the option review.

Keep draft terms clearly identified as drafts. Have counsel review communications before describing an award as approved, tax-favored or guaranteed to produce a particular result.

If the contemplated stock option is unsuitable, that is a useful conclusion. The owner can then evaluate another compensation or ownership approach in a separate review, rather than stretching ISO or NSO terminology to fit.

The finance team’s role is to establish reliable financial inputs and make the proposal’s economics understandable. Entity-level books, documented shared costs and payroll records help advisers distinguish the issuing business from the broader practice operation.

Bottom Line

The first decision is not whether ISOs or NSOs sound more tax-efficient. It is whether the specific issuer, recipient and proposed ownership arrangement support the instrument at all.

Assemble the records, complete the adviser worksheet, model cash needs and document the conclusion. Only then should the practice move from a compensation idea to an approved grant.

FAQs

Can a medical practice organized as an LLC simply offer NSOs instead of ISOs?

Do not assume that substitution works. Ask counsel to review the entity’s legal form, tax classification, governing documents and proposed underlying interest. An LLC ownership arrangement is not automatically a corporate stock option, and changing its label does not resolve those differences.

Can a practice administrator receive the same options as an employed physician?

Not necessarily. Review each person’s employer, service relationship and ability to hold the contemplated ownership interest. ISO eligibility and healthcare ownership permissibility are separate questions. A proposal that works for one recipient should not be copied to another without review.

Are ISOs always better for the recipient and NSOs always better for the practice?

No. The result depends on qualification, grant terms, valuation, exercise and sale events, the recipient’s circumstances and the practice’s obligations. Request a written, fact-specific comparison rather than relying on a headline tax rate or an assumed employer deduction.

Is this the same review as choosing vesting terms or phantom equity?

No. This review asks whether a particular stock-option instrument is feasible and how it would be administered. Vesting design concerns when rights are earned. Phantom-equity arrangements concern contractual economic benefits rather than the same right to purchase shares. Each needs its own analysis.

What if the practice has already promised options in an offer letter?

Give counsel the offer letter, related messages, employment agreement and any approval records before issuing or revising an award. Ask advisers to distinguish what was promised, what was authorized and what remains possible. Do not assume that changing the label or exercise terms is an administrative correction with no further consequences.
