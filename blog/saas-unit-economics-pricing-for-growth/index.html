Medical Practice Unit Economics: Pricing Beyond SaaS Metrics

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A staffed medical practice needs to know what a completed service is likely to collect, what delivering it costs, and what remains to support payroll, rent and other overhead. Posted charges alone cannot answer those questions.

That is the practical purpose of medical practice unit economics: understanding the financial contribution of a defined service or service category. It provides inputs for payer-contract discussions, service sustainability reviews and pricing decisions the practice is permitted to make.

Software subscription metrics are a poor starting point. Patients are not subscription customers, repeat visits are not recurring subscription revenue, and medical services require clinician time, staff capacity and physical resources. A practice also may not control its collectible price simply by changing its fee schedule.

The useful starting equation is:

Expected collectible revenue per completed service − variable cost per completed service = contribution toward fixed overhead.

Contribution is not profit. The next step is checking whether the practice's combined contribution can cover its remaining operating costs.

Why SaaS Pricing Assumptions Do Not Transfer Cleanly

A software business may sell recurring access to a product that can serve additional users without a proportional increase in delivery labor. A medical practice has different revenue constraints and cost behavior.

SaaS-oriented assumption Why it can mislead a practice owner More useful practice-finance input
Subscription revenue repeats predictably Completed services, payer terms and collection timing determine receipts; repeat care is not a subscription commitment Expected collectible revenue for a defined service cohort
Churn measures lost customer revenue Changes in visit frequency can reflect many factors and should not be interpreted as subscription cancellations Completed-service counts, payer mix and collection patterns
A higher listed price produces higher revenue Contractual payment terms may limit what a payer allows, regardless of posted charges Applicable allowed amounts and expected payer and patient collections
Software gross-margin benchmarks indicate a healthy business Provider labor, support staffing, supplies and facility capacity materially affect practice costs A consistent service-cost model and contribution analysis
Upselling improves customer lifetime value Clinical services should not be selected to increase a patient's financial value Financial review of services delivered, without directing clinical decisions

Unit economics remains useful. What changes is the unit, the revenue estimate and the treatment of labor and overhead—not the need to understand financial sustainability.

Define the Service Before Reviewing Its Price

Choose a unit narrow enough to compare consistently. It might be a completed service with a defined billing basis or a service category with similar resource requirements.

Document:

  • Scope: What is included, and what is billed separately?
  • Completion basis: Which completed services belong in the denominator?
  • Payer: Which contract and payment terms apply?
  • Location and entity: Which office delivered the service, and which entity earns the revenue?
  • Resource use: What provider time, support time, supplies or outside services are associated with delivery?
  • Collection window: Has enough time passed to estimate ultimate collections reasonably?

Avoid grouping services with substantially different time requirements or supply costs simply because they share an administrative label. Likewise, a blended payer average can conceal a weak contract behind stronger receipts elsewhere.

Start with one meaningful service category and separate it by payer where volume supports a useful comparison. Expand only after the definitions and source data are reliable.

Separate Charges, Adjustments and Collections

The financial path from a posted charge to cash has several distinct steps:

  1. Posted charges are the practice's billed amounts before adjustments.
  2. Contractual adjustments reduce those charges to the applicable allowed amounts. They are not the same as unpaid patient balances.
  3. Allowed amounts represent the applicable payment basis, including payer and patient responsibility; they are not necessarily cash the practice will collect in full.
  4. Collections to date are actual receipts applied to the services being analyzed.
  5. Expected remaining collections estimate how much of the unresolved balance is reasonably collectible.

For a defined cohort:

Expected total collections = collections already received + expected remaining collections.

Expected collectible revenue per service = expected total collections ÷ completed services in that cohort.

Do not divide this month's bank deposits by this month's completed visits. Deposits may pay for services delivered in earlier periods, while recent services may still be awaiting payment. Use service-date cohorts and matched receipts where available.

For forward-looking pricing work, compare historical collection experience with current contract terms. An older cohort may not reflect a recently changed payment schedule or payer mix.

Assume a practice reviews a sufficiently developed cohort of 100 completed services in one defined category under one payer contract. Some patient balances remain unresolved.

The pricing input is $145 of expected collectible revenue, not the $250 posted charge. The $500 expected shortfall should remain separate from contractual adjustments so the owner can distinguish payment terms from collection performance.

Variable-cost worksheet

Continuing the same illustrative example: Assume these costs vary with completed services under the practice's actual arrangements.

Variable cost Cohort total Per completed service
Service-linked clinician compensation $3,000 $30
Supplies consumed $1,500 $15
Outside service costs attributable to delivery $500 $5

Total variable costs $5,500 $55
Contribution toward fixed overhead $9,000 $90

The result is $145 minus $55, or $90 of contribution per completed service. It does not mean the practice earns $90 of profit on each service.

Salaried provider and administrative payroll, rent, insurance and other costs not included above still need coverage. If this hypothetical service cohort were the practice's only activity and remaining operating costs were $10,500 for the matching period, its $9,000 contribution would leave a $1,500 operating shortfall.

Classify Labor and Shared Costs Without Double Counting

A directly attributable cost is not always a variable cost. Provider time may be identifiable to a service even when the provider's salary does not change with one additional completed service.

Keep two views:

  • Incremental contribution: Subtract costs that actually change with delivery volume. This helps assess what additional activity contributes within existing capacity.
  • Sustainable service cost: Also consider attributable salaried labor, capacity requirements and a reasonable share of overhead. This helps assess whether payment supports ongoing delivery over time.

Do not treat salaried labor as free because it is fixed in the short run. Additional activity may eventually require another staff member, added sessions or more space. Those capacity changes belong in the longer-term review.

For multiple locations, assign costs directly where possible. Allocate genuinely shared costs using a documented basis, such as staff time, occupied space or another relevant driver. Show contribution before shared-cost allocation as well as the result afterward, so an allocation choice does not hide the underlying economics.

For multi-entity owners, keep revenue and costs tied to the appropriate entity and identify shared charges consistently. Do not count a payroll cost in the service worksheet and subtract it again as overhead.

Turn the Findings Into Pricing and Contract Questions

Is the service financially sustainable?

Positive contribution means the service helps cover fixed costs under the assumptions used. It does not establish that its payment level supports staffing, facilities and necessary capacity over time.

Ask whether the practice's overall service mix produces enough contribution to cover remaining costs. If not, investigate the causes: payment terms, collection shortfalls, resource costs, unused capacity or shared-cost allocations.

This is a financial review, not a recommendation to choose or withhold clinical services. Clinical leadership should remain responsible for care decisions.

What does the payer contract actually support?

Review comparable services by payer rather than relying only on a practice-wide average. Examine applicable allowed amounts, expected patient collections and any documented administrative burden that changes delivery economics.

Illustrative sensitivity example: In the worksheet above, expected collections of $145 and variable costs of $55 produce $90 of contribution. If expected collections were $135 and variable costs were $60, contribution would fall to $75. That $15 difference identifies an issue to investigate; it does not establish why the difference occurred.

Use the findings to prepare specific contract-review questions:

  • Do current allowed amounts support the documented resources required?
  • Are actual payments consistent with the applicable terms?
  • Is weak performance caused by the payment level, unresolved claims or collection assumptions?
  • Would a proposed contract change improve expected collections, or only change the nominal rate?

Which prices can the practice actually change?

A higher posted charge does not necessarily increase payment under a contracted arrangement. Model a proposed fee-schedule change using the applicable payment methodology rather than assuming every additional billed dollar becomes revenue.

Where the practice has discretion over self-pay or other prices, unit economics provides an input—not automatic permission or a complete pricing policy. Review applicable payer agreements, patient disclosures and legal requirements with qualified advisers before implementing changes. Do not assume discounts, waivers or different prices are permitted merely because the worksheet supports them financially.

Build a Repeatable Owner Review

A useful pricing review should end with a documented decision or a clearly assigned follow-up question.

  1. Validate the source data. Reconcile receipt totals to the books and bank activity, and explain differences such as unapplied cash or timing. IRS Publication 583 provides guidance on complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation.
  2. Refresh collection assumptions. Separate actual receipts from estimates and flag immature cohorts rather than presenting them as settled results.
  3. Confirm cost behavior. Check compensation arrangements, supply costs and capacity changes with the people responsible for them.
  4. Compare like with like. Keep service definitions consistent and distinguish payer, location and entity differences.
  5. Record the next action. Assign responsibility for a contract question, collection review, cost investigation or permitted pricing review, then revisit the outcome when reliable data is available.

Use aggregated service and payer information where possible. Avoid sending patient-identifiable details through ordinary Slack or general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; financial-analysis workflows should be reviewed for their specific circumstances.

FAQs

What is the most useful unit for medical practice pricing analysis?

Usually, a completed service or a clearly defined service category with comparable resource requirements. Separate payer and location differences when they materially affect expected collections or costs. Avoid treating the patient as a subscription unit or using expected lifetime spending to guide care.

Should a practice use posted charges or collections in its pricing worksheet?

Use expected collectible revenue as the economic input. Show posted charges and contractual adjustments separately, then distinguish cash already received from estimated remaining collections. Match receipts to the services analyzed rather than dividing unrelated monthly deposits by current-month service counts.

Does positive contribution mean a service is profitable?

No. Contribution is the amount left after the variable costs included in the analysis. It still needs to support salaried labor, facilities and other remaining costs. Review attributable labor and shared overhead separately, and avoid counting any expense twice.

Can increasing the fee schedule fix weak service economics?

Not necessarily. Contract terms may limit the effect on collectible revenue. First determine whether the issue is payment terms, collection performance, resource costs or capacity. Any pricing change should be evaluated under the applicable agreements and requirements before implementation.
