Using AI to Diagnose Medical Practice Finance Workflow Bottlenecks

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A late financial report does not necessarily mean your bookkeeper needs to work faster. The underlying problem may be a missing deposit summary, an unclear shared-cost allocation, or an approval that sits between the practice manager and CPA reviewer without a named owner.

For a staffed private medical practice, these delays affect useful decisions: whether cash can support another hire, why one location's expenses increased, or whether entity-level financial statements are ready for review.

AI can help examine workflow records and identify patterns worth investigating. Its role is to organize evidence and propose explanations—not to determine accounting treatment, approve entries, or declare who is responsible for a delay.

Start with one representative month-end close. Map the handoffs, record waiting and correction cycles, and turn each AI-suggested bottleneck into a question a person can verify. Then test a limited workflow change and compare observed results.

Define the financial decision and the close boundary

Before collecting logs, identify what the owner needs from the close. A single-location practice may need a reconciled income statement and cash summary. A multi-location practice may also need location-level P&Ls, while a multi-entity group may need separately reviewed entity accounts and documented shared-cost allocations.

Write down what counts as complete. For example: all in-scope bank accounts reconciled, payroll entries reviewed, material unresolved items assessed by the CPA, and the agreed management reports approved for release.

Without a clear endpoint, one person may call the books closed when transaction entry is finished while another means the CPA has approved the reports. AI cannot make those competing definitions comparable.

Select a cycle that reflects ordinary operations. If the month includes catch-up bookkeeping, a new location, or a bank transition, record that context. Those conditions may explain delays that should not become the baseline for every future close.

Build a sanitized workflow record

The diagnostic input should describe how work moved, not expose the underlying patient or employee records.

Use task histories, checklist status changes, approval requests, and reviewer correction notes. If work happened outside the task system, ask staff to reconstruct the sequence and label those entries as estimates. Missing timestamps are an evidence gap, not permission for AI to invent a timeline.

IRS Publication 583 discusses complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those records support the accounting work; the diagnostic log should reference their availability without copying their sensitive contents into an AI prompt.

Include only fields needed to explain the handoff

Field What to record Why it matters
Task and event IDs Stable identifiers such as CLOSE-A and EVENT-A1 Connect each finding to evidence
Workstream Bank reconciliation, payroll posting, shared costs, or reporting Group related delays
Entity or location alias Entity A or Location West Identify scope without unnecessary identifiers
Responsible role Practice manager, bookkeeper, CPA reviewer, or owner Show who can move the task forward
Event and timestamp Requested, ready, started, returned, resubmitted, or approved Reconstruct the sequence
Dependency Required document, decision, or upstream task Separate blocked work from work ready to begin
Correction reason Wrong period, missing support, unclear allocation, or other defined category Identify repeated rework
Completion evidence Internal reference to the approved checklist or review record Verify the endpoint

Record active work time only if it was actually tracked. The interval between two status changes is elapsed time, not proof that someone spent that entire period working on the task.

Exclude patient information and sensitive payroll details

Do not submit patient names, account numbers, dates of service, claim-level details, or identifiable patient-balance records to a general-purpose AI tool. For a payer-receipt reconciliation issue, a workflow entry such as “aggregate receipt summary unavailable” is usually more useful than a remittance file.

Similarly, describe a payroll problem as “approved department allocation missing,” rather than including employee names, individual pay, bank details, tax identifiers, or garnishment information. Keep the actual supporting records in the practice's authorized systems.

Aliases alone do not make a document safe to share. Free-text notes, attachments, and combinations of details can still reveal identities. Prepare a deliberately limited workflow extract and review it before use.

HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions. For this diagnostic exercise, keep patient information out of the input rather than assuming an accounting purpose makes disclosure appropriate. Any proposed use involving protected health information requires separate evaluation of the service and applicable obligations.

Map one close from request to approval

Lay out the sequence before asking AI for conclusions:

Input requested → input available → preparation → reviewer handoff → corrections → approval → report release.

A useful map includes both the task and its dependencies. A location P&L may be waiting on shared-cost approval, even though its other accounts are complete. Several late reports can therefore trace back to one unresolved decision.

Illustrative example: a close-cycle event log

The following invented scenario uses business-day labels, not performance benchmarks. All timings and events are illustrative.

Evidence ID Task Observed sequence Downstream effect
E1 Entity A bank reconciliation Aggregate payer-receipt summary requested on Day 1; received Day 4; reconciliation submitted Day 5 CPA review could not begin until Day 5
E2 Payroll journal review Submitted Day 3; returned Day 4 for missing department allocation; corrected Day 5; approved Day 6 Location payroll expense remained provisional
E3 Shared administrative costs Allocation question sent Day 4; no decision owner recorded; owner approved Day 8 Both location P&Ls remained blocked
E4 Management report release Other review tasks completed Day 7; shared costs posted Day 8; final reports approved Day 9 Owner received the complete package on Day 9

This sequence suggests several questions, not several proven causes. Was the payer summary available earlier but not delivered? Was the payroll allocation requirement communicated before preparation? Did the owner know the shared-cost question required a decision?

It also shows why the task with the longest duration is not always the best first target. The receipt summary delayed reconciliation, but the shared-cost approval was the final unresolved dependency before reporting could finish.

Ask AI to identify patterns, not assign blame

Give AI the sanitized event table, the close definition, and the meaning of each status. Ask it to distinguish observations from hypotheses.

A useful prompt is:

Analyze this sanitized medical practice close log. Identify waiting intervals, repeated correction reasons, unclear handoffs, and unresolved approvals. Cite event IDs for every finding. Separate observed facts from possible causes. Mark missing evidence explicitly. Do not infer active labor time from elapsed time, assign blame, recommend accounting entries, or invent events. For each issue, propose a root-cause check and one limited workflow change to test.

AI may group similar reviewer comments, trace dependencies across tasks, or highlight approvals with no assigned decision-maker. It may also misread a sequence or merge unrelated issues. Check every suggested finding against the original internal record before acting.

Avoid prompts such as “Which employee is slowing us down?” A task history rarely captures workload, competing responsibilities, or all work performed outside the system. The useful question is which handoff condition prevented progress.

Create an evidence-linked bottleneck register

Do not turn an AI summary directly into a new policy. Build a register that connects each suggestion to evidence, a verification step, and a proposed response.

The register below continues the illustrative example above. Its findings remain hypotheses until checked.

AI-suggested issue Source evidence Root-cause check Proposed workflow change Owner and priority
Receipt-summary handoff lacks a readiness deadline E1: requested Day 1, received Day 4 Confirm when the summary became available and who was expected to deliver it Define a delivery owner, readiness date, and escalation route Practice manager; investigate after the final reporting blocker
Payroll review repeats a missing-input correction E2: returned for department allocation Check whether preparation instructions required an approved allocation and whether it existed Add an allocation-readiness check before submission Bookkeeper and payroll administrator; test next close
Shared-cost decision has no accountable approver E3: question pending Day 4–8 with no owner recorded Verify whether authority was undefined or the request failed to reach the approver Name the decision-maker and backup; specify what support the request must contain Practice owner; first priority because E4 depended on it
Reporting delay may originate upstream, not in report preparation E4: other reviews complete before shared costs posted Confirm whether any reporting work could reasonably proceed earlier Separate draft preparation from final release and display the unresolved dependency CPA reviewer; validate alongside shared-cost change

Keep a status for each item: unverified, confirmed, being tested, resolved, or not supported. A rejected AI suggestion is a useful outcome when the evidence does not support it.

Prioritize by decision impact and evidence strength

Start with issues that block the agreed close endpoint or make a report unreliable. Then consider repeated corrections that consume preparer and reviewer attention.

For each candidate, ask:

  • Does this task actually delay final completion, or does it have room to finish later without affecting release?
  • Is the problem repeated, or was it an unusual event?
  • Is the evidence complete enough to justify a change?
  • Can a named person resolve the underlying condition?
  • Would the proposed change preserve appropriate review and approval?

Do not remove CPA review merely because the review stage contains waiting time. The better intervention may be a complete submission package, clearer questions, or an agreed review window.

Test a limited change and measure what happens

Choose a small change tied to a verified cause. For the illustrative shared-cost issue, the test might be a named approver, a backup, and a standard decision request showing the entities affected and the basis requiring approval.

Keep the accounting judgment with the appropriate reviewer. AI can help summarize the history, but it should not decide the allocation method or approve the entry.

Compare the next close with the baseline using consistent definitions:

Measure Definition Interpretation caution
Close completion timing Time from the agreed starting boundary to final approval Use the same scope and business-day or calendar-day convention
Ready-to-start waiting Time between all required inputs being available and work beginning Requires a reliable readiness timestamp
Rework Returned submissions and reasons for return Fewer returns are not better if review became less thorough
Approval waiting Time from a complete approval request to the recorded decision Distinguish incomplete requests from approver delays
Outstanding items at release Unresolved items, their significance, and reviewer disposition A faster release should not hide unfinished work

Review the results with the people who prepared and reviewed the work. Note changes in transaction volume, staffing, unusual transactions, and scope. One faster month does not prove that the intervention caused the improvement.

If waiting falls but corrections rise, the handoff may have become earlier rather than better. If the target task improves but final reporting does not, another dependency may now determine completion. Update the register rather than expanding automation by default.

Use finance support to resolve ownership gaps

Some bottlenecks need better coordination rather than another tool. An owner may need a bookkeeper to define input readiness, a CPA to resolve review questions, or CFO support to connect reporting priorities with operating decisions.

FAQs

Do we need years of historical data to begin?

No. Start with one representative close that has enough task and status evidence to reconstruct the workflow. Earlier cycles can help establish whether a pattern repeats, but missing history should be disclosed rather than filled with assumptions.

Can we use billing exports or payroll files as AI inputs?

Use a sanitized workflow extract instead. Record that an aggregate receipt summary or approved payroll allocation was missing without uploading patient-level billing records or sensitive employee details. If the task cannot be analyzed without protected information, pause and evaluate the proposed service and applicable obligations before proceeding.

What if AI says the CPA review is the bottleneck?

Check whether the submission was complete, when it became ready for review, and what questions remained unresolved. A long review-stage interval may include waiting for staff answers rather than active review. Agree on clearer handoff requirements or review scheduling without bypassing necessary oversight.

How do we know a workflow change helped?

Compare completion timing, waiting, returned submissions, and unresolved items using the same definitions across close cycles. Confirm that review quality and reporting scope stayed intact. Treat the result as an observation to investigate, not a guaranteed or permanent performance gain.
