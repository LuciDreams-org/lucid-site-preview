5 Performance-Based Equity Scenarios for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Performance-linked ownership incentives start with a difficult question: What must someone accomplish before receiving an ownership interest, and how will the practice prove it?

For a staffed private medical practice, “increase profit” or “open another location” is not a complete condition. Payer receipt timing, provider compensation, shared administrative costs and owner decisions can change the apparent result. An incentive can reward the wrong behavior if its measurement rules are vague.

The five scenarios below are explicitly hypothetical—not client case studies or recommended award terms. All numerical terms are illustrative. They examine performance-condition design, including the records needed to evaluate a milestone and the financial consequences of an award.

Before promising equity, identify the right and the entity

Do not assume a physician, practice administrator or other employee may legally own an interest in a particular practice entity. Before discussing an award as available, have healthcare counsel assess participant eligibility, entity structure, applicable ownership restrictions and compensation-related requirements.

Also distinguish the proposed economic arrangement:

  • Actual equity: An ownership interest whose voting, distribution, transfer and other rights depend on the governing documents.
  • A conditional right to receive equity: A contractual promise subject to specified conditions; it is not necessarily present ownership.
  • A cash-settled incentive: A contractual payment tied to a formula or value measure, not actual ownership merely because it is described as “equity-like.”

A cash arrangement still requires legal and tax review. It is not an automatic workaround for ownership restrictions.

Identify the exact entity involved. An interest in a professional practice entity is not interchangeable with an interest in a separate administrative entity. Likewise, a location-level profit measure does not create ownership in that location unless the legal structure and documents provide it.

Scenario 1: A profitability milestone

Owner objective and hypothetical condition

An owner wants to reward an eligible participant for improving durable operating profitability without reducing necessary staffing or postponing expenses.

Illustrative example: A proposed award would deliver a 3% post-issuance ownership interest if defined annual operating profit reaches $600,000. The agreement must specify whether that percentage assumes other outstanding awards and whether existing owners share dilution proportionately.

Measurement records and disputed definitions

Before the measurement period begins, attach a definition of operating profit and a sample calculation. Specify the accounting basis, entity scope, revenue cutoff and treatment of provider compensation, owner compensation, depreciation, financing costs and unusual expenses.

Use a monthly P&L supported by reconciled bank accounts, payroll reports, payer receipt summaries and accounts-receivable reports. If the measure uses cash receipts, specify how collections from earlier periods count. If it uses accrual revenue, define receivable adjustments rather than treating billed charges as collectible revenue.

Shared administrative costs need a documented allocation method. Otherwise, shifting expenses between entities can make a target appear achieved without improving the practice group’s economics.

Dilution, cash and adviser questions

Actual issuance can dilute existing owners even without an immediate cash payment. A cash-settled alternative preserves ownership percentages but creates a funding need. Model both, including any distribution rights attached to actual equity.

  • Legal: Who certifies achievement, and what prevents either party from changing compensation or expense allocations to influence the result?
  • Tax: How would the proposed award be treated, and could an obligation arise before the participant receives cash?
  • Valuation: How should unusual expenses and owner compensation affect the award’s value, separately from the milestone calculation?

Design lesson: Define profit before measuring it. “Profit per the books” is too open-ended if the accounting policies can change during the award period.

Scenario 2: A location-opening milestone

Owner objective and hypothetical condition

A multi-location owner wants to reward successful expansion, not merely the signing of a lease.

Illustrative example: A conditional award is divided into an opening component and a stabilization component. The latter requires three consecutive monthly closes showing positive location contribution after an agreed allocation of central administrative expenses.

The agreement should identify the entity whose equity would be issued. A location is an operating unit, not necessarily a separate legal entity.

Measurement records and disputed definitions

Define “open” through an approved readiness checklist rather than a ceremonial opening date. Relevant evidence may include completed operational approvals, staffed schedules, a finalized opening budget and actual operations.

For stabilization, use a location P&L, provider and administrative payroll allocations, occupancy costs, payer receipts and receivable summaries. Define whether pre-opening expenses are included in the milestone measure, tracked separately or spread across the evaluation period solely for that calculation.

Also address transferred activity. Moving established providers and their existing receipts into the new site may improve its results without generating equivalent growth for the whole practice.

Dilution, cash and adviser questions

Expansion already requires cash for buildout, deposits, payroll and collection delays. An ownership award may add dilution; a cash award may compete with working capital. Model both the location and consolidated practice cash position.

  • Legal: Which entity may issue the interest, who is eligible to receive it, and what approvals are required before issuance?
  • Tax: How do separate award components affect the proposed arrangement’s treatment and reporting?
  • Valuation: Is value measured at the entity or location level, and how are startup losses and transferred operations reflected?

Design lesson: Reward an opening that meets defined operating conditions, not speed alone. Do not make necessary staffing or operational readiness an obstacle to earning the award.

Scenario 3: A time-and-performance combination

Owner objective and hypothetical condition

The owner wants sustained contribution and continuity, rather than an award based only on tenure or one unusually strong year.

Illustrative example: A proposed award has three annual portions. Each requires both completion of that service year and achievement of that year’s defined financial condition. Service alone does not satisfy the performance requirement.

Measurement records and disputed definitions

Maintain separate service and performance schedules. The first records service dates and approved treatment of role changes or absences. The second records the financial target, actual result, permitted adjustments and approval for each annual portion.

Specify whether a missed portion expires or can be earned later. Also distinguish a performance condition being satisfied from an interest actually being issued. The documents should make each stage understandable to the participant.

Use consistent financial reports across periods. If the owner changes the participant’s responsibilities or reallocates a location, determine whether a pre-agreed adjustment process applies; do not improvise one after results are known.

Dilution, cash and adviser questions

Model cumulative dilution if every portion is earned, alongside other promised awards. For cash-settled rights, forecast payments by expected settlement date rather than service date alone.

  • Legal: What does “continued service” mean, and how do leave, reduced hours, changed responsibilities or termination affect each portion?
  • Tax: Which events matter under the proposed structure, and what reporting or participant cash obligations could follow?
  • Valuation: Is each portion valued separately, and what happens if practice value changes substantially between award dates?

Design lesson: Time and performance are separate conditions. State explicitly whether both must be satisfied and whether later achievement can cure an earlier miss.

Scenario 4: A missed target

Owner objective and hypothetical condition

The owner wants meaningful standards without making the result depend on an arbitrary last-minute decision.

Illustrative example: The target is $500,000 of defined operating profit, but the final calculation is $480,000. The participant asks to count a delayed $30,000 payer receipt after the measurement period.

This request exposes a design choice: is the award based on cash collected within the period, or revenue attributable to that period under agreed accounting rules?

Measurement records and disputed definitions

Review the closed ledger, receipt dates, payer remittance summaries, receivable adjustments and written cutoff policy. Apply the agreed method consistently. A later receipt should not be included merely because it makes the target pass.

Choose the award curve in advance: a strict threshold, a graduated award or another defined approach. If adjustments for specified events are permitted, document the event, calculation, approval authority and participant review process.

Dilution, cash and adviser questions

Under a strict threshold, no award may be earned. Under a graduated formula, a smaller award may result. A discretionary exception can create unplanned dilution or cash cost and inconsistent expectations among participants.

  • Legal: Does the agreement permit an adjustment, and what amendment or dispute process applies?
  • Tax: Would changing the condition or settlement terms affect the arrangement’s treatment or reporting?
  • Valuation: If a partial award is permitted, how is it valued without treating partial performance as an automatic ownership percentage?

Design lesson: Decide how near misses work before there is a near miss. A target should not become negotiable simply because the outcome is uncomfortable.

Scenario 5: A participant departure

Owner objective and hypothetical condition

The owner wants an orderly exit without confusing earned performance rights, continued-service requirements and existing ownership.

Illustrative example: A participant departs after the performance year ends but before results are certified. A prior award portion has already been issued as actual equity. The current portion remains conditional.

Measurement records and disputed definitions

Create a status record for each portion: proposed, granted conditionally, performance satisfied, service satisfied, issued or settled. Match it to signed documents, approval records and the ownership ledger.

The documents should address departure before measurement ends, after measurement but before certification, and after issuance. If partial-period credit is possible, define the formula. Do not assume an unissued right disappears or previously issued ownership is automatically forfeited.

Dilution, cash and adviser questions

Previously issued equity may remain outstanding unless an applicable transfer or repurchase provision operates. A repurchase can require cash when the practice is also paying recruitment and coverage costs. Model the proposed payment schedule against payroll, debt payments and working-capital needs.

  • Legal: What survives departure, what repurchase or transfer rights apply, and how do different departure circumstances affect them?
  • Tax: How would forfeiture, settlement, repurchase or installment payments be treated for each party?
  • Valuation: What valuation date, method, adjustments and dispute process govern a repurchase?

Design lesson: Separate the departure rules for conditional rights from those for actual ownership. They are not the same asset or obligation.

Build the measurement file before making the promise

Give legal, tax and valuation advisers the same proposed terms and financial model. The file should contain:

  • The participant, issuing entity and proposed type of right.
  • A plain-language condition, calculation formula and illustrative pass/fail examples.
  • Source reports, accounting policies and shared-cost allocations.
  • Measurement dates, certification authority and correction procedures.
  • Maximum dilution or cash exposure, including other outstanding awards.
  • Rules for changed responsibilities, missed targets, departure and disputes.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide a foundation for measurement; they do not establish that a particular award is legally permissible or correctly valued.

Use aggregate financial information where possible. Patient-identifiable information should not be placed in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools to evaluate an incentive.

FAQs

Can a medical practice administrator receive equity?

Do not assume eligibility. Have healthcare counsel review the participant, entity, jurisdiction and proposed rights before an offer is made. A contractual cash incentive is not actual ownership and also needs appropriate review.

Is profitability a better condition than revenue growth?

It depends on the objective and the participant’s responsibilities. Revenue growth can overlook staffing and overhead costs; profit can be distorted by compensation decisions, allocations or deferred spending. Use a measure the participant can meaningfully influence, with definitions and safeguards agreed in advance.

What should happen when a target is almost achieved?

Apply the documented threshold, graduated formula or permitted adjustment process. If the terms are silent, obtain adviser guidance before changing them. Avoid rewriting the condition after the result is known simply to produce a preferred award.

Conclusion

A useful performance-based equity condition is measurable, understandable and tied to the owner’s actual objective. Before making a promise, confirm eligibility, identify the entity and right, test the calculation against disputed outcomes, and model maximum dilution and cash needs. The strongest design answers not only “What earns the award?” but also “What happens when results are ambiguous or the participant leaves?”
