Cash Flow Volatility in Medical Practices: Causes and Fixes

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A medical practice can report steady monthly profit while its bank balance swings sharply from week to week. Payer deposits may arrive in batches, provider and administrative payroll may fall before those deposits, and owner distributions or equipment purchases may consume cash without appearing as current operating expenses.

Cash flow volatility is the variation in cash receipts, disbursements, and resulting balances over time. It is not necessarily evidence of an unprofitable practice. But repeated declines can reveal a recurring operating shortfall that moving payment dates will not solve.

For an owner or manager, the useful question is: What caused the change in cash, and does the proposed fix change the underlying economics or only the week in which money moves?

This guide explains how to diagnose observed cash swings, build a weekly cash-movement bridge, and choose actions that address the actual cause.

Why Monthly Profit and Weekly Cash Can Tell Different Stories

A profit and loss statement measures financial performance under the practice’s accounting method. A bank balance measures cash held at a particular moment. They answer different questions.

Under accrual accounting, recognized revenue and expenses may precede the associated receipts and payments. Under cash-basis reporting, revenue and expenses more closely follow cash movement, but the P&L still does not explain every bank transaction. Loan principal, owner distributions, transfers, and some equipment purchases need separate treatment.

Monthly totals also hide weekly concentration. A month can produce positive operating cash overall while a payroll week produces a substantial decline.

Item Why cash and profit may differ What the owner should inspect
Payer receipts Deposits may relate to services from earlier periods Deposit dates, aggregate receivables, and collection status
Patient balances Amounts billed are not necessarily amounts collected Balance aging and actual payments received
Payroll Pay dates and cash withdrawals may differ from expense recognition Payroll calendar and actual bank debits
Owner distributions Generally separate from operating expenses Distribution ledger and authorization
Equipment purchases Cash may leave immediately while accounting expense is recognized differently Purchase documents and accounting treatment
Debt payments Principal reduces cash without being an operating expense Principal and interest breakdown
Account transfers Change one account’s balance without changing total cash within the same reporting scope Both sides of the transfer

Before changing operations, ask the bookkeeper to explain which accounting basis the reports use and reconcile the cash accounts. The IRS guidance on business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliations. These basics make the diagnosis more reliable.

Identify What Is Actually Moving the Balance

Collection timing and payment concentration

A low-receipt week does not automatically mean the practice earned less revenue. A payer batch might have arrived later, a payment processor might not yet have settled funds, or a large deposit might simply fall outside the reporting cutoff.

Start with aggregate receipts by payer and patient-payment channel. Compare the quiet week with the weeks immediately before and after it. Then ask the billing team whether the difference reflects settlement timing, submission delays, unresolved denials, or balances becoming harder to collect.

These causes require different responses. A settlement-date shift may explain volatility without reducing total collections. Repeated unresolved denials or a growing backlog may indicate an operational collection problem rather than harmless timing.

Payroll calendars and clustered obligations

Provider and administrative payroll, payroll-related debits, benefits, rent, and vendor payments can cluster in the same week. A biweekly payroll schedule can also create months containing an additional payroll date relative to other months.

Use actual bank withdrawal dates, not just pay-period labels. Separate payroll funding from other payroll-related withdrawals so the same amount is not counted twice.

Do not treat delayed employee pay or missed required payments as cash-management tools. Review only changes that are permitted, agreed, and operationally appropriate.

Owner distributions and one-time purchases

Distributions can make a healthy operating week look weak. Conversely, owner contributions can conceal an operating decline. Both should be visible outside the operating subtotal.

Equipment, build-outs, and other nonroutine purchases also deserve separate lines. Labeling an expense “one-time” does not mean it can be ignored: repeated purchases may represent an ongoing investment requirement even when each individual purchase is unusual.

Recurring operating shortfalls

A practice has more than a timing problem when ordinary collections repeatedly fail to cover recurring cash operating costs over a representative period.

Look beyond the consolidated total. One location may consistently require support from another, or shared administrative costs may be missing from a location’s apparent results. Review location-level P&Ls alongside cash movement, with a consistent method for allocating shared costs. A location P&L is not a substitute for the entity’s bank reconciliation.

Build a Weekly Cash-Movement Bridge

A cash bridge explains the movement from an opening balance to a closing balance. Build it from actual transactions rather than estimates of what should have arrived.

Step 1: Set a consistent reporting scope

Choose the accounts and legal entity included in the review. Use the same weekly cutoff throughout.

Transfers between included accounts cancel out in the combined bridge. Transfers between separate entities should remain visible in each entity’s bridge and be classified with the accountant’s guidance. Do not assume every entity’s balance is interchangeable spending money.

Keep a separate view of funds earmarked for known obligations so the reported closing balance is not mistaken for an amount freely available for distributions.

Step 2: Group transactions by cause

Use categories that lead to decisions: payer receipts, patient payments, payroll, occupancy, supplies, other operating payments, equipment, debt service, and owner activity. Show refunds or recoupments separately when material.

Reconcile net deposits carefully. If a processor deducts fees before depositing funds, either show the net receipt consistently or reconstruct gross receipts and fees from supporting records. Do not subtract the fee twice.

Step 3: Reconcile opening cash to closing cash

Illustrative example only: The following bridge describes one week for a staffed private medical practice. All amounts are invented.

Cash-movement category Amount Diagnostic note
Opening included bank balances $120,000 Same account scope as closing balance
Payer receipts +$75,000 Confirm against deposits
Patient payments +$15,000 Use settled receipts
Provider and administrative payroll −$58,000 Actual cash withdrawn
Other payroll-related payments −$12,000 Excludes amounts already counted above
Rent, supplies, and other operating payments −$25,000 Separate unusual items in supporting detail
Net operating cash movement −$5,000 Receipts less the operating payments above

Owner distribution −$10,000 Separate from payroll and operating expenses
Total cash movement −$35,000 Includes all movements shown

The balance fell by $35,000, but only $5,000 of that decline came from the operating categories shown. The equipment purchase and distribution explain the remainder. This does not establish whether operations are sustainable; it identifies what needs further review.

Step 4: Compare several consecutive weeks

Repeat the bridge across a period that includes ordinary payroll cycles, major payer receipts, and recurring obligations. Investigate unexplained differences instead of placing them permanently in “other.”

For each significant movement, record its cause, supporting evidence, responsible person, and whether it reversed later. A delayed deposit that arrives the following week tells a different story from collections that remain missing while receivables age.

Use a Cause-to-Action Worksheet

The worksheet should connect an observed movement to evidence and a specific response. Avoid explanations such as “cash was tight” that merely restate the problem.

Some actions address both timing and structure. Faster resolution of a submission backlog may initially release accumulated cash, then improve the ongoing collection process. Separate that initial release from the sustainable result.

Test Whether a Fix Only Moves the Problem

A timing change can be useful without creating savings. Staggering agreed vendor payment dates may reduce concentration, but the obligation still exists.

Before approving a change, answer five questions:

  1. What cash movement changes? Identify the exact receipt or payment.
  2. Where does it move? Name the receiving week, not just the week that improves.
  3. Does total cash over the comparison period change? Include fees, discounts, and added costs.
  4. What accumulates? Check unpaid bills, uncollected balances, and new commitments.
  5. Can the change be repeated without disruption? A one-time deferral is not a recurring correction.

Illustrative example only: Moving a $12,000 vendor payment from one week to the next improves the first week’s closing balance by $12,000 and reduces the next week’s balance by the same amount. Across both weeks, total cash movement is unchanged, assuming no fees or other effects.

That change may still be worthwhile if it better aligns an agreed payment date with receipts. It has not reduced cost or corrected an operating loss. If the following week already contains heavy payroll withdrawals, it may simply relocate the concentration.

Likewise, buying equipment in installments can reduce an immediate outflow while creating future obligations. Evaluate the full commitment, not just the smaller initial payment.

Turn the Diagnosis Into an Owner Review Routine

Assign the bookkeeper responsibility for the reconciled bridge, the practice manager responsibility for operational explanations, and the owner responsibility for distributions and major spending decisions. Involve the billing team when collection evidence is needed.

At each weekly review:

  • Explain the largest movements with supporting records.
  • Separate operating activity from purchases, financing, transfers, and distributions.
  • Check whether prior timing differences actually reversed.
  • Assign each corrective action an owner and review date.
  • Evaluate both weekly concentration and cumulative operating cash movement.

Use aggregated payer and payment information wherever possible. Patient names and clinical details generally are not needed to explain bank movement. Avoid putting patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

Can a profitable medical practice still have volatile cash flow?

Yes. Monthly profit can mask weekly payment concentration, while distributions, loan principal, and equipment purchases can reduce cash without being current operating expenses. The accounting basis also matters. Reconcile the P&L with a cash bridge before concluding that a lower bank balance means lower profitability.

How can I distinguish a timing problem from an operating shortfall?

Track whether the movement reverses and compare operating receipts with recurring operating payments across complete collection and payroll cycles. A late deposit that subsequently arrives may be timing. Repeated operating declines, growing unresolved receivables, or rising unpaid bills require deeper investigation. Exclude owner contributions and borrowing when judging whether operations support themselves.

Does delaying a vendor payment reduce cash flow volatility?

It may reduce concentration in one week, but it can increase it in another. Evaluate both weeks, the total obligation, and any fees or relationship effects. Only consider changes consistent with agreed terms. A payment-date change is a timing adjustment, not a cost reduction.

Should a multi-location practice use one combined cash report?

Use a combined view where appropriate, but retain entity-level bridges and location-level operating reports. Combined totals can conceal a location’s recurring support needs. Eliminate transfers only within the chosen reporting scope, and use consistent shared-cost allocations when comparing location profitability.
