Financial Resilience Planning for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A private medical practice can report a profit and still struggle to fund payroll when payer receipts arrive late. A temporary provider absence can reduce future collections while rent, administrative payroll and debt payments continue. If the owner or practice manager is unavailable, even a well-funded practice may have trouble approving payments or accessing essential financial records.

Financial resilience means having the cash, records and decision process to keep essential operations funded during a disruption. It is not simply a reserve balance or a new financial tool.

For a staffed practice, the useful output is a working continuity plan: which commitments must be funded, which receipts could be interrupted, who can act and what information they need. Start with actual bank activity and obligations, then test specific disruptions rather than adopting a generic minimum reserve rule.

Map the Cash Commitments That Cannot Wait

Begin with a payment calendar, not just the monthly profit and loss statement. Expenses on the P&L do not necessarily match the timing of cash leaving the bank. Loan principal, equipment purchases and owner distributions also affect available cash without appearing as ordinary operating expenses in the same way.

For each obligation, record the paying entity, due date, amount or calculation method, responsible person and consequences of delay. Distinguish payments that can be changed by management from those that require contractual review or agreement with another party.

Commitment What to document Continuity decision
Provider and administrative payroll Payroll funding dates, compensation terms and payroll approval backup Who confirms funding and authorizes payroll if the usual approver is unavailable?
Payroll-related obligations and benefits Amounts due, funding process and responsible adviser or administrator Which funds must remain available for these obligations?
Rent, equipment leases and debt Payment calendar, contract terms and lender or landlord contacts Is there an agreed adjustment option, or must the original payment be funded?
Essential vendors Service dependency, payment terms and outstanding balances Which services would be disrupted by nonpayment?
Planned purchases and discretionary spending Approval status, deposits and cancellation terms What can be paused without creating a larger cost?
Owner distributions Approved amounts, timing and entity involved Who may pause a distribution, subject to governing agreements and professional advice?

Do not classify all staffing costs as immediately adjustable. Compensation agreements, coverage needs and employment requirements can limit the available options. A continuity forecast should reflect changes the practice can actually implement, not hypothetical cuts.

Identify Where Collections Are Concentrated

A payer mix report is a starting point, but resilience planning requires a cash view. Measure receipts by payer or payment channel using recent, representative periods. Then examine what could interrupt those receipts.

Concentration may come from a large payer, one productive provider, a single location or a shared administrative process. Several payers can still depend on the same claim-submission workflow. Conversely, a large payer balance may reflect a temporary backlog rather than the practice's normal collection pattern.

Review these exposures separately:

  • Payer receipts: Which payer delays would create a cash shortfall before the next major payment cycle?
  • Patient balances: How much expected cash depends on collecting outstanding balances, and how reliable has that timing been?
  • Provider dependence: How much future collection activity depends on a particular provider's availability?
  • Location dependence: Could a location interruption reduce receipts while shared overhead continues?
  • Process dependence: Could the absence of one employee delay payment posting, financial reporting or payment approval?

Use historical collections and current receivables information to estimate cash timing. Do not treat gross charges or the entire accounts receivable balance as cash available to spend. Ask the billing team to explain unusual delays, disputed amounts and changes in collection patterns; the finance forecast should reflect that information without attempting to replace billing work.

Build a Short-Term Cash Forecast by Entity

Use weekly periods for the near term and extend the forecast far enough to capture both the disruption and the expected recovery. Each period should show opening available cash, expected receipts, cash payments and closing available cash.

Separate unrestricted operating cash from amounts unavailable for general spending. Also separate confirmed financing availability from credit that is merely being considered. A potential loan is not cash until its terms, access and timing are sufficiently established.

For multi-entity owners, create an entity-level forecast before consolidating the results. A healthy combined balance can conceal a payroll shortfall in the entity employing staff. Do not assume money can move freely between entities. Ask the CPA and legal adviser to review proposed transfers, intercompany balances and governing restrictions.

Location-level P&Ls add another perspective: they show where operating performance is weakening. But shared-cost allocations are not the same as cash payments. Keep the allocation method consistent and identify which entity actually pays each shared bill.

Stress-Test Specific Financial Shocks

A useful stress test changes a small set of assumptions and identifies the decision that follows. Avoid applying an arbitrary revenue reduction to every line of the forecast.

Disruption Forecast adjustment Decision to prepare
Major payer receipts arrive late Move the affected receipts into later periods; do not automatically erase collectible revenue When should discretionary spending pause or a financing discussion begin?
Temporary provider absence Model existing receivable collections separately from reduced future activity and any coverage costs Which commitments continue, and who approves coverage spending?
Location interruption Separate lost activity, continuing site costs and any feasible transfer of operations What spending can stop, and which shared costs remain?
Financial administrator unavailable Model possible delays in approvals, payroll processing or access to reports Who has authorized backup access and authority?
Collection recovery takes longer than expected Extend the reduced-receipt period and revise the catch-up assumption At what point does the practice need a longer-term operating response?

Illustrative example: delayed payer receipts

The following numbers are an illustrative example, not a recommended reserve level.

A practice begins a four-week forecast with $150,000 in unrestricted operating cash. It expects $220,000 in receipts and $260,000 in cash payments. Its baseline closing cash is therefore $110,000.

If $80,000 of expected payer receipts moves beyond that period, closing cash falls to $30,000. Deferring a genuinely optional $20,000 equipment purchase would raise that figure to $50,000, assuming no cancellation cost or other change.

Neither figure establishes whether the practice is adequately funded. The owner must examine the lowest weekly balance, the next payroll funding date and the likelihood of further delays. A positive ending balance can hide a shortfall earlier in the period.

This is how to size liquidity deliberately: identify the largest cumulative shortfall under a credible disruption, add room for uncertainty and compare that need with accessible funds. Document the assumptions rather than presenting the result as a universal reserve target.

Plan for Temporary Provider Absence Without Assuming Costs Disappear

Provider absence creates a timing problem as well as a revenue problem. Collections from previously completed services may continue initially, masking the decline in new activity. The cash impact can become more visible later, including after the provider returns.

Model the absence and recovery in separate phases:

  1. Initial period: Estimate receipts from existing receivables and identify any immediate coverage expense.
  2. Reduced-activity period: Revise expected future collections based on feasible scheduling and coverage assumptions supplied by practice leadership.
  3. Return period: Allow for the time between resumed activity and cash collection; do not assume bank deposits recover immediately.

Confirm compensation arrangements rather than assuming provider payroll stops. Include administrative payroll, occupancy costs and other commitments that remain in place.

If insurance may help, review the actual policy with the broker. Do not count a possible benefit as available cash without understanding coverage, exclusions, documentation, timing and any applicable waiting period. Keep uncertain proceeds separate from the core cash forecast.

Make Financial Records Recoverable

A folder of reports is not enough if nobody knows whether the records are current or can access them when needed. Maintain a controlled financial continuity package with:

  • Reconciled bank balances and recent financial statements.
  • Accounts payable, receivables summaries and the payment calendar.
  • Payroll funding instructions and authorized contacts.
  • Debt, lease, insurance and significant vendor documents.
  • An entity and bank-account map, including responsibility for shared costs.
  • The current cash forecast, its assumptions and the date it was last updated.
  • Approved access and recovery procedures for essential financial systems.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals also make a continuity forecast more dependable.

Test recovery rather than assuming it works. Have an authorized backup person locate the latest forecast, reconcile it to available bank information and explain the next payment obligations. Store recovery credentials through an approved access-management process, not in a broadly shared spreadsheet.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary team chat and general-purpose AI tools. Financial planning can often use aggregate payer receipts, summarized receivables and entity-level expenses. If outside accounting work requires protected health information, review the arrangement and appropriate safeguards with qualified advisers. HHS guidance on covered entities and business associates explains the relevant roles; its minimum necessary guidance describes the standard and its exceptions.

Assign Authority Before a Disruption

The plan should distinguish who prepares information, who recommends action and who approves it. Otherwise, a practice may identify a shortfall without being able to respond.

Responsibility Primary role Backup or escalation
Update cash forecast and explain changes Finance lead or accounting team Designated reviewer with access to source records
Confirm payroll funding Practice manager or payroll lead Authorized payroll approver
Pause discretionary purchases Owner or delegated manager Named alternate within documented limits
Contact lender, landlord or major vendor Authorized owner or manager Designated relationship contact
Approve entity transfers or borrowing Authorized decision-maker CPA and legal review where needed

Set practice-specific triggers. Examples include a projected balance below the next essential payment requirement, a material payer delay or an absence extending beyond the modeled period. Define the action attached to each trigger and how quickly it must be reviewed.

During a disruption, record the decision, owner, expected cash effect and next review date. This keeps a temporary response from quietly becoming a permanent operating change.

Use Financial Tools to Support the Plan

Accounting systems, spreadsheets and AI-assisted work can help organize transactions, prepare draft summaries and compare assumptions. They do not establish whether a payer will pay on time, whether a contract permits deferral or whether a proposed action is authorized.

Evaluate the workflow rather than a feature list: can the team trace forecast inputs to records, identify stale assumptions, produce entity-level views and maintain an accessible backup process? A human reviewer should validate material figures and decisions.

Keep the Plan Ready to Use

Review the plan when payer patterns, staffing, locations, debt or ownership arrangements change. During ordinary operations, refresh it on a defined schedule; during a disruption, update it as cash assumptions change.

The owner's final test is practical: Can someone other than the usual decision-maker explain available cash, identify the next essential payments and carry out an authorized response? If not, address that gap before adding more forecast detail.

FAQs

How much cash should a private medical practice keep in reserve?

Base the target on essential payment timing, collection concentration, credible disruption scenarios and accessible financing. Model the lowest cash point through disruption and recovery. A generic number of months can overlook payroll timing, entity restrictions and provider dependence.

Is a profitable practice financially resilient?

Not necessarily. Profit and cash availability differ. Delayed receipts, debt principal payments or large purchases can create a cash shortage even when the income statement shows a profit.

How should a multi-location practice plan for a disruption?

Use location-level operating results alongside entity-level cash forecasts. Identify continuing shared costs, the entity responsible for payments and any constraints on moving funds. Do not rely solely on consolidated totals.

Can AI manage the continuity plan?

AI-assisted work can support draft analysis and scenario comparisons, but it should not replace verified records or authorized judgment. Keep assumptions visible, review material outputs and avoid entering patient-identifiable information into general-purpose AI tools.
