Medical Practice Cost of Capital Checklist

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Before financing equipment, renovating a location or committing owner cash, a private medical practice needs more than a quoted interest rate. The decision depends on the complete payment schedule, upfront cash requirements, collateral, personal guarantees and restrictions—and whether the practice can meet those obligations when payer receipts arrive later than expected.

The goal is to compare complete proposals on consistent terms, then evaluate the investment separately. An attractive loan does not make an unproductive purchase worthwhile. Paying cash avoids lender interest, but it can leave too little liquidity for provider and administrative payroll.

Use this checklist to build a decision packet for your finance team, CPA and attorney before signing.

1. Define the Purchase and the Borrower

Start with the capital decision, not the lender’s offer. Write down:

  • What you are buying: Equipment, installation, facility improvements or another defined investment.
  • The complete project budget: Include delivery, setup, training, necessary accessories and working capital during implementation.
  • When cash is needed: Separate deposits, delivery payments and later expenses.
  • Which entity will borrow and own the asset: Confirm the proposed borrower, asset owner and operating location.
  • How much the owners will contribute: Distinguish existing practice cash from new personal funds.
  • What cash must remain available: Set a reserve based on payroll, occupancy, existing debt and the timing of collections.

For a multi-location practice, identify which location receives the asset and which entity makes the payments. If another entity contributes cash or guarantees the debt, have your advisers review the arrangement rather than treating all accounts as one pool.

The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Those basics help establish how much cash is actually available for a capital commitment.

2. Collect the Complete Financing Proposal

Ask each financing provider for a written proposal, repayment schedule and applicable agreements. A marketing summary may omit terms that materially change the decision. Mark missing items as unresolved—not zero or “not applicable.”

Rates, Fees and Repayment

  • [ ] Gross amount financed and net funds available for the purchase.
  • [ ] Fixed or variable interest rate; for variable rates, the index, margin, reset timing and any floor or cap.
  • [ ] Every lender, broker, documentation, closing and ongoing fee.
  • [ ] Whether fees are paid upfront, withheld from proceeds or added to principal.
  • [ ] Payment amount, frequency and first payment date.
  • [ ] Amortization period, maturity date and any balloon payment.
  • [ ] Interest accrued during any payment deferral.
  • [ ] Conditions and charges for early repayment.
  • [ ] Late-payment charges and default-rate provisions.

A lower monthly payment may reflect a longer term or a larger final payment rather than cheaper financing. Compare the full schedule, not just the first payment.

Security, Guarantees and Restrictions

  • [ ] Assets pledged: only the purchased equipment or a broader set of practice assets.
  • [ ] Personal guarantees, including who signs and any stated limits or release conditions.
  • [ ] Other entities required to guarantee the obligation.
  • [ ] Restrictions on distributions, additional borrowing, asset sales or ownership changes.
  • [ ] Financial reporting requirements and financial covenants.
  • [ ] Cross-default provisions connecting this obligation to other agreements.
  • [ ] Insurance, account-balance or other ongoing requirements.
  • [ ] Approval conditions, expiration date and remaining closing requirements.

Ask an attorney to explain guarantee exposure, collateral rights, default provisions and restrictions. Do not assume that an equipment loan exposes only the equipment or that a proposal is binding before its conditions are satisfied.

Owner Cash and Equity Terms

  • [ ] Required down payment and other cash due at closing.
  • [ ] Whether the owner contribution is equity or a documented owner loan.
  • [ ] Any commitment to provide additional capital later.
  • [ ] For an equity proposal, ownership percentage before and after the transaction.
  • [ ] Distribution rights, voting rights, approval rights and exit or buyout provisions.
  • [ ] Any priority return, redemption obligation or other preferential economic terms.

Equity has no ordinary loan amortization schedule, but that does not make it free. Owners may give up future distributions, sale proceeds and decision-making rights. Have qualified legal counsel review any proposed ownership arrangement and its suitability for the practice.

4. Compare an Equipment Loan With an Owner-Funded Purchase

Illustrative example only—not a financing quote, market benchmark or recommendation. A practice is considering equipment with a total installed purchase cost of $120,000. It can use available practice cash or borrow $100,000 after a $20,000 down payment.

Assume a fixed-rate loan at 8% annually, repaid through 60 equal monthly payments beginning one month after funding. A $2,000 financing fee is paid separately at closing. There is no balloon or other charge in this simplified example.

The loan preserves $98,000 at closing but adds scheduled payments and financing charges. Paying cash avoids those charges but immediately reduces liquidity by the full purchase price.

The $23,660 is a total dollar financing cost over the loan term, not an annual percentage rate. For a more precise effective borrowing-cost comparison, ask the finance team to calculate the rate implied by net proceeds and all dated payments, including fees.

If personal funds are contributed instead of existing practice cash, document whether they are equity or an owner loan. That distinction changes the practice’s obligations and accounting; discuss the treatment with the CPA and attorney.

5. Keep Project Return Separate From Financing Cost

Prepare two linked analyses rather than blending everything into one profitability estimate.

First: Does the Investment Make Financial Sense?

Estimate the project’s incremental operating cash flows before financing. For equipment, consider:

  • Expected collected revenue—not simply charges submitted.
  • Timing of payer receipts and patient-balance collections.
  • Additional staffing, supplies, maintenance and other operating costs.
  • Installation downtime and the ramp-up period.
  • Whether revenue is genuinely new or shifts from existing equipment or another location.
  • Expected useful life and any supported residual value.

Use documented assumptions and downside scenarios. Financing should not conceal weak project economics.

Second: Can the Practice Fund It Safely?

Add the chosen financing schedule to the practice cash forecast. Test whether cash remains above the owner-approved reserve after payroll, rent, existing debt and other obligations.

Stress-test slower collections, delayed installation and lower-than-planned utilization. For variable-rate financing, also test higher payments. Include the balloon at maturity rather than assuming refinancing will be available.

An owner-funded purchase can be attractive on total cost yet unacceptable on liquidity. Conversely, affordable monthly payments do not prove that an investment will produce an adequate return.

6. Understand Why Textbook WACC May Not Be the Best Starting Point

Weighted-average cost of capital, or WACC, combines the estimated costs of debt and equity using their relative values. It can help with valuation and investment analysis, but a closely held medical practice may not have reliable inputs.

Ownership interests do not have a continuously observable market price. Book equity is not necessarily market value. An owner’s required return may reflect concentrated personal risk, limited liquidity and guarantees. Owner compensation for working in the practice must also be distinguished from returns on ownership.

Tax effects add complexity and depend on the entity, owners and transaction. Do not apply a generic tax adjustment or assume interest deductibility; ask the CPA to model the applicable treatment.

For an immediate purchase decision, prioritize complete proposals, dated cash flows, liquidity and contractual exposure. If WACC is needed for a larger valuation or investment analysis, have a qualified adviser document the assumptions and show how the conclusion changes across a reasonable range.

7. Assemble the Approval Packet

Before committing capital, give the decision-makers:

  1. The project budget and operating-return analysis.
  2. Completed comparison worksheets and written proposals.
  3. Base-case and downside cash forecasts by relevant entity.
  4. A summary of collateral, guarantees, restrictions and unresolved terms.
  5. CPA and legal review questions, with responsibility for resolving each.
  6. A written decision, funding source and minimum-cash requirement.

FAQs

Is the lowest interest rate always the lowest-cost financing?

No. Fees, payment timing, amortization, balloon payments and prepayment terms can change the effective cost. Compare net funds received against all scheduled payments, then review guarantees and restrictions separately.

Is owner-funded equipment free capital?

It avoids external interest if no borrowing is involved, but it uses cash that could support payroll, reserves or another investment. A new owner contribution may also create equity or an owner-loan obligation. Document the arrangement and review it with your advisers.

Does a private medical practice need WACC before accepting a loan?

Not necessarily. Complete loan terms, a project-return analysis and a cash forecast are usually more direct inputs for comparing specific proposals. WACC can be useful for broader analysis, but closely held ownership values and required returns may be difficult to estimate reliably.

What should remain unresolved before signing?

Ideally, no material financing term. Missing fees, an unclear guarantee, uncertain collateral coverage or an unexplained balloon should be resolved in writing. Have the CPA address accounting and tax treatment and the attorney review legal obligations before commitment.
