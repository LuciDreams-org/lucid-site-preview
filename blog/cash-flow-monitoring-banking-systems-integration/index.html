Connecting Bank Data to Medical Practice Cash Monitoring

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A bank balance can tell a medical practice owner how much money the bank reports. It cannot, by itself, tell the owner how much remains available after provider and administrative payroll, scheduled vendor payments, outstanding checks, and funds reserved for specific purposes.

Bank-connected financial data makes cash monitoring more useful when the practice knows which accounts are included, how current each connection is, and which obligations sit outside the feed. The operational question is: What cash can this entity use now, after accounting for known commitments and restrictions?

This guide explains how to select connections, map accounts, monitor missing or delayed data, and maintain a cash view during an outage. It addresses current cash and known obligations—not predictions of future payer receipts or a bank-reconciliation tutorial.

Define What Your Cash Monitor Must Show

Build a written inventory before connecting accounts. Include operating accounts, payroll accounts, savings and reserve accounts, and any other accounts holding practice funds. Track credit cards and credit lines separately; available borrowing capacity is not cash.

For each account, record:

  • Bank name and masked account identifier.
  • Legal entity that owns the account.
  • Purpose, such as operating receipts, payroll, or reserves.
  • Associated location, if the account is location-specific.
  • Accounting ledger account and connection method.
  • Person responsible for connection access and exception review.

A multi-location practice may collect receipts into one entity-level operating account. That account does not establish how much cash belongs to each location. Location profitability requires accounting allocations and other supporting records, not just bank activity.

Similarly, do not treat balances across separate entities as freely interchangeable. Keep the entity view primary and obtain appropriate advice before moving funds between entities.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports separate business accounts and complete records. Connecting a feed helps collect records; it does not replace those responsibilities.

Choose a Connection Method Based on Actual Coverage

Connection availability depends on the specific bank, account type, and receiving software. Confirm what is supported rather than assuming that an API or a named connector provides instantaneous updates.

Connection method What to confirm before selection Operational limitation to plan for
Built-in bank feed, direct or through a data provider Supported accounts, authorization process, refresh schedule, balance fields, and transaction history Refreshes may be delayed, interrupted, or require renewed authorization
Custom API connection Available endpoints, permissions, technical ownership, update frequency, and support responsibilities Requires implementation and maintenance; an API does not inherently mean continuous updates
Bank export and file import Accepted format, required columns, date range, transaction identifiers, and duplicate controls Data remains current only through the export's coverage period
Manual bank-portal verification Authorized access, balance definitions, and a documented review time Useful for fallback checks but not a substitute for transaction capture

A supported built-in feed may be sufficient for a staffed private practice. Custom development should solve a specific coverage or workflow problem, not simply pursue a “real-time” label.

For file imports, test the bank's available format—such as CSV or OFX—against the receiving system. Confirm debit and credit signs, date interpretation, account selection, and whether the export contains posted transactions only.

Separate data access from payment authority

Importing bank activity and initiating payments are different functions. Confirm whether the proposed connection only reads data or also permits payment actions. Use the least access needed for monitoring, with separate payment approvals where applicable.

Ask how authorization is granted, renewed, and revoked. Use the bank's and software provider's supported process rather than sharing credentials informally. Assign a backup administrator so an employee departure does not leave the practice unable to restore a feed.

Limit sensitive information

Cash monitoring usually needs account-level balances, deposit totals, transaction references, and obligation totals—not patient names or clinical details.

Bank descriptions and exported files can still contain sensitive information. Review what passes into reports and collaboration tools. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Where protected health information is involved, have the appropriate privacy lead review the workflow and service relationships. HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard provides relevant context, including exceptions.

Map and Test Accounts Before Relying on the Dashboard

Map each bank account to the correct entity and ledger account. Use masked account identifiers rather than names alone: two entities may both have an account labeled “Operating.”

Test the connection against bank records before using it for payment decisions. Confirm that:

  • Every expected account appears, including accounts requiring separate authorization.
  • The imported history begins on the intended date without an unexplained gap.
  • Balance fields are identified as current, ledger, or available, according to the bank's definitions.
  • Posted deposits and withdrawals retain usable dates, amounts, and references.
  • Pending transactions are distinguishable from posted transactions, if provided.
  • Transfers between practice accounts are not classified as new revenue or operating expense.

A payer deposit is evidence of cash received, not a complete explanation of the underlying collections. A patient-payment processor may also deposit a settlement net of fees or adjustments. Use appropriate settlement and billing summaries to explain those amounts; a bank feed alone does not update patient balances or establish collection accuracy.

Decide how pending transactions and duplicates will work

Some connections supply pending transactions; others supply only posted activity. Pending amounts and descriptions may change before posting. Confirm whether the receiving system replaces the pending entry, links it to the posted entry, or imports both for review.

Duplicates can also arise when a feed resumes after a manual import, an account is reconnected, or historical transactions are downloaded again. Record import date ranges and use available transaction identifiers. Review possible duplicates using account, amount, dates, reference, and status—not amount alone, since recurring payments can legitimately match.

Do not delete an apparent duplicate until the reviewer confirms which entry represents the actual transaction and whether either entry is already linked to accounting records.

Build a Cash-Monitoring Map Beyond Bank Balances

The dashboard should separate bank-reported money from commitments and restrictions. Give every figure a source and an “as of” time.

Cash-monitoring layer Source How to use it
Bank-reported balances Feed or verified bank portal Establish the starting balance for each entity and account; identify the balance type
Pending bank activity Bank portal or feed, if supplied Determine what is already reflected in the starting balance
Committed payroll Approved payroll funding summary Include provider/admin wages, employer obligations, and other funding requirements not already reflected
Scheduled payments and outstanding checks Approved payment register, accounts payable, and check records Identify committed outflows that have not reduced the starting balance
Restricted or designated funds Supporting agreements and management records Distinguish actual restrictions from internal reserves; identify overlap with other commitments
Residual cash after identified commitments Reviewed combination of the above Support current payment decisions without presenting it as profit or a forecast

Prevent double counting. If an available balance already reflects a pending payroll debit, subtracting the same payroll requirement again understates cash. Conversely, a scheduled payment in accounts payable may not appear at the bank yet and still needs to be considered.

Keep a reference linking each commitment to its bank or payment status. Record whether it is already reflected in the starting balance and whether it overlaps with a reserve.

Illustrative example: reviewing cash before releasing payments

The following amounts are an illustrative example, not practice benchmarks.

A single-entity practice verifies an operating-account available balance of $180,000. Its reviewer confirms that the following amounts are not reflected in that balance and do not overlap:

Item Treatment Amount
Verified available balance Starting point $180,000
Approved payroll funding requirement Subtract $70,000
Scheduled vendor payments and outstanding checks Subtract $25,000
Management-designated reserve Exclude from discretionary use $20,000
Residual after identified commitments and reserve Operational reference $65,000

The residual is not permission to distribute $65,000. It excludes unidentified obligations and does not establish profitability or future collection timing. The owner should review unresolved items before approving discretionary spending.

Do not add expected payer deposits or unpaid patient balances to current available cash. Those are collection expectations, not money currently available at the bank.

Distinguish Feed Freshness From Accounting Accuracy

A feed that refreshed recently can still contain uncategorized transactions, duplicates, missing accounts, or incorrect mappings. A reconciled accounting period can also be accurate while today's connection is stale.

Track these dimensions separately:

Status What it answers Evidence to display
Connection freshness When did data last arrive successfully? Last successful refresh and any error message
Data coverage Are the required accounts and dates included? Account inventory, import ranges, and known gaps
Review status Have exceptions been investigated? Open pending/posting issues, duplicates, and unmapped items
Accounting accuracy Have books been checked against supporting records? Last reconciled period and unresolved differences

A “connected” indicator is not proof of complete data. An account with no new activity may be valid—or may have stopped updating. Compare expected accounts with received data and investigate unexplained silence.

Show the time of the last successful retrieval separately from the date of the newest transaction. These timestamps answer different questions. Label stale data clearly rather than allowing an old balance to look current.

Assign a Repeatable Monitoring Workflow

Choose a review frequency based on payment timing and cash sensitivity. A review before payroll funding or a large payment release may matter more than a generic dashboard schedule.

The practice manager can check account coverage and feed status, while the bookkeeper investigates imports and mappings. The payroll administrator confirms approved funding totals, and the owner or authorized approver decides which payments to release. Name backups for each responsibility.

At each review:

  1. Check expected accounts against connected accounts.
  2. Inspect successful-refresh times and connection errors.
  3. Refresh payroll and scheduled-payment commitments from approved records.
  4. Investigate pending items, missing deposits, duplicates, and unusual transfers.
  5. Confirm restrictions, designated reserves, and entity boundaries.
  6. Escalate unresolved gaps before discretionary payments are approved.

Use alerts where the selected system supports them, but retain a manual status check. A low-balance alert cannot protect the practice if its underlying balance has stopped updating.

Outage-Response Checklist

A missing feed is a data problem, not proof that funds are missing. It also should not be treated as harmless when payment decisions depend on it.

  • Identify the scope: Record affected accounts, entities, last successful refresh, and known missing dates.
  • Mark the dashboard: Label stale balances and notify the payment approver.
  • Verify directly: Have an authorized user check the bank portal for balances, pending activity, and posted transactions. Record the verification time.
  • Update commitments: Confirm payroll funding, scheduled debits, outstanding checks, and restrictions independently of the failed feed.
  • Restore access: Check authorization expiry, bank notices, account changes, and provider support guidance through approved channels.
  • Control fallback imports: Log account identifiers and exact date ranges before importing files. Avoid overlapping imports without duplicate review.
  • Check recovery: Verify coverage across the outage period and review overlapping, missing, and pending-to-posted entries.
  • Close the incident: Record resolution, reviewer, and remaining exceptions before removing the stale-data warning.

Make the Connection Useful to the Owner

A reliable cash monitor combines bank data with clear account ownership, visible freshness, and reviewed commitments. It should help an owner decide whether a payment can be released from the correct entity—not imply that every displayed dollar is available to spend.

FAQs

Does a bank connection show my medical practice's cash in real time?

Not necessarily. Update timing depends on the bank, connection provider, receiving system, and transaction status. Check the last successful refresh and verify balances directly with the bank when a time-sensitive payment depends on them. A recent refresh still does not establish that transactions are correctly accounted for.

Can I use the available balance to decide whether payroll is covered?

Use it as a starting point. Confirm the payroll funding requirement and timing, other committed payments, and any restricted or designated funds. Determine whether pending payroll debits are already reflected in the balance so you do not count them twice. Verify the specific account and entity responsible for funding payroll.

What should we do when one bank account disappears from the dashboard?

Compare the dashboard with the account inventory, mark the missing account's data as unavailable, and check authorization and mapping. Obtain a direct bank verification through an authorized user. Do not treat the missing balance as zero or leave an old balance unlabeled. After restoration, check the missing period and possible duplicate imports.

Does connecting bank feeds eliminate reconciliation?

No. Feeds collect data; reconciliation checks accounting records against bank records and helps identify differences. Keep feed freshness, exception review, and the last reconciled period visible as separate statuses. A functioning connection does not replace bookkeeping review.
