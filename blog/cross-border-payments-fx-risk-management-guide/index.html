FX Risk in Cross-Border Medical Practice Payments

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A foreign-currency vendor invoice can change in dollar cost between the day a medical practice commits to a purchase and the day it pays. That difference matters when the same operating account must also cover provider payroll, administrative wages, rent, and routine supplies.

For a US private practice paying a legitimate overseas equipment or administrative-service vendor, the goal is not to predict currency markets. It is to understand the obligation, compare the full cost of payment choices, and preserve enough cash to meet other commitments.

This guide focuses on transaction exposure: the risk that an agreed foreign-currency payment will require more US dollars before it is settled. It does not recommend currency speculation or specific hedging products.

Identify Where the Currency Exposure Begins and Ends

Start with the contract or purchase order, not just the invoice. A practice may become committed to a foreign-currency price before the vendor issues its final bill.

For example, an equipment order might require a deposit at acceptance and a balance before shipment. Each unpaid installment has its own amount, due date, and period of currency exposure.

The key questions are:

  • What currency is the practice legally required to pay?
  • When does the price become binding, and can the vendor revise it?
  • How much remains unpaid?
  • When must the vendor receive the funds?
  • When will the practice's dollar cost become fixed?
  • What happens if delivery is delayed, the order is canceled, or a refund is issued?

For a straightforward payment, the dollar cost generally becomes fixed when the conversion is executed at a confirmed rate. Settlement may happen later. A payment instruction entered today does not necessarily establish today's rate; confirm when the provider actually converts the funds.

Overseas Vendor Does Not Always Mean FX Exposure

An overseas vendor may offer a binding US-dollar price. If the practice owes a fixed dollar amount with no currency adjustment clause, that invoice does not leave the practice directly exposed to changes in the foreign exchange rate.

However, dollar pricing is not automatically cheaper. The vendor may build a currency cushion into its quote, and transfer fees may still apply. Compare a firm dollar quote with the total dollar cost of the foreign-currency alternative.

Likewise, a quote displayed in dollars may not be fixed if the contract allows repricing before shipment. The contractual payment terms matter more than the currency symbol on a preliminary estimate.

Build a Simple Exposure Register

An exposure register is a list of unpaid foreign-currency commitments. It helps the owner and practice manager see which obligations can change in dollar cost and when cash will be needed.

Keep committed payments separate from possible future purchases. Otherwise, a tentative equipment plan can be mistaken for a payable that must be funded.

Illustrative example: The following register describes invented obligations for a practice reviewing overseas vendor payments.

Responsible entity and location Equipment-owning entity; north location Operating entity; shared administration
Commitment status Signed purchase order Approved monthly invoice

Add the current planning rate, estimated dollar requirement, adverse-rate scenario, payment lead time, and next review date to the working version.

For a multi-entity practice, identify the entity that owes the invoice and the account expected to fund it. Do not treat a consolidated bank balance as freely available cash for every entity. Ask the CPA how any funding between entities should be handled.

For shared administrative costs, note the allocation method used in location reporting. This keeps a cross-border payment from becoming an unexplained expense at whichever location happened to pay it.

Compare the Total Dollar Cost, Not Just the Transfer Fee

A low advertised transfer fee does not establish that a payment is inexpensive. The exchange rate may include a conversion spread, and intermediary or receiving-bank deductions may affect what the vendor receives.

Request quotes on the same basis:

Comparison item What to confirm
Invoice currency Is the obligation fixed in USD or a foreign currency?
Foreign amount delivered Will the vendor receive the full invoice amount?
Executable exchange rate What rate will actually apply, and when does the quote expire?
Conversion spread Is the conversion cost already included in that rate?
Transfer fees Which charges are paid separately by the practice?
Other deductions Could intermediary or receiving charges reduce delivery?
Timing When is cash debited, converted, and available to the vendor?
Changes and refunds What happens if the transfer or purchase changes?

For a rate expressed as US dollars per unit of foreign currency:

Estimated dollar outflow = foreign-currency amount × executable exchange rate + separately charged fees.

If the executable rate already includes the provider's spread, do not add that spread again. If deductions could leave the vendor short, obtain a quote for delivering the full required amount rather than assuming the invoice will be satisfied.

Illustrative Equipment-Invoice Comparison

All amounts and rate changes below are illustrative, not market quotes or forecasts. Assume a vendor offers either a EUR 20,000 equipment invoice or a binding USD 22,400 alternative for the same equipment and delivery terms.

For the euro payment, assume:

  • A starting planning rate of USD 1.10 per euro.
  • A conversion markup of 1% applied to each scenario rate.
  • A separate USD 40 transfer fee.
  • No additional deductions, so the vendor receives EUR 20,000.

Assume the USD 22,400 alternative includes all payment charges, remains available through the payment date, and cannot be repriced for currency movements. These assumptions must be verified in a real comparison.

At the starting rate, the euro payment costs $140 less than the fixed-dollar alternative. In the adverse scenario, it costs $971 more. The euro payment's dollar requirement increases by $1,111 between the starting and adverse scenarios, including the change in the assumed markup.

This does not establish which choice is best. It shows the price of certainty under these assumptions and the additional cash the practice might need if it leaves the euro amount unconverted.

Choose stress scenarios that inform the actual decision. They are planning tests, not predictions of how far a currency will move.

Match Payment Timing to Available Practice Cash

Converting earlier can remove exchange-rate uncertainty for the converted amount, but it also uses dollars sooner. Holding foreign currency does not mean those funds remain available for US payroll or other dollar obligations without another conversion.

Paying the vendor early goes further: it also places cash with the vendor before the original due date. Consider cancellation rights, delivery uncertainty, and refund terms before doing so.

Build the payment into the practice's rolling cash forecast:

  1. Reserve essential outflows. Include provider and administrative payroll, rent, routine vendors, debt payments, and other scheduled obligations.
  2. Forecast receipts conservatively. Distinguish cash already received from expected payer receipts and patient-balance collections. Do not assume receivables will arrive exactly when needed.
  3. Add the foreign payment at its all-in dollar cost. Show both the planning amount and an adverse-rate amount.
  4. Allow for processing time. Confirm conversion timing, transfer cutoffs, and the vendor's required receipt date with the provider.
  5. Set a decision date. Decide when to approve conversion or obtain adviser input rather than repeatedly delaying payment in hope of a better rate.

If the adverse scenario would leave insufficient cash for payroll, the owner needs a funding or purchase-timing decision—not a currency prediction. Possible discussions include revising the purchase schedule, negotiating payment milestones, or seeking a binding dollar quote. Each has commercial costs and should be evaluated before commitment.

Review Refunds and Changed Orders Before Paying

An invoice can be settled successfully and still create later currency exposure if the vendor refunds money in foreign currency.

Ask whether a refund will be made in the invoice currency or as a fixed dollar amount. Confirm who bears conversion charges, whether original transfer fees are recoverable, and whether the vendor can deduct cancellation or restocking charges.

A refund of the same foreign-currency amount does not necessarily restore the original dollar outflow. The exchange rate may have changed, and a second conversion may carry additional costs.

For equipment orders, document what happens if shipment is delayed or only part of the order is supplied. For administrative services, distinguish a cash refund from a credit against future invoices. A credit does not replenish the practice's operating account today.

Questions for Banking or Treasury Advisers

For a material or recurring exposure, bring the register and cash forecast to the practice's banking or treasury adviser before choosing a risk-management arrangement.

Ask:

  • Can you show the total dollar cost of delivering the required foreign amount, with all known fees identified?
  • When does the rate become binding, and what happens if settlement is delayed?
  • What arrangements, if any, fit the actual invoice amount and payment date?
  • What upfront funding, deposits, collateral, or credit capacity could be required?
  • Could an arrangement create additional cash demands before the vendor payment is due?
  • What happens if the order is reduced, delayed, or canceled?
  • What are the costs and obligations of changing or ending the arrangement?
  • What flexibility or benefit would the practice give up in exchange for greater cost certainty?

Do not enter an arrangement simply because it is described as protection. The obligations should fit a documented business payment, and the owner should understand potential cash demands. Avoid transactions intended to profit from currency movements rather than support practice operations.

Close the Loop After Settlement

Retain the contract, invoice, approval, conversion confirmation, fee details, bank debit, and evidence of the amount delivered. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts, and reconciled bank records.

Compare actual dollar outflow with the forecast. Separate the effect of rate changes from fees, changes in the order, and timing differences. Ask the CPA how the invoice, settlement differences, and charges should be recorded; this payment-planning worksheet is not a substitute for accounting treatment.

Close the exposure register entry only after confirming the obligation has been satisfied. Track unresolved credits or refunds separately. Vendor-payment analysis generally does not need patient-identifiable information.

FAQs

Does every overseas invoice create FX risk for a medical practice?

No. A binding dollar invoice without currency-based repricing leaves the practice owing a fixed dollar amount. A foreign-currency invoice generally leaves the dollar cost uncertain until conversion is fixed. Review the contract as well as the invoice, including fees and refund terms.

Should the practice convert currency as soon as an invoice arrives?

Not automatically. Earlier conversion can establish the dollar cost, but it uses cash earlier and may create a later conversion need if the purchase changes. Compare the payment deadline, available cash, cancellation terms, and effect on payroll reserves before deciding.

How much currency exposure deserves owner attention?

Use the potential dollar impact rather than a universal percentage threshold. Escalate an exposure when an adverse-rate scenario could disrupt essential payments, exceed purchasing authority, or materially change the approved equipment or operating budget. Recurring smaller invoices also deserve review when their combined cash requirement becomes meaningful.

How often should the exposure register be reviewed?

Review it when a commitment is made or changed, during the cash-planning cycle, and before conversion or payment. Review again when expected receipts, shipment dates, or refund terms change. The review frequency should reflect the size of the obligation and how soon funds must arrive.
