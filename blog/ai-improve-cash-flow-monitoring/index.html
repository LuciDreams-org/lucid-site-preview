AI-Assisted Cash Flow Monitoring for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A practice’s bank balance can change without making the reason clear. A payer deposit may arrive in several batches, a patient-payment settlement may be net of fees, or a payroll withdrawal may differ from the payroll summary someone reviewed. An unusual transaction deserves attention, but it is not automatically an error.

For owners and managers of staffed U.S. private medical practices, AI-assisted cash flow monitoring should support a controlled exception-review process. The purpose is to understand cash movements already recorded, identify differences that need investigation and assign a person to resolve them.

This is not cash forecasting. The workflow below compares observed activity with documented receipts and approved disbursements for the review period. It does not project future collections, recommend hiring or estimate how long cash will last.

AI may help surface duplicate-looking payments, unusual timing or mismatched amounts. Source records, reconciliation and human approval must determine what happens next.

1. Establish the Review Scope and Confirm Data Freshness

Before reviewing alerts, define which accounts, entities and dates are included. A consolidated cash total can hide a problem in an individual practice entity or omit an account used for payroll.

Maintain an account list with the legal entity, account purpose and responsible reviewer. Include operating accounts, payroll accounts and any separate accounts receiving practice collections. For multiple locations sharing one account, use supporting settlement and accounting records to identify location activity; the bank description alone may not provide that detail.

Then confirm how current each source is. An export generated today may contain transactions only through an earlier date. Pending bank activity also should not be treated as settled cash.

If a source is stale, mark the review as incomplete rather than concluding that cash is missing. Record the gap, who will obtain the updated source and when they will check it again.

Keep posted and pending activity in separate views. Otherwise, the same withdrawal may appear twice as its status changes.

2. Compare Observed Cash With Documented Activity

Use a consistent review period and cutoff. Compare what the bank shows with the supporting records for that same period, allowing for documented settlement timing.

“Expected” in this workflow means an amount supported by an existing record: a released payment batch, an approved payroll run or a settlement report. It does not mean a prediction based on average collections.

Review receipts by deposit or settlement batch

For payer receipts, match bank deposits to available payment or remittance references. One bank deposit may cover multiple batches, and one documented payment may require investigation across multiple entries. Do not assume every receipt has a one-to-one match.

For patient payments, compare the settlement report with the posted deposit. Determine whether fees, refunds or other adjustments explain the difference between gross collections and net cash. A gross-to-net difference should not be classified as lost revenue without checking those components.

Keep patient balances separate from received cash. An outstanding patient balance is not a bank receipt and should not be counted as one in the monitoring register.

Review disbursements against approvals

Match payroll withdrawals to approved funding reports, including separately withdrawn components. Compare vendor payments with invoices, payment references and approval records.

For shared costs, distinguish the bank movement from its accounting allocation. A rent payment from one entity’s account may require allocation or an inter-entity entry, but allocating that expense across locations does not create another cash outflow.

Similarly, transfers between included practice accounts should be identified on both sides. They change account-level balances but should not be counted as new receipts or expenses in a combined view.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping and bank reconciliation provides a useful foundation: maintain complete records, keep business accounts separate and reconcile bank records with the books.

3. Use AI to Prioritize Exceptions, Not Establish Conclusions

Depending on the tool and the records available, AI-assisted analysis may help identify transactions worth reviewing. It should produce a question and supporting references—not an unsupported verdict.

Useful exception categories include:

  • Duplicate-looking disbursements: Similar vendor names, amounts and dates appear more than once.
  • Timing differences: A documented settlement or released payment does not appear within its stated processing window.
  • Amount differences: The bank amount differs from the supporting settlement, invoice or payroll report.
  • Unmatched transactions: A posted item has no identified source record or ledger match.
  • Entity or account mismatches: Activity appears in an account other than the one shown in the supporting documentation.

Each alert should identify the source records, dates, amount, reason for review and relevant uncertainty. “Two payments have the same amount” is useful evidence. “The vendor was paid twice by mistake” is a conclusion that still requires investigation.

A repeat amount might represent two legitimate invoices. A late-looking deposit might reflect a weekend or a different cutoff. An unfamiliar bank description might be a known vendor’s payment processor.

Do not allow an AI alert alone to trigger a payment cancellation, refund, accounting correction or accusation of fraud. Investigate and reconcile the relevant records, then obtain the required human approval before changing cash or the books.

4. Maintain a Weekly Exception Register

A register turns alerts into accountable work. It also prevents the same unresolved issue from being rediscovered every week without an owner.

Include a status, date opened, follow-up deadline, evidence reference and approver alongside these fields. Use a controlled document reference rather than copying sensitive source material into every register row.

A closed item should explain what happened. “Reviewed” is not enough. Record whether it was a timing difference, valid separate payment, recording error or confirmed issue requiring further action.

5. Investigate, Reconcile and Obtain Approval

Use the same sequence for each exception so that urgency does not replace evidence.

Verify the underlying records

Start with the original bank activity and relevant settlement report, invoice, payroll funding detail or payment confirmation. Confirm the entity, account, amount, date and transaction reference.

If records conflict, document the conflict. Do not force a match simply because the amounts are similar.

Reconcile the difference

Determine whether the explanation is a timing item, a net settlement, a missing entry, a duplicate import or an actual payment problem. A duplicate ledger entry is not the same as a duplicate bank withdrawal: the first affects the books, while the second affects cash.

For a cash monitoring review, reconcile the flagged activity to its source records and the ledger. Continue the practice’s formal bank reconciliation process as well; reviewing alerts is not a replacement for reconciling the full account.

Approve the response

The reviewer should recommend a specific action with supporting evidence. Depending on the issue, that might be correcting an entry, contacting the payer about a documented settlement, asking a vendor to investigate a payment or taking no action beyond recording the explanation.

Use the practice’s approval rules before releasing funds, requesting a refund, reversing a payment or posting a correction. Where practical, separate preparation from approval. If staffing limits that separation, have the owner review the evidence and proposed action independently.

For a suspicious withdrawal, promptly follow the practice’s established bank-contact and escalation procedures. An alert alone is not proof of fraud.

6. Set a Review Cadence and Protect Sensitive Information

A practical cadence is a brief check of current activity when updated records are available, followed by a weekly review of unresolved exceptions. The appropriate frequency depends on transaction volume, payroll activity and the availability of reliable source data.

The weekly owner or manager review should answer:

  1. Are all included accounts current through the stated cutoff?
  2. Which receipts or disbursements remain unmatched?
  3. Which items involve confirmed cash differences versus incomplete information?
  4. Who owns each follow-up, and when is it due?
  5. Which proposed actions need approval?

Keep confirmed corrections separate from monitoring flags. Track repeated false positives and adjust review criteria so the team is not repeatedly investigating an explained settlement pattern.

Financial review should use the least patient information needed for the task. Batch references, totals and non-identifying transaction IDs may be sufficient. Do not put patient-identifiable records into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool.

HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions. If a workflow involves protected health information, obtain appropriate privacy and security review. HHS cloud guidance also explains that cloud services maintaining electronic protected health information can have business-associate responsibilities; encryption alone does not settle those requirements.

Make Monitoring an Accountable Finance Routine

The useful output of AI-assisted cash monitoring is not a dashboard full of warnings. It is a reviewed record of what moved, what remains unexplained and who is responsible for resolving it.

Start with current source records, compare posted activity with documented receipts and approved payments, and keep an exception register. Require reconciliation and human approval before acting on an alert.

FAQs

How is cash flow monitoring different from cash forecasting?

Monitoring reviews observed transactions and compares them with supporting records. Forecasting estimates future cash receipts and payments. A settlement that should already have arrived belongs in an exception review; estimating next month’s collections is a separate planning task.

Does an AI flag mean a duplicate payment or fraud occurred?

No. Similar transactions may be legitimate, and timing differences may reflect processing or incomplete data. Verify bank records, invoices, payment references and approvals. Reconcile the difference and obtain human approval before making a correction or changing a payment.

How much historical data is needed to begin?

There is no universal history requirement for this workflow. Begin with current, complete bank records and the supporting documents for the review period. Historical activity can add context, but it cannot replace evidence for an individual transaction. Tool-specific data requirements should be evaluated separately.

Can one register cover several locations or entities?

Yes, provided every item identifies the relevant entity, account and location where supported. Review account-level activity before combining totals. Match internal transfers on both sides and avoid counting shared-cost allocations as additional cash movements.

What should happen when a source has not updated?

Label the affected review incomplete, record the latest available date and assign someone to obtain an updated source. Do not classify an absent transaction as a confirmed missing payment solely because it is absent from a stale export.
