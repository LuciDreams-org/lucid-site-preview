Seasonal Cash Flow Planning for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can have a full appointment calendar and still face a cash shortage. Visits, claims, payer payments and patient collections occur on different schedules, while provider and administrative payroll continue to come due.

Seasonal planning helps owners connect those schedules before approving leave coverage, equipment purchases or owner distributions. The goal is not to predict every deposit perfectly. It is to identify when cash could become tight, understand why and decide what to do while there is still time.

For a staffed private practice, an effective plan combines a seasonal operating calendar, a weekly cash forecast and a clear review process. It also keeps two questions separate: Can the practice deliver the scheduled care, and can it fund its obligations when they come due?

Separate seasonal capacity from seasonal cash flow

Operational capacity concerns available providers, support staff, appointment slots and office hours. Cash planning concerns the timing of receipts and payments. The two interact, but they are not interchangeable.

Provider leave may reduce visits immediately, while collections remain steady because payers are still paying earlier claims. The cash impact can arrive after the provider returns. Conversely, a busy month may increase staffing and supply costs before the related receipts reach the bank.

Seasonal event Capacity question Cash planning question
Provider vacation Who will cover appointments, and which visits will be rescheduled? When will lower visit volume affect collections?
Holiday office closure How many clinical and administrative workdays are available? Which payroll, rent and vendor payments still fall during the closure?
Seasonal appointment increase Can existing staff and space support the schedule? Will additional payroll and supplies be paid before collections arrive?
Change in patient payment patterns Does the schedule or front-desk workload change? How much patient cash is likely to arrive, and when?
Payer processing delay Can billing staff address the affected claims? Which expected deposits should move into later forecast weeks?

Do not assume that lower appointment volume produces an equivalent reduction in expenses. Salaried compensation, benefits, rent and many service contracts may continue unchanged. Review actual employment terms and payment commitments rather than applying a blanket expense reduction.

1. Build a seasonal calendar from your own practice data

Start with available historical records, ideally covering more than one seasonal cycle. Compare like periods, but identify changes that make prior years less representative: a new provider, a closed location, a different payer mix or a major change in office hours.

Bring together three views:

  • Operating calendar: provider leave, holidays, office closures, planned coverage and expected appointment patterns.
  • Collection history: payer deposits, patient receipts and the timing between services and collections, using aggregate information where possible.
  • Payment calendar: provider and administrative payroll, payroll-related payments, rent, debt service, recurring vendors, equipment commitments and planned owner distributions.

Look at weeks as well as months. A monthly total can conceal a payroll payment that occurs before a large payer deposit. Depending on the payroll calendar, some months may also contain more pay dates than others.

Record the reason for each expected seasonal change. “Provider leave reduces available visits” is an assumption the manager can check. “Summer is always slow” is too vague to support a spending decision.

2. Establish reliable starting cash and records

A forecast built on an unexplained bank balance will be difficult to trust. Reconcile accounts, identify outstanding payments and distinguish available operating cash from amounts that are restricted, committed or otherwise unavailable for general spending.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. Those practices also provide a sound foundation for cash planning.

Before forecasting, confirm:

  • Which bank accounts belong to each legal entity.
  • Whether recent payroll, vendor payments and deposits have been recorded.
  • Which transfers are pending.
  • Whether owner contributions or distributions have been classified correctly.
  • Whether any account balances should be excluded from available operating cash.

Keep bank-to-bank transfers separate from operating receipts. Moving money between accounts does not create new cash for the practice as a whole.

3. Forecast receipts by expected payment date

Charges, reported revenue and cash receipts are different measures. A seasonal cash forecast should estimate when money will become available, not simply repeat the production budget.

Separate expected receipts into useful categories, such as major payer groups, patient collections and other operating receipts. Use enough detail to explain timing differences without creating an unmanageable model.

For payer receipts, review historical payment timing alongside current aggregate receivables information and known processing issues. Do not assume every outstanding claim will be collected in the next forecast period. Likewise, patient balances should not be treated as immediate cash simply because statements have been issued.

For future visits, estimate collections using the practice's own experience with service mix, payer mix and collection timing. Avoid treating gross charges as expected deposits. Also avoid counting the same receipts once in existing receivables and again in projected future collections.

Where timing is uncertain, show that uncertainty explicitly. A delayed-receipts scenario is more useful than silently assuming every deposit will arrive on schedule.

4. Place outflows on their actual payment schedule

List payments by the week they are expected to leave the account. Separate recurring obligations from spending that can still be changed.

Outflow category What to include Planning decision
Provider and administrative payroll Scheduled payroll, approved coverage and known compensation changes What staffing commitments already exist?
Payroll-related payments and benefits Expected withdrawals based on actual schedules Are these included separately without double counting payroll?
Occupancy and recurring services Rent, utilities, software and contracted services Which costs continue during closures?
Clinical and office supplies Planned orders and existing vendor obligations Can discretionary orders be timed differently without disrupting care?
Debt and equipment Scheduled payments and approved purchases Can an uncommitted purchase wait until after the forecast low point?
Taxes and owner distributions Amounts and timing reviewed with the practice's advisers What cash must remain available before a distribution is approved?

Use confirmed payment schedules where available. Have the CPA or relevant adviser confirm uncertain tax obligations rather than inserting a generic percentage or deadline.

5. Build a rolling weekly cash forecast

Choose a forecast horizon that extends through the expected seasonal dip and the subsequent collection period. A rolling 13-week view is one practical starting format; extend it if the effects of leave or payment delays run longer.

For each week, calculate:

Beginning available cash + expected receipts − expected payments = ending available cash.

Carry the ending balance into the following week. Keep financing, owner contributions and distributions visible as separate lines so operating cash needs do not disappear inside a net total.

Illustrative example: collections fall after provider leave

The following hypothetical example shows a practice experiencing lower receipts after provider leave. All amounts are illustrative, not benchmarks or recommended cash balances.

Cash forecast Week 1 Week 2 Week 3 Week 4
Beginning available cash $90,000 $80,000 $55,000 $50,000
Expected payer and patient receipts $45,000 $35,000 $50,000 $60,000
Expected payments $55,000 $60,000 $55,000 $50,000
Ending available cash $80,000 $55,000 $50,000 $60,000

The forecast's low point is Week 3, even though the leave may already be over. That timing matters when deciding whether to purchase equipment or make an owner distribution in Week 1.

The $50,000 low point is not automatically adequate. The owner still needs to review upcoming obligations, receipt uncertainty and the period beyond Week 4. A positive ending balance alone does not establish that all commitments are comfortably funded.

6. Set a cash floor from commitments, not a generic rule

Avoid choosing a reserve solely because a round number or a fixed number of months sounds prudent. Determine the practice's operating cash floor from its payment calendar and exposure to delayed receipts.

Consider:

  • Essential payments due before the next reasonably dependable collection cycle.
  • The reliability and concentration of expected payer receipts.
  • Known payroll or coverage commitments during the seasonal period.
  • Debt, tax and other obligations not visible in routine operating expenses.
  • The availability, terms and timing of any existing financing arrangements.

Document the assumptions behind the floor and revisit them when staffing, locations or payment patterns change. Financing should not be treated as cash already available unless access and terms have been confirmed.

Then establish decision triggers. For example, if the downside forecast falls below the practice's documented operating floor, pause an uncommitted capital purchase and review discretionary distributions before approving them. Assign responsibility for each action and specify when a decision is needed.

7. Test delays and lower receipts without confusing them

At minimum, compare an expected case with a downside case. Make the changes specific enough to explain.

A timing scenario moves receipts into later weeks without necessarily reducing the total ultimately collected. A lower-collections scenario reduces expected cash because fewer services occur or collections are weaker than anticipated. These can require different responses.

Useful tests include:

  • A major payer deposit arriving later than expected.
  • Provider leave extending beyond the original schedule.
  • Patient receipts coming in below the practice's recent experience.
  • Approved coverage costs exceeding the initial estimate.
  • A planned purchase occurring before, rather than after, the cash low point.

Do not assume that adding appointment capacity immediately fixes a cash shortage. Additional visits may require spending now while collections arrive later. Evaluate the cash timing separately from the operational value of those visits.

8. Keep location performance and entity cash distinct

Multi-location practices need both a performance view and a bank-account view. A location P&L can show where revenue and expenses arise, but it does not establish how much cash a particular legal entity can use.

Maintain entity-level cash forecasts where accounts and obligations are separate. Use location reporting to understand the seasonal drivers, and apply a consistent, documented method for allocating shared administrative costs in management reports.

Show intercompany transfers separately and eliminate them from consolidated cash totals to avoid double counting. Do not assume cash in one entity can automatically cover another entity's payroll; review proposed transfers and their accounting treatment with the practice's advisers.

Make the forecast a weekly management routine

Each week, replace estimates with actual receipts and payments, reconcile the starting position and extend the forecast. Review the largest differences between forecast and actual results. Was a receipt late, was the amount overestimated, or was an expense missing?

The practice manager can update leave, scheduling and payment information. The bookkeeping team can verify recorded activity and balances. The owner can approve spending decisions, while the CPA or finance adviser reviews assumptions and financing or tax questions.

Use aggregate financial data whenever possible. Patient-level detail is usually unnecessary for a seasonal cash discussion. Where protected health information is involved, applicable requirements and arrangements need appropriate review; HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard explains the standard and its exceptions. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.
