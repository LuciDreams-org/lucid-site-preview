Explainable AI in Medical Practice Financial Compliance

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An AI-generated warning that says “possible payroll compliance issue” is not enough to act on. A practice manager needs to know which payroll period is affected, what supporting record appears to be missing, and whether the system actually had access to that record.

For owners of staffed private medical practices, explainable AI means making an individual financial output understandable enough for a qualified person to examine and challenge it. The goal is not to make every warning sound convincing. It is to connect the warning to verifiable evidence, identify assumptions, and support a documented human decision.

This matters when reviewing inconsistent tax coding, missing payroll support, or an unusual payment approval. A flag may identify a real problem, a harmless exception, or an incomplete data export. A plausible explanation alone cannot distinguish among them—and does not prove correctness or compliance.

What Makes an AI Flag Ready for Review?

A useful explanation should let someone other than the person who generated it locate the underlying records and understand the proposed concern.

Before sending a flag to your CPA or another reviewer, ask:

  • What exactly was flagged? Identify the transaction, payroll period, account, location, or legal entity.
  • Which records support the flag? Provide accessible references to the relevant documents or ledger entries.
  • What comparison produced the concern? Name the coding policy, approval policy, reconciliation expectation, or other basis used.
  • What is unknown? Identify missing files, incomplete exports, assumptions, and alternative explanations.
  • What decision is requested? Specify whether the reviewer should confirm a classification, request evidence, approve an exception, or evaluate a tax or legal question.

If the explanation cannot answer these questions, it may be ready for evidence gathering—but not for a substantive compliance conclusion.

Separate Evidence, Model Rationale, and Professional Judgment

The most important discipline is keeping three layers separate. An AI-generated paragraph can blend them together so smoothly that an assumption starts to look like a fact.

Layer What it should contain Medical-practice example What it does not establish
Source evidence Records that a reviewer can inspect An invoice, ledger entry, payroll register, or approval record Whether the transaction received the correct legal or tax treatment
Model rationale The stated reason for the flag, including comparisons and assumptions Similar vendor charges were assigned different accounting codes That the charges are economically identical or that the explanation faithfully describes the model’s internal process
Reviewer conclusion A human decision supported by checked evidence Keep the coding because the invoices describe different services That every related transaction or filing is correct

A generated narrative is not necessarily a faithful account of how a model produced its output. It may be a summary written after the prediction. Likewise, a feature-importance display can show which inputs influenced a model without establishing that those inputs are accurate or legally relevant.

For practice owners, the practical requirement is a checkable explanation, not an elaborate account of hidden model reasoning. Ask the reviewer to verify the cited facts and test the inference independently.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those records provide a foundation for reviewing financial flags; an AI explanation does not replace them.

Use Case: Inconsistent Tax Coding

An AI output might report that recurring charges from the same vendor have different tax-related accounting codes. That is a reason to investigate consistency—not proof that a deduction, reporting treatment, or return is wrong.

Illustrative example: A medical practice purchases equipment and a support agreement from the same supplier. The system flags different ledger classifications because the vendor name is identical. The invoices, however, describe different purchases.

Review component What to capture
Source evidence Relevant invoices, ledger entries, entity, transaction dates, and existing CPA-approved coding guidance
Model rationale Vendor-name similarity and differing classifications triggered the flag
Uncertainty The model may not have read the invoice detail or distinguished equipment from services
Reviewer action Compare the underlying purchases and ask the CPA whether any classification or tax treatment needs correction
Possible conclusion Different coding is supported, coding needs correction, or evidence remains insufficient

A multi-entity owner should also confirm which entity incurred the expense. Similar vendor charges in separate entity accounts do not automatically require identical treatment.

The bookkeeper can gather documents and identify inconsistencies. The CPA should evaluate tax implications. Do not let an AI explanation convert “different from prior transactions” into “not deductible” or “must be amended.” Those conclusions require facts and professional analysis beyond a pattern match.

Use Case: Missing Payroll Support

A payroll flag should identify the missing evidence precisely. “Payroll documentation incomplete” is too vague to resolve efficiently.

Illustrative example: The system finds an adjustment in administrative payroll but no supporting authorization in the files it received. The practice manager later locates the approval in a separate payroll folder.

The initial supported observation is: “No authorization was found in the supplied records.” It is not: “No authorization exists.”

A reviewer should establish:

  • Which employer entity, payroll period, and payroll entry are involved.
  • Which records the system searched and which were unavailable.
  • Whether the concern involves authorization, wage calculations, tax deposits, or another distinct issue.
  • Whether the payroll register reconciles to the relevant ledger and payment records.
  • Who can supply or evaluate the missing support.

The IRS employment-tax recordkeeping guidance identifies supporting records such as wage details, withholding certificates, deposit confirmations, and filed returns. The Department of Labor’s FLSA recordkeeping guidance addresses payroll records and records underlying wage calculations.

These sources cover different obligations. Ask the appropriate professional to confirm which requirements and retention periods apply rather than adopting a single AI-generated retention schedule.

Closing the document request also does not necessarily resolve the underlying payroll question. An approval may exist while the wage calculation still needs review. Record those conclusions separately, and escalate employment-law questions to qualified counsel when needed.

Use Case: An Unusual Approval Exception

A payment can fall outside the practice’s normal approval process without being unlawful or improper. The explanation must distinguish an internal-policy exception from a potential legal or tax issue.

Illustrative example: A manager approves an urgent equipment repair while the usual approver is unavailable. The AI flags the payment because the standard approval evidence is absent from the supplied records.

The reviewer should inspect the policy version in effect, the invoice, the documented authorization, and any permitted exception process. In a multi-location practice, the reviewer should also confirm whether the manager’s authority covered that location and paying entity.

Weak explanation Reviewable explanation
“This payment is noncompliant.” “The supplied payment record does not include the approval specified by the practice’s policy.”
“The manager was unauthorized.” “The records supplied do not establish the manager’s authority for this entity.”
“Reverse the transaction.” “Confirm the applicable approval policy and exception documentation before deciding on corrective action.”

Possible outcomes include confirming a permitted exception, documenting an internal control gap, correcting an entity allocation, or escalating an unresolved question. None should be inferred solely from the model’s warning.

An Explanation Record Template

Use a consistent record for each material flag. This can be maintained in an appropriate document or work-management system; it does not require a specialized AI feature.

Field What to record
Flag identifier and scope A reference number, affected entity or location, period, and transaction references
Original output The original flag and explanation, with the generation date and tool/version if available
Inputs reviewed Files, reports, date ranges, filters, and document versions supplied to the system
Source evidence Specific ledger references, document locations, and relevant excerpts without unnecessary sensitive information
Comparison basis The policy, accounting guidance, or other expectation used, including its source and applicable version
Stated rationale The observable mismatch and the inference drawn from it; distinguish a documented trigger from a generated narrative
Uncertainty and alternatives Missing inputs, assumptions, conflicting evidence, and plausible non-error explanations
Requested decision The specific question the reviewer needs to answer
Reviewer action Documents inspected, checks performed, people consulted, and corrections requested
Reviewer conclusion Confirmed issue, supported exception, unsupported flag, or insufficient evidence—with reasons
Follow-up ownership Responsible person, agreed follow-up date, and unresolved questions
Resolution reference Links to approved corrections, supporting documents, or the final decision record

Do not silently overwrite the initial output when new evidence changes the result. Keep the original flag distinguishable from the reviewer’s later findings. This is a recommended documentation practice, not a claim that a system creates an immutable or automatically compliant audit trail.

A Practical Review Workflow

Gather the minimum useful evidence

Start with the narrow transaction or period in question. Check entity, location, date range, and export completeness before interpreting the model’s conclusion. A flag based on the wrong entity or an incomplete report may be misleading even if its explanation is coherent.

Avoid including patient-identifiable information when ledger references, vendor records, or appropriately limited summaries will answer the question. Financial work can involve protected health information. HHS explains that accounting services can create business-associate responsibilities when PHI is involved, and its minimum necessary guidance describes a standard with specific exceptions.

Use practice-approved workflows. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool to clarify a flag.

Test the explanation against the records

Open the cited documents. Confirm that they say what the output claims. Reperform the relevant comparison or reconciliation where practical, and check whether the stated policy actually applies.

Ask what evidence could disprove the flag. A missing attachment, a valid policy exception, or a different invoice purpose may change the conclusion. Treat a confidence score as model output—not as a probability of legal compliance unless that interpretation has been specifically validated.

Route the decision to the right reviewer

A practice manager may locate an approval. A bookkeeper may reconcile an entry. A CPA may evaluate accounting or tax treatment. Legal questions require appropriately qualified counsel.

Record whether the issue is ready for a decision, needs more evidence, or requires escalation. Where consequences are significant, agree with the relevant professional whether a proposed change should wait for review. Do not post a tax-sensitive adjustment simply because the explanation sounds persuasive.

Close only the question that was resolved

“Approval located” resolves a missing-document question. It does not necessarily establish that the approver had authority. Similarly, a corrected ledger code does not by itself establish that a filed return needs changing—or that it does not.

Keep remaining questions open with a named owner and a follow-up date.

The Owner’s Decision Standard

A flag is sufficiently understandable for professional review when the reviewer can locate the evidence, identify the comparison, challenge the assumptions, and see the exact decision requested. It is sufficiently supported for action only after the appropriate reviewer has evaluated the relevant facts.

FAQs

Does an explainable AI flag prove a medical practice is out of compliance?

No. A clear explanation can still rely on incorrect data, an inapplicable policy, or missing context. It helps a reviewer investigate; it does not establish a violation or guarantee compliance. Qualified professionals must evaluate legal and tax implications.

What should we do if the AI cannot identify its source evidence?

Treat the output as an unverified lead, not a finding. Request the relevant transaction references and input scope, or reproduce the comparison manually. If the evidence cannot be located, document that limitation and do not use the explanation alone to support a material correction.

Can the practice manager close a flag without involving the CPA?

A manager may resolve a straightforward document request or confirm an internal approval within their authority. Tax treatment, accounting judgments, payroll-law questions, and other professional issues should go to the appropriately qualified reviewer. Closing one document gap should not imply that every related compliance question has been resolved.
