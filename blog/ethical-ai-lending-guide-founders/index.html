Ethical AI Lending Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

When a lender uses AI to evaluate your medical practice, the most useful question is not whether its technology is sophisticated. It is whether you can understand what information informed the decision, correct inaccurate records and reach someone accountable for reconsideration.

For a staffed private practice, financing may support provider recruitment, equipment purchases, a new location or working capital while payer receipts arrive. An automated assessment can miss important context if it reads the wrong entity’s accounts, treats transfers as revenue or evaluates a partial reporting period without explanation.

This guide covers borrowing for the practice—not patient financing or patient credit scoring. It provides questions to ask before sharing information and a workflow for reconciling an unfavorable financing outcome with your submitted records. It is not a guide to building credit models, comparing loan prices or using AI to summarize loan documents.

What ethical AI lending means from the borrower’s side

A practice owner usually cannot inspect a lender’s entire model or independently validate its fairness. You can, however, evaluate how the lender handles data, communicates decisions and responds to errors.

Five borrower-facing principles make that evaluation practical:

  • Relevant data: The lender can identify the information it uses and explain its relationship to the application.
  • Meaningful consent: You understand what access you are authorizing and how information may be shared or reused.
  • Useful explanations: The lender connects its decision to understandable factors rather than simply referring to an algorithm.
  • Correction and reconsideration: There is a documented route for correcting records and requesting human review.
  • Clear accountability: You know which organization makes the decision and who handles questions or disputes.

These are evaluation criteria, not proof of legal compliance. A clear explanation can still describe a decision based on inaccurate information or inappropriate assumptions. Likewise, a human review process is not automatically fair simply because a person participates.

Questions about applicable borrower rights, notices, discrimination or dispute procedures should go to licensed counsel familiar with the financing arrangement and jurisdiction.

First establish where AI enters the financing process

“AI-powered financing” can describe very different activities. Ask the lender or platform to identify what the technology actually does.

Use of technology What to ask Why the distinction matters
Document extraction or natural-language analysis Does the tool only read documents, or do its extracted figures feed the credit decision? A transcription or classification error may affect underwriting even if the tool does not approve loans.
Application screening Can an automated screen stop an application before an underwriter sees it? You need a route to address missing information or an incorrect initial classification.
Credit assessment Does the model influence approval, credit limits, required guarantees or other conditions? Decision factors and reconsideration procedures become especially important.
Lender matching Which lenders receive the application, and who makes the final decision? A platform’s recommendation is not necessarily a lender’s credit decision.

Document analysis is not the same as ethical underwriting. Accurate extraction does not establish that the downstream decision is fair. Similarly, comparing interest rates and fees does not answer questions about consent, data correction or accountability.

A lender-question checklist before you apply

Ask for written answers where possible. Keep them with the application materials so the practice manager and owner can refer to the same record.

Topic Questions for the lender or platform What to clarify before proceeding
Data sources Which submitted records, connected accounts and third-party sources will you use? Do you use nonfinancial information such as device or location data? The sources, reporting dates and business purpose of the information requested.
Entity and account scope How do you distinguish the borrowing entity from related practices, management entities and owner accounts? Which accounts and obligations belong in this application.
Consent What access am I authorizing, for how long, and for which purposes? Whether authorization is one-time or continuing and how future access can be stopped.
Sharing and reuse Who receives the information? Is it reused for purposes beyond this application, including model development? Which organizations receive data and which choices are available to the borrower.
Decision role Does AI recommend an outcome or determine it? Who is the actual creditor? Responsibility should not disappear between the platform, vendor and lender.
Explanation Can you identify the principal factors behind an unfavorable outcome and the records or periods associated with them? Whether the explanation will be specific enough to investigate.
Correction How do I challenge a wrong balance, duplicate liability or misclassified deposit? Submission channel, supporting documents and a named contact or case reference.
Human reconsideration Who can review corrected information, and can that person change the outcome? A review process rather than a restatement of the original automated result.
Fairness oversight How do you evaluate inappropriate proxy inputs or uneven outcomes, and who is accountable for that work? The lender’s governance approach, without treating a general assurance as proof.
Closure and follow-up How will you communicate the final result and handle access after the application ends? Written closure, unresolved issues and available access-management options.

A lender may not disclose proprietary model details. That does not prevent you from asking which information affected your application or how to correct an error. If answers remain vague, consider whether you are comfortable granting access or proceeding with that provider.

Prepare records that represent the practice accurately

Borrower accountability starts with a coherent application. It does not mean accepting responsibility for the lender’s model. It means supplying accurate records and explaining material distinctions before they become underwriting misunderstandings.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Those practices also give you a stronger basis for checking what a lender received.

Match the borrower to its financial records

Identify the legal entity applying for financing, the accounts it owns and any related entities included in the package. For a multi-location group, distinguish a location-level P&L from an entity-level statement. A location report may omit central administrative payroll, shared rent or other allocated costs.

Do not combine unrelated accounts merely to produce a larger revenue total. If consolidated information is requested, provide a clear reconciliation showing what is included and how intercompany activity is treated.

Explain timing and classification differences

Payer receipts, patient-balance collections and accounting revenue may differ because they measure different activity or periods. Transfers between practice accounts are not new patient-service revenue. Loan proceeds and owner contributions should also remain distinguishable from operating receipts.

Provider and administrative payroll can move between periods because of payroll timing. Shared costs can also change a location’s apparent profitability when allocation methods differ. Explain the accounting basis and material adjustments consistently rather than presenting whichever view looks strongest.

Minimize unnecessary patient information

A practice financing package should ordinarily focus on business-level financial information. Start with aggregate reports rather than patient-level detail. If a lender requests identifiable patient information, pause and involve appropriate privacy and legal advisers before sharing it.

HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard describes requirements and exceptions that depend on the circumstances. It is not a blanket authorization to disclose information to a lender. Do not put patient-identifiable records into ordinary Slack conversations or general-purpose AI tools to explain the application.

How to reconcile an unfavorable financing outcome

A denial, reduced offer or unexpected condition is a reason to investigate—not evidence by itself that the model was unfair. Use a structured process to separate record errors, interpretation differences and unresolved decision concerns.

1. Preserve the application as submitted

Save the submitted financial statements, debt schedule, account list, reporting dates, authorizations and correspondence. Record the application reference and submission date. Keep later corrections separate so you can distinguish what the lender originally received from what you subsequently provided.

For connected accounts, document which accounts and periods were authorized. Ask the lender to confirm what it actually retrieved and whether any connection failed or returned an incomplete period.

2. Obtain the explanation and identify the decision-maker

Request the reasons for the outcome and ask which source records and reporting periods relate to those reasons. Preserve any notice supplied by the lender.

If the application went through a platform, establish whether the platform screened it out or a named lender made the credit decision. Direct correction requests to the party able to act on them.

Do not assume a generic phrase such as “insufficient cash flow” tells you which figure was used. Ask whether the concern involved operating receipts, existing debt payments, payroll, cash balances or another factor.

3. Build a reconciliation worksheet

Compare each stated concern with the submitted records. Keep factual corrections separate from requests to interpret the same facts differently.

Stated concern Practice records to check Potential discrepancy to investigate
Revenue appears inconsistent Bank reconciliations, monthly P&L and aggregate collections reports Missing account, incomplete period or confusion between accounting revenue and receipts.
Debt payments appear high Debt schedule, lender statements and general ledger Duplicate obligation or debt assigned to the wrong entity.
Location profitability appears weak Location P&L and shared-cost allocation schedule Inconsistent allocation method or mismatched reporting periods.
Cash appears insufficient Dated bank statements and cash reconciliation Incorrect balance date or an account omitted from the review.
Payroll appears volatile Payroll summaries and accounting entries Payroll timing, duplicate entries or costs belonging to another entity.

Illustrative example: A two-location practice submits records showing $240,000 in monthly bank deposits. Of that amount, $30,000 consists of transfers between its own accounts and $20,000 is loan proceeds. Operating receipts are therefore $190,000 for that period. The practice should identify those categories explicitly—not argue that every deposit represents revenue. If the lender also missed an operating account, reconcile that separately with supporting statements.

4. Submit a focused correction package

Provide a short cover note, a numbered list of discrepancies and the documents supporting each correction. Reference the original file, relevant page or reporting period so the reviewer can follow the reconciliation.

Ask the lender to confirm receipt and explain whether corrected records will replace the earlier figures, trigger a new assessment or receive manual review. Request an expected response date without assuming that a particular deadline applies.

5. Request human reconsideration and document the result

Ask the reviewer to address the corrected facts and explain any remaining concerns. A useful review distinguishes “the figures were corrected, but the application still does not meet our criteria” from “the original figures were used again.”

Keep the response and note unresolved discrepancies. If the explanation conflicts with the records, responsibility remains unclear or you suspect unlawful treatment, consult licensed counsel about rights and next steps. Do not assume resubmitting an application preserves any dispute rights or deadlines.

Decide whether to proceed

Ethical evaluation and credit approval are separate questions. A lender can have a transparent process and still decline the application. An attractive offer can also come with data-use terms or accountability gaps the owner is unwilling to accept.

Before proceeding, confirm that you understand the requested access, the party making the decision and the correction route. Then evaluate affordability and financing terms separately with your advisers.

FAQs

Does an explanation prove that an AI lending decision is fair?

No. An explanation may help identify the factors behind a result, but it does not independently establish that the data was accurate, the factors were appropriate or the process complied with applicable law. Treat it as a starting point for reconciliation. Bring legal fairness or rights questions to licensed counsel.

What if the lender will not disclose its algorithm?

You can still ask which records informed your application, what factors drove the outcome and how to correct inaccurate information. You do not need source code to identify a duplicate liability or an omitted bank account. If the lender cannot offer a usable explanation or correction route, consider that limitation when deciding whether to proceed.

Can human review guarantee a different outcome?

No. Human reconsideration can provide a route for reviewing corrections and context, but it does not guarantee approval. Ask whether the reviewer can change the decision and request a response that addresses the specific discrepancies you submitted.

What records should the practice keep?

Keep the submitted application package, authorizations, account scope, explanations, notices, correction requests and final responses together. Ask your CPA and counsel to establish an appropriate retention approach for the records involved; there is no single retention period supplied by this guide.
