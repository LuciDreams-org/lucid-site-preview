7 Investment Treaty Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

If you own a staffed U.S. private medical practice and are considering an ownership investment overseas, investment treaties may belong on your legal due-diligence list. They should not be treated as insurance, permission to own a foreign medical business, or a substitute for a sound purchase agreement.

The practical decision is whether to commit capital—and through which owner or entity—after understanding the investment’s legal and financial risks. A treaty may not apply to your proposed transaction at all. Even when investment protections exist, access to a dispute process is a separate question.

Use the following seven questions to prepare a briefing for licensed cross-border counsel. Your finance team’s role is to make the proposed ownership, funding and business economics clear. Counsel must assess treaty eligibility, local ownership restrictions and available remedies.

First, Separate Three Different Legal Instruments

An overseas ownership purchase can involve several legal instruments with different purposes. Do not assume that one answers questions governed by another.

Instrument Question it addresses What not to assume
Investment treaty Whether qualifying investors and investments have specified protections concerning host-government conduct That every foreign owner qualifies or can bring an arbitration claim
Income-tax treaty How specified cross-border tax matters are treated between the relevant countries That tax-treaty treatment establishes investment-treaty coverage
Purchase agreement, shareholder agreement or other contract What the contracting parties promise, including payment, governance and contractual remedies That a private contractual dispute is also a treaty violation

A payment to an overseas equipment supplier is not the same transaction as acquiring an ownership interest in an overseas business. This article concerns genuine ownership investments, not routine vendor payments or cross-border tax reporting.

1. Who Would Legally Be the Investor?

Start with the proposed legal owner, not the name used on your U.S. practice’s website. Would you invest personally, through an existing practice entity, or through a separate holding company? Who would own and control that entity?

Ask counsel which facts matter under any potentially relevant treaty: individual nationality, place of incorporation, ownership, control, actual business activity and the dates on which those facts must be established. Do not assume that U.S. residence, a U.S. bank account or a U.S. practice address resolves investor identity.

Hypothetical ownership-chain diagram

This illustrative structure is a discussion aid, not a recommendation or a statement that any ownership arrangement is permitted:

U.S. practice owners
↓ Proposed ownership interest
Proposed U.S. holding company
↓ Proposed equity investment
Overseas operating company in Country A
↓ Business operations and assets
Proposed outpatient business in Country A

The owners’ existing U.S. medical practice would sit outside this hypothetical chain. If it would instead supply funds, guarantee debt or provide management services, show those relationships separately.

Have the finance team annotate each connection as equity, debt, guarantee or service arrangement. Counsel should evaluate the actual structure—not a simplified chart that hides intermediate owners or obligations. Do not create an entity solely on the assumption that incorporation will secure treaty protection.

2. Where Would the Investment Actually Be Located?

A foreign mailing address does not fully describe an investment. Identify the country of incorporation, operating locations, asset locations and the government bodies responsible for relevant approvals.

For a proposed interest in an overseas outpatient business, the operating company, leased premises, equipment and contractual rights may have different legal characteristics. Ask counsel how the applicable instrument defines territory and which proposed assets fall within it.

Prepare a location-and-asset schedule:

Proposed item Facts to provide Question for counsel
Shares in the overseas company Issuing entity, jurisdiction and acquisition terms Where is this ownership interest treated as an investment?
Premises and equipment Owner, physical location and lease or purchase documents Which rights belong to the investor versus the operating company?
Approvals and operating rights Issuing authority, named holder and conditions Are these rights relevant to coverage, and can the transaction proceed as proposed?
Funding commitments Funding entity, receiving entity and intended use How should each contribution be characterized?

Separately, obtain local advice on whether the ownership and operating model is lawful. A treaty analysis does not establish permission to own or operate a medical business.

3. Which Instruments Could Potentially Apply?

Ask counsel to identify possible instruments from the actual investor–host-country relationship. Starting with a list of “best treaties” reverses the analysis: a well-known agreement is irrelevant if its parties, dates or definitions do not fit.

Potential instruments may include a bilateral investment treaty or the investment provisions of a broader trade agreement. Counsel should verify the operative text, entry into force, amendments, reservations, termination provisions and any provisions addressing earlier investments.

Request a short written screening result for each candidate:

  • The instrument’s name and official text.
  • The proposed investor and host country being assessed.
  • The relevant investment and transaction dates.
  • Why the instrument might apply—or why it does not.
  • Facts or legal questions still requiring verification.

Do not confuse an arbitration framework with substantive investment rights. ICSID’s explanation of its jurisdiction, for example, emphasizes the parties’ consent to arbitration. Membership alone does not supply consent for a particular dispute or create investment protections.

The useful result may be “no applicable investment treaty identified.” That finding belongs in the investment decision rather than being treated as an administrative gap.

4. Do the Investor and Assets Meet the Coverage Definitions?

A potentially relevant treaty is only the beginning. Ask counsel to test the proposed investor and each material asset against its definitions and conditions.

An ownership interest, shareholder loan, equipment purchase and contractual payment right should not be grouped together as automatically protected “capital.” Their treatment can differ. Provide the underlying documents and ask for an asset-by-asset assessment where necessary.

Also ask what conduct the relevant provisions address. Investment treaties concern specified obligations involving states; they are not general guarantees against disappointing returns, declining patient demand or an overseas partner’s failure to pay.

For practice-finance purposes, separate the investment case into:

  • Commercial risk: operating performance, staffing costs, collections, competition and management execution.
  • Contractual risk: breaches of purchase, shareholder, financing or service agreements.
  • Potential government-related risk: a specific measure counsel would need to assess under applicable law and any relevant treaty.

This separation prevents a financial forecast from treating uncertain legal recourse as protection against ordinary business losses.

5. What Exclusions or Reservations Could Change the Answer?

Ask counsel to identify the limits that matter to this transaction, not merely summarize the treaty’s broad protections.

Depending on the instrument, issues requiring review may include sector reservations, public-health provisions, taxation exclusions, security exceptions, legality requirements or denial-of-benefits provisions. Their wording and effect must be evaluated in context.

For an overseas medical-business investment, request separate answers to three questions:

  1. Is the proposed ownership and operating arrangement permitted under applicable local law?
  2. Does a potentially relevant treaty cover the investor and investment?
  3. Could a reservation, exclusion or exception affect the particular protection being considered?

Keep unresolved points visible in the approval memo. For example, label an ownership issue “Pending local counsel verification” and identify who must resolve it before funds are committed.

A forecast should not assume that regulation will remain unchanged or that an adverse regulatory decision necessarily creates a compensable treaty claim. Model the business’s exposure independently of any possible remedy.

6. What Dispute Mechanism, If Any, Would Be Available?

Protection and enforcement require separate analysis. Ask counsel whether the relevant instrument permits this investor to bring this type of claim, against which respondent, and in which forum.

Counsel should identify applicable consent requirements, notice procedures, waiting periods, limitation periods, domestic-proceeding requirements, waivers and restrictions on parallel proceedings. Do not use deadlines borrowed from another treaty.

If a dispute already exists or is becoming foreseeable, seek advice before restructuring ownership or choosing a court or arbitration route. Those actions may affect later options.

Your financial review should also address practical affordability:

Financial issue Information to request or prepare
Advisory and dispute costs Matter-specific estimates from counsel, including assumptions and stages
Management burden Expected demands on owners, managers and finance staff
Operating cash exposure How the business would fund payroll, occupancy and other commitments during a dispute
Recovery uncertainty Counsel’s assessment of enforcement issues, separate from the merits

Do not count a possible award as available operating cash. Keep the investment’s base-case funding plan separate from contingent legal recovery.

7. What Records Would Support the Investment and Any Later Review?

Build the record before closing. Counsel needs evidence of who invested, what was acquired, when funds moved and which obligations or approvals applied. Your accounting team needs the same transaction to reconcile cleanly across entities.

Maintain a transaction file containing ownership records, signed agreements, approvals, funding evidence, entity-level ledgers and relevant government correspondence. Keep original budgets and later revisions distinguishable rather than overwriting the original investment case.

For a staffed practice owner, protecting the U.S. business also requires a clear funding record. Distinguish owner contributions, practice distributions, intercompany loans and guarantees. Do not let a foreign investment become an unexplained withdrawal from the account used for provider and administrative payroll.

IRS Publication 583 provides general guidance on business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. It does not establish treaty eligibility or replace counsel’s evidence requirements.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. An ownership or valuation review ordinarily needs business economics, not patient-identifiable records. If an adviser requests patient information, have the appropriate privacy and legal personnel evaluate the request and sharing arrangements. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; it is not a universal rule for every disclosure.

Adviser Briefing Checklist Before Committing Capital

Give licensed cross-border counsel, local counsel and your CPA a consistent factual packet. Assign an owner and status to each open issue.

Briefing item What to include Primary reviewer
Transaction summary Target business, commercial purpose, proposed interest and closing conditions Cross-border and local counsel
Ownership chain Existing and proposed entities, owners, control and jurisdictions Counsel
Funding plan Source of capital, equity versus debt, guarantees and remaining U.S. practice liquidity CPA and owners, with counsel reviewing documents
Investment economics Target financial statements, cash forecast, staffing obligations and downside assumptions Finance team
Shared arrangements Proposed management charges, shared staff or other intercompany costs CPA and counsel
Legal screening Candidate instruments, coverage questions, exclusions and unresolved approvals Counsel
Records and communications Agreements, approvals, bank evidence and correspondence index Finance team and counsel

Ask for a written conclusion distinguishing confirmed facts, legal conclusions and unresolved questions. Before approval, make sure the owner understands both the business’s downside funding needs and the limits of any potential treaty recourse.

FAQs

Does owning a U.S. medical practice make me eligible for investment treaty protection?

No automatic eligibility follows from owning a U.S. practice. Counsel must assess the proposed investor, host country, assets, dates and applicable instrument. The contemplated transaction may have no applicable investment treaty protection.

Is an investment treaty the same as an income-tax treaty?

No. They address different subjects. Do not use tax-treaty eligibility or treatment as proof of investment-treaty coverage. Coordinate the reviews, but request separate conclusions from appropriately qualified advisers.

Would a dispute with an overseas business partner qualify as a treaty claim?

Not merely because the investment is overseas. A partner dispute may be governed by contracts and local law. Counsel must separately assess whether relevant government conduct and an applicable treaty could support a claim.

Should I change the ownership structure to obtain treaty protection?

Do not restructure based on a generic treaty comparison. Obtain advice before acting, particularly if a dispute exists or is foreseeable. Counsel should evaluate eligibility and legal consequences, while your CPA assesses the financial and tax implications without assuming a favorable result.
