How are year‑end bonuses for practice staff taxed?

December 2, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 2, 2026

A staff bonus is taxed as wages. You withhold federal income tax at a flat 22%, or add the bonus to a regular paycheck and withhold on the total. You and the employee each pay 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare. State tax comes on top, and gift cards count as wages too.

Key points

  • If you pay a bonus on its own check, you can withhold federal income tax at a flat 22%.
  • A $5,000 bonus costs your practice $5,382.50 once you add your 7.65% share of Social Security and Medicare.
  • A gift card is taxable wages at any amount; a small non‑cash holiday gift usually isn't.

What do the terms on a bonus paycheck mean?

Supplemental wages are pay on top of regular wages, such as a bonus, commission or award. The flat rate method is withholding federal income tax from supplemental wages at a single 22% rate. The aggregate method is adding a bonus to a regular paycheck and withholding as if the total were one normal paycheck. FICA is the Social Security and Medicare tax you and each employee pay on wages. A gross‑up is a larger bonus you pay so the employee takes home a set amount after tax. A de minimis benefit is a gift so small and rare that the IRS doesn't count it as wages.

How do you withhold federal tax on a staff bonus?

You have two choices. If you pay the bonus separately from regular wages, you can withhold a flat 22% of it. Or you can add it to a regular paycheck and withhold on the total. Either way, you also withhold Social Security and Medicare and pay your matching share.

You can use the flat 22% only if you withheld income tax from that employee's regular wages this year or last year. Once a person's supplemental wages from you pass $1 million in a year, you withhold 37% on the amount over $1 million, whatever their Form W‑4 says.

Congress kept these rates in the 2025 tax law, so they apply to 2026 bonuses.

When staff say a bonus was "taxed at 40%," they're adding 22% federal withholding, 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare, and state tax.

How is each kind of bonus taxed?

Cash bonuses and gift cards are wages at any amount, so you run them through payroll and report them on the employee's W‑2. A small non‑cash holiday gift, such as a ham or a fruit basket, usually isn't wages. If you cover the employee's tax, the tax you cover is wages too.

Bonus type Is it taxable wages? How you report it
Cash, check or direct deposit Yes: income tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare and federal unemployment tax On the W‑2, with regular pay
Gift card or gift certificate Yes, at any amount On the W‑2; run its value through payroll
Small non‑cash holiday gift Usually not, as a de minimis benefit Not on the W‑2
Gross‑up (you cover the employee's tax) Yes, including the tax you cover On the W‑2, at the grossed‑up amount

The IRS says cash equivalents such as gift cards, "no matter how little, are never excludable as a de minimis benefit" (IRS Publication 15‑B).

How much does a $5,000 bonus cost you, and what does the employee keep?

On a $5,000 bonus, your medical assistant keeps about $3,500 before state tax, and your practice pays about $5,380 in total. You'll withhold a little more or less by method, but she'll owe the same tax and you'll pay the same cost.

Example 1: a $5,000 year‑end bonus to a medical assistant. She earns $1,800 every two weeks ($46,800 a year), files as single, and has a 2020 or later Form W‑4 with nothing else entered. You pay it in December 2026, on its own check or added to her regular paycheck.

Line Flat 22% Aggregate method
Bonus $5,000.00 $5,000.00
Federal income tax withheld $1,100.00 $1,066.54
Her Social Security, 6.2% $310.00 $310.00
Her Medicare, 1.45% $72.50 $72.50
She takes home, before state tax $3,517.50 $3,550.96
Your Social Security and Medicare, 7.65% $382.50 $382.50
Your total cost $5,382.50 $5,382.50

For the aggregate column, you figure withholding on a $6,800 paycheck ($1,198.69) and subtract the $132.15 you'd withhold on her regular check.

Her actual federal tax on the bonus is about $600. With $51,800 in 2026 wages and the $16,100 standard deduction, she's in the 12% bracket, so she'd get about $500 back when she files. By December you've likely paid federal unemployment tax on her first $7,000, the yearly limit, so you won't owe it on the bonus.

Example 2: a gross‑up so the employee takes home $500. You want a front desk employee to take home exactly $500, paid on its own check at the flat 22%, in a state with no income tax. You divide $500 by what's left after 22% and 7.65%: $500 ÷ 0.7035 is $710.73, and you round up a cent so they take home the full $500.

Line Amount
Grossed‑up bonus $710.74
Federal income tax withheld, 22% $156.36
Their Social Security, 6.2% $44.07
Their Medicare, 1.45% $10.31
They take home $500.00
Your Social Security and Medicare, 7.65% $54.37
Your total cost $765.11

With a state income tax, add its rate to the 29.65% first.

Can you deduct a bonus you pay in January on this year's return?

It depends on your accounting method. With the cash method, you deduct a bonus in the year you pay it, so a bonus you pay in January 2027 goes on your 2027 return. With the accrual method, you can often deduct it for 2026 if you pay it by March 15, 2027.

For the accrual rule, you must fix the bonus and its amount by December 31, for example in a written plan. You can't use it for a bonus to yourself or another S corp owner; you deduct that only in the year the owner is paid.

For other staff costs you can deduct, see tax deductions for medical practice owners.

Which December and January dates matter for bonuses?

Pay a bonus by Thursday, December 31, 2026 if you want it on 2026 W‑2s and, with the cash method, on your 2026 return. You'll send W‑2s by Monday, February 1, 2027. Banks close on Friday, December 25 and Friday, January 1, so approve bonuses before your payroll provider's last December cutoff.

Date What happens Who it applies to

Fri, Jan 1, 2027 Bank holiday; banks don't send direct deposits Every practice with payroll

Should you take a year‑end bonus from your own S corp?

Sometimes. If your practice is taxed as an S corp and you work in it, a bonus through payroll is wages, so you and the practice pay Social Security and Medicare on it. You don't on a distribution. Owners still choose a bonus to reach a reasonable salary, raise retirement plan contributions, or cover an estimated tax shortfall through withholding.

Example 3: an owner's $40,000 bonus. You're the only owner of a practice taxed as an S corp, with a $150,000 salary through payroll in 2026. You're choosing between a $40,000 bonus on the last December payroll and a $40,000 distribution.

Line Bonus through payroll Distribution
Social Security, 6.2% each side, on $34,500 $4,278 $0
Medicare, 1.45% each side, on $40,000 $1,160 $0
Total Social Security and Medicare $5,438 $0
Federal income tax withheld now, 22% $8,800 $0

You owe income tax on the $40,000 either way. With a bonus, the practice withholds it now; with a distribution, you pay it on your own return, because S corp profit is taxed to you whether or not you take it out.

You stop paying Social Security once your 2026 wages reach $184,500, so you pay it on only $34,500 of the bonus. You'd pay the extra 0.9% Medicare tax only on wages over $200,000. The IRS generally treats income tax withheld from wages as paid evenly through the year, so you can use the $8,800 to make up for a short estimated payment earlier in 2026. You can't do that with a distribution.

What should med spa and dental practice owners watch for?

Med spa and dental practices often tie bonuses to sales or production. For employees, those are wages. You don't withhold tax from a bonus you pay a contractor, such as a contract injector or hygienist; you add it to their Form 1099‑NEC total.

  • Med spa owners: run per‑treatment and retail bonuses for employed injectors through payroll. See bookkeeping for med spas.
  • Dental practice owners: if staff bonuses are a share of collections, put the formula in writing before December 31. See bookkeeping for dental practices.

To set amounts by role, start from your staff base pay bands.

Questions practice owners ask about bonus taxes

This guide is general information, not tax advice for your practice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and your numbers. Talk to a CPA before you act on it.

Why are bonuses withheld at 22%?

The IRS lets employers withhold a flat 22% of federal income tax from bonuses and other supplemental wages paid separately from regular pay. It's a withholding shortcut, not a tax rate. Your employee's real rate depends on their total income, so they'll get the difference back, or owe it, when they file.

How much of a $1,000 bonus will my employee take home?

On its own check at the flat 22%, your employee keeps $703.50 of a $1,000 bonus before state tax: $220 goes to federal income tax, $62 to Social Security and $14.50 to Medicare. Your practice adds $76.50 for its share, so the bonus costs you $1,076.50.

Are gift cards to employees taxable?

Yes. A gift card is taxable wages at any amount, even $25, because the IRS treats it like cash. Run it through payroll in the pay period you hand it out and include it on the W‑2. A turkey or a small holiday gift that isn't cash or a card usually isn't taxable.

Can I gross up a bonus so staff get the full amount?

Yes. You pay a larger bonus so the employee nets the amount you chose. The tax you cover is wages too, so you divide the take‑home amount by what's left after all the withholding rates, not just add the tax back once.

Can my practice deduct a bonus paid after December 31?

Only if your practice uses the accrual method, fixed the bonus before year end and pays it by March 15. With the cash method, you deduct it in the year you pay it. An S corp can't deduct an owner's accrued bonus before the owner is paid. Your accounting method is on your last business tax return.

Should owners take a year‑end bonus too?

If your practice is taxed as an S corp and your salary is low for your work, a payroll bonus can bring it to a reasonable level, though you'll pay Social Security and Medicare on it. If the IRS taxes your practice as a sole proprietorship or partnership, you can't pay yourself a W‑2 bonus; you take a draw instead.
