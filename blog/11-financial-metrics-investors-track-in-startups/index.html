11 Financial Metrics for Medical Practice Capital Discussions

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A lender or prospective partner needs more than a growing revenue total. They need to understand what the practice earns, when collections arrive, which costs move with activity and whether cash can support existing obligations plus the proposed investment.

For a staffed private medical practice, the goal is a defensible financial snapshot—not a dashboard crowded with ratios. These 11 measures connect operating performance to a specific capital decision, such as financing equipment, opening another location or bringing in a partner.

Use them together, with documented definitions and reconciled records. No single result establishes borrowing capacity, investment suitability or practice value.

Establish the reporting perimeter first

Before calculating anything, put these details on the scorecard cover sheet:

  • Decision: State the amount and intended use of capital, anticipated timing and expected operating changes.
  • Scope: Identify the entities and locations included. Distinguish the operating practice from any related property or management entity.
  • Period: Use a common reporting cutoff, trailing-period results and a comparable prior period. Add monthly detail where seasonality matters.
  • Accounting basis: Identify cash, accrual or another basis, and disclose adjustments. Do not compare cash-basis revenue with accrual-basis expenses without explaining the mismatch.
  • Reconciliation status: Note unresolved bank, payroll, receivables or intercompany differences.

For multiple locations, show both consolidated results and location P&Ls. Document how shared administrative payroll, rent and other overhead are allocated. Eliminate intercompany activity in consolidated reporting rather than counting internal transfers as outside revenue.

The IRS business-recordkeeping guidance supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. These are useful foundations for a capital package, not substitutes for explaining the numbers.

Metric dictionary

The definitions below are working conventions. Agree on any lender-specific definition before presenting a ratio as satisfying a financing requirement.

Metric Calculation or reporting definition
1. Net revenue Recognized patient-service revenue after contractual adjustments, discounts, refunds and applicable revenue reductions under the stated accounting policy; separately identify other operating revenue
2. Revenue growth (Current-period net revenue − comparable prior-period net revenue) ÷ prior-period net revenue × 100
3. Collection lag For a defined service cohort, sum of each collected dollar amount × days from service to receipt, divided by total dollars collected for that cohort
4. Accounts-receivable aging Outstanding A/R grouped by a stated aging date convention; bucket share = bucket balance ÷ total included A/R × 100
5. Payer concentration Net revenue attributable to a payer or payer group ÷ total net revenue on the same basis × 100
6. Contribution margin Net revenue − defined variable costs; contribution margin percentage = contribution ÷ net revenue × 100
7. Operating margin Operating income ÷ net revenue × 100
8. Staff cost share Defined provider and administrative staff costs ÷ net revenue × 100
9. Operating cash flow Cash received from operations − cash paid for operating activities
10. Debt-service coverage Agreed cash available for debt service ÷ required principal and interest for the same period
11. Unrestricted cash runway Unrestricted cash ÷ projected average monthly net cash deficit, when that deficit is positive

1. Net revenue: establish the economic starting point

Required records: General ledger, billing summaries, contractual adjustments, refunds, remittance summaries and bank reconciliations.

On an accrual basis, distinguish gross charges from expected realizable revenue. On a cash basis, identify patient and payer receipts net of refunds; do not describe those receipts as revenue earned during the same service period. Exclude loan proceeds and owner contributions from revenue.

Decision relevance: Net revenue anchors margin calculations and the scale of operations supporting the capital request.

Interpretation limits: Adjustment policies and changes in revenue estimates can alter results. Reconcile billing-system totals to the ledger and explain differences rather than assuming deposits equal earned revenue.

2. Revenue growth: separate expansion from timing

Required records: Comparable net-revenue reports, monthly P&Ls and explanations of provider, location or accounting changes.

Compare equal-length periods using the same basis and scope. Show same-location results separately when acquisitions or new offices materially change the practice.

Decision relevance: Growth helps assess whether additional space, staffing or equipment is supported by existing activity or depends on future assumptions.

Interpretation limits: A payment backlog clearing can lift cash-basis growth without increasing current services. Growth also says nothing by itself about profitability. A zero prior-period denominator makes percentage growth undefined; a negative or unusually small denominator can make it misleading. Show dollar changes instead.

3. Collection lag: show how long revenue takes to become cash

Required records: Service dates, payment dates, payment amounts and links between collections and service cohorts, preferably in an aggregated or appropriately de-identified workpaper.

Use the dollar-weighted formula in the dictionary. Specify the service cohort and report cutoff. Separate payer and patient collections when their timing differs materially.

Decision relevance: Collection timing informs the working capital needed between payroll payments and payer receipts.

Interpretation limits: This calculation measures collected dollars only. Unpaid balances are absent, so recent cohorts can appear artificially fast. Pair lag with A/R aging and disclose cohort maturity. A simple A/R-to-revenue-days ratio is a different measure, not a substitute with the same meaning.

4. Accounts-receivable aging: assess what remains uncollected

Required records: A/R aging, credit-balance report, adjustments, write-offs and reconciliation to the general ledger where receivables are recorded.

Use consistent buckets, such as current, 31–60, 61–90 and over 90 days. State whether aging begins at service, claim submission or another date. Show payer and patient balances separately where useful.

Decision relevance: Aging helps a capital provider evaluate collection uncertainty and possible cash pressure.

Interpretation limits: An old balance is not automatically collectible—or automatically worthless. Gross-charge A/R may overstate expected receipts. Netting credit balances against debit balances can distort bucket percentages, so identify credits separately. Explain why a cash-basis ledger may not contain operational A/R.

5. Payer concentration: identify dependence on a few receipt sources

Required records: Payer-level revenue or collection reports, payer-group mapping and a reconciliation to the selected denominator.

Show the largest payer groups and distinguish patient responsibility from insurer-paid amounts without double counting. If only collections are available, label the result “share of collections,” not accrual revenue concentration.

Decision relevance: Concentration helps frame the cash effect of delayed receipts, contract changes or disruption involving a major payer.

Interpretation limits: A large share is exposure, not proof of a problem. Payer categories, service mix and collection timing affect the result. Use the same accounting basis and period in numerator and denominator.

6. Contribution margin: test the proposed use of capital

Required records: Net revenue, supply expenses, variable compensation agreements and a written cost-classification schedule.

Subtract costs that change with the relevant activity. A procedure-related supply expense may be variable; salaried administrative payroll may remain fixed within current capacity. Provider compensation may have both components.

Decision relevance: Contribution shows what additional activity could leave to cover fixed costs and financing obligations.

Interpretation limits: A positive contribution does not establish overall profitability. Added volume may trigger another staffing layer or additional space. Do not treat allocated fixed overhead as variable merely because it appears on a location P&L. Disclose the scope and denominator used for every percentage.

7. Operating margin: test profitability after operating costs

Required records: P&L, expense detail, depreciation schedule and a schedule of proposed adjustments.

Define operating income consistently, including the treatment of depreciation, owner compensation and related-party expenses. Keep financing costs and income taxes separate under the stated presentation.

Decision relevance: Operating margin shows what remains after operating costs, before the financing structure is considered.

Interpretation limits: An owner working in the practice may be paid differently from an employed replacement. Present actual results first and any normalized compensation adjustment separately. Margin is not cash flow, and a small or zero revenue denominator makes the percentage unstable or undefined.

8. Staff cost share: explain the payroll commitment

Required records: Payroll registers, employer payroll taxes, benefits, contract-labor records and provider compensation schedules.

Define whether staff cost includes employed providers, owners on payroll, temporary staff and contractors. Show provider and administrative components alongside the combined measure. Owner distributions are not automatically payroll expense.

Decision relevance: The ratio helps explain recurring cash commitments and whether planned expansion requires hiring before collections arrive.

Interpretation limits: Outsourcing or owner compensation choices can shift costs between categories without changing the underlying economics. Align payroll and revenue periods; cash-basis timing can distort a single month. A low ratio is not, by itself, evidence of adequate staffing or better performance.

9. Operating cash flow: distinguish earnings from liquidity

Required records: Reconciled cash accounts, cash-flow statement or cash-movement schedule, payroll disbursements and operating payment detail.

Separate operating receipts and payments from borrowing, owner funding, distributions, equipment purchases and debt principal. An accrual-to-cash reconciliation should explain working-capital changes and noncash expenses.

Decision relevance: Operating cash flow shows whether operations are generating cash before investing and financing decisions.

Interpretation limits: Delaying vendor payments can temporarily improve cash flow. A payer catch-up payment can do the same. Show monthly patterns and significant unpaid obligations. Unlike a margin, this measure has no revenue denominator; do not confuse it with the change in the bank balance.

10. Debt-service coverage: use the financing party’s definition

Required records: Debt schedules, principal and interest requirements, financial statements and a bridge to the proposed numerator.

Confirm the lender’s definition of cash available for debt service and its treatment of leases, owner compensation and other adjustments. Include existing and proposed obligations when evaluating the proposed financing.

Decision relevance: Coverage connects available resources to required payments over the same period.

Interpretation limits: Operating income, EBITDA and operating cash flow are not interchangeable numerators. Show every adjustment and avoid implying a universal passing ratio. Zero debt service makes the ratio inapplicable, not infinite borrowing capacity. Annual coverage can also conceal a seasonal cash shortfall.

11. Unrestricted cash runway: measure a defined downside

Required records: Reconciled cash balances, restrictions, near-term obligations and a forward cash forecast.

Exclude restricted or unavailable funds and undrawn credit from cash. Calculate runway against a documented projected monthly deficit, stating whether it includes debt service, capital spending and planned owner distributions.

Decision relevance: Runway helps assess how long available cash could absorb a delayed opening, slower collections or another defined shortfall.

Interpretation limits: If the practice is cash-generative, report runway as not applicable under the base case rather than “unlimited.” Use a downside scenario instead. Average deficits can hide an earlier cash shortfall, so review the forecast’s lowest cash point as well.

Turn the snapshot into a capital conversation

Package the scorecard with financial statements, reconciliation schedules, the debt schedule and a forward cash forecast showing the proposed transaction. Explain what changes after funding: staffing, occupancy, equipment payments and the timing of additional receipts.

Use aggregated financial data wherever practical. Avoid patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum-necessary guidance provides relevant privacy context, with exceptions and applicability that require appropriate review.

FAQs

Can a cash-basis practice prepare these measures?

Yes, but label cash-basis results accurately. Keep operational A/R and service-cohort reports separate from the cash-basis ledger, and explain timing differences. Additional accrual schedules may be needed for a particular financing discussion.

Should each location have its own scorecard?

Use location scorecards when capital decisions depend on local performance. Show shared-cost allocations and consolidated results alongside them. A location’s apparent margin can change materially with the allocation method, while consolidated profit remains unchanged.

What if a requested ratio cannot be supported?

Mark it unavailable, identify the missing records and provide a reconciliation plan. A clearly disclosed gap is more defensible than a precise-looking ratio built from incompatible periods, accounting bases or entity scopes.
