Planning Bridge Financing for a Medical Practice

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Bridge financing should connect a specific near-term use of cash to an identifiable longer-term funding event. For a staffed medical practice, that might mean paying expansion costs before a proposed long-term loan closes. The central decision is not simply how much to borrow. It is whether the practice can meet the bridge’s obligations if that closing is delayed, reduced, or canceled.

A useful proposal starts with the expected repayment event and works backward. It identifies which expenses must be paid first, which entity will incur them, what evidence supports the funding timeline, and what happens if the expected money never arrives.

Before signing, prepare three linked schedules: a funding timeline, a sources-and-uses schedule, and a downside repayment worksheet. Together, they should show whether the bridge is a manageable temporary obligation or a commitment the practice cannot safely unwind.

1. Define the funding event before choosing the bridge

Describe the expected permanent financing in concrete terms. “Refinance after opening” is not enough. Record the proposed lender, borrowing entity, intended loan purpose, current application status, remaining conditions, expected net proceeds, and anticipated availability date.

Distinguish between an initial conversation, an indicative proposal, a conditional approval, and executed documents. None should be treated as cash already available to repay the bridge. Even an advanced financing process can have unresolved conditions.

For an expansion, ask the proposed permanent lender:

  • Can its proceeds repay the proposed bridge and reimburse costs incurred before closing?
  • Which expenses are eligible, and what documentation is required?
  • Must construction, equipment installation, or other work be completed first?
  • Could new interim debt, liens, or guarantees affect approval?
  • What could reduce the loan amount or delay disbursement?

Have the lender confirm relevant requirements and have legal counsel review the documents. Do not assume that an expense qualifying for the bridge will also qualify for permanent financing.

Identify the correct entity

A multi-entity practice needs more than a consolidated cash balance. The operating practice, a separate location entity, and a property-owning entity may have different obligations and accounts.

Identify who borrows, who pays each expense, who receives the permanent proceeds, and who is responsible for repayment. Ask the CPA and legal adviser to review any proposed transfers between entities. Cash in one account is not automatically available to satisfy another entity’s debt.

2. Build a timeline with decision gates

The timeline should connect spending commitments to evidence of financing progress. Include both the expected closing date and the bridge’s contractual maturity date; these are different deadlines.

Illustrative example: A practice is considering interim funding for a second location while pursuing a longer-term expansion loan. The following timeline is a planning scenario, not a promised lending timetable.

Planning point Required evidence or action Spending decision
Before signing the bridge Review proposed permanent financing, interim debt restrictions, and repayment eligibility Do not treat an indicative loan amount as committed funding
Before initial project payments Confirm bridge documents, usable proceeds, vendor schedules, and entity responsibilities Release only approved initial payments
During buildout Update project costs, lender conditions, and expected closing date weekly Reassess uncommitted equipment and hiring costs
At the internal delay trigger Compare remaining cash with the revised closing date and bridge maturity Pause deferrable spending and request written financing updates
Before bridge maturity Confirm repayment funds or an executed alternative arrangement Do not rely on an unsigned extension
At permanent funding Reconcile actual proceeds and settlement deductions Repay according to the documents and retain payment evidence

Choose the internal delay trigger early enough to leave time to act. It should precede the point when the practice must make another large, irreversible payment or loses the ability to preserve payroll cash.

Give each action an owner. The practice manager may maintain vendor commitments, the finance team may update cash forecasts, and the owner may handle lender decisions. One person should maintain the consolidated timeline so that a revised contractor schedule also reaches the financing model.

3. Prepare a sources-and-uses schedule

List cash uses by payment date, entity, and commitment status. Separate amounts already contracted from amounts that can still be reduced or deferred.

For a staffed practice, include provider and administrative payroll during the ramp-up period, occupancy costs, deposits, equipment, professional fees, and financing costs where applicable. Forecast payer receipts and patient collections by expected collection date—not by appointment date or billed charges.

Buildout and equipment payments $140,000 Confirm payment milestones and cancellation terms
Incremental provider and administrative payroll $50,000 Schedule by actual pay period

Financing and professional costs $10,000 Replace estimates with documented quotes

This schedule balances, but that alone does not establish feasibility. A source may arrive after a payment is due. Fees withheld from loan proceeds can also make usable cash lower than the loan’s face amount. Reconcile gross borrowing, deductions, and cash deposited so costs are neither omitted nor counted twice.

Protect operating cash explicitly

Set an operating cash floor based on the practice’s payroll calendar, recurring bills, debt service, and collection variability. Show it separately from project funding. Do not count the same dollars as both expansion cash and protection for existing operations.

For multiple locations, review location-level performance and shared-cost allocations. A profitable established office may support an expansion, but the proposal should disclose that support rather than hide it in consolidated results.

4. Compare financing options using actual proposed terms

Debt and owner contributions are options to examine with lenders, CPAs, and legal advisers—not structures presumed available to every practice.

Option to examine Questions before proceeding
Short-term loan What are the net proceeds, payment schedule, maturity, collateral requirements, guarantees, and default consequences?
Draw-based credit facility Are draws committed or conditional? Can the facility fund the intended costs, and what could restrict availability?
Owner loan Can the owner fund the commitment? What documentation, repayment terms, approvals, and lender restrictions apply?
Owner capital contribution How will it be documented and reflected in ownership records? Is any later return of funds permitted or expected?
Reduced or phased project Which obligations can be deferred without creating larger costs or disrupting existing operations?

Do not choose solely on the stated interest rate. Compare total dollar cost through the expected payoff date and through a delayed closing. Include fees, required payments, prepayment provisions, extension terms, and any charges supported by the actual proposal.

An owner contribution should not be modeled as repayable on demand merely because the owner hopes to recover it after refinancing. Likewise, an owner loan should not be assumed to rank ahead of other obligations. Those expectations require document review.

5. Test repayment under three outcomes

The repayment model should separate the expected source from a fallback. “Obtain another loan” is not a dependable fallback unless there is a documented arrangement whose conditions the practice can meet.

Start with net proceeds:

Cash available for bridge repayment = permanent financing proceeds − closing deductions − other required uses − cash needed to preserve operations.

Then compare available cash with principal, accrued interest, and any applicable payoff charges. Avoid counting receivables at face value or equipment at purchase price as immediately available repayment cash.

Downside repayment worksheet

Illustrative example: Continue with the proposed $180,000 bridge. Assume the expected payoff is $189,000, including $9,000 of financing cost due at payoff. These are modeling assumptions, not market rates or quoted terms.

The expected permanent financing is $300,000. After $15,000 of closing deductions and $90,000 of other required uses, $195,000 remains for bridge repayment.

Worksheet item Closes as expected Closing delayed Permanent financing fails
Bridge principal $180,000 $180,000 $180,000
Financing cost due at payoff $9,000 $12,000 $12,000
Total repayment obligation $189,000 $192,000 $192,000
Cash available for repayment $195,000 $186,000 $115,000
Repayment shortfall $0 $6,000 $77,000

In the illustrative delay case, additional operating costs consume $9,000 of the originally available $195,000, leaving $186,000. The higher financing cost adds further pressure. This scenario assumes an extension is available; the actual documents may instead require payment at the original maturity.

In the failure case, the illustrative $115,000 consists of $45,000 of cash above the operating floor, $40,000 of estimated net equipment-sale proceeds, and a $30,000 owner commitment. Each needs verification. Asset-sale timing, lender consent, and the owner’s ability to fund can materially change the result.

A projected shortfall is a decision signal, not a detail to defer until closing. Reduce the project, secure a documented alternative, change the proposal, or decline the bridge if repayment depends on assumptions the practice cannot support.

6. Assemble the approval package and monitor it

Prepare a concise decision memo with supporting schedules:

  • Purpose, borrowing entity, requested amount, and proposed terms.
  • Permanent-funding status and unresolved conditions.
  • Weekly cash forecast through expected repayment and a longer delay period.
  • Sources and uses tied to contracts, quotes, and payment dates.
  • Existing debt, proposed guarantees, and collateral obligations.
  • Repayment scenarios, spending triggers, and responsible decision-makers.

Support the package with reconciled books and entity-level accounts. IRS Publication 583 explains the importance of complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices help owners distinguish available cash from unsupported balances.

After funding, compare actual spending and receipts with the proposal each week. Update the remaining bridge balance, unused funding, permanent-loan conditions, and projected payoff. Escalate changes before they threaten payroll or contractual payments.

Use aggregated financial information where possible. Patient-identifiable details generally are not needed to evaluate bridge repayment; do not put them into ordinary Slack conversations or general-purpose AI tools.

Make the decision on repayment resilience

A sound bridge proposal does more than show that expansion costs can be funded. It shows when obligations become binding, how repayment cash will arrive, and what the practice will do if the expected financing disappears.

Proceed only when the timing, documents, and downside plan support the obligation—not merely because the expected closing would make the numbers work.

FAQs

Can a medical practice use bridge financing before a long-term loan closes?

It may be an option, subject to lender approval and the relevant agreements. Confirm that interim borrowing is permitted and that permanent proceeds can repay it. An expected long-term loan is not a guarantee of repayment funding.

How much should the practice request?

Base the request on dated cash uses, available practice cash above the operating floor, usable loan proceeds, and a defined timing contingency. Then test whether the resulting obligation remains manageable under delay and failure scenarios. The largest available loan is not necessarily the right amount.

What if permanent financing does not close?

Use the pre-agreed downside plan: stop uncommitted spending, update cash needs, contact the lender promptly, and assess documented alternatives with advisers. An extension, asset sale, owner contribution, or replacement loan should not be treated as assured. If the fallback leaves a material repayment shortfall, resolve that risk before signing the bridge.

Should an owner advance money instead of borrowing externally?

Compare both proposals using the owner’s actual funding capacity, documentation, repayment expectations, and the effects on the practice. Have the CPA and legal adviser distinguish a loan from a capital contribution and review restrictions in existing financing and ownership agreements. Neither option eliminates the need for a downside cash plan.
