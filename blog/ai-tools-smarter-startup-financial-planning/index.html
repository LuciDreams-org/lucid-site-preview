Evaluating AI Financial Planning Tools for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An AI planning tool may produce a convincing forecast without answering the question a private practice owner actually needs resolved: can the practice afford another provider and the supporting staff if payer receipts arrive later than expected?

Selecting a tool requires more than a polished demonstration. You need evidence that it uses approved financial inputs, preserves entity boundaries, exposes its calculations, and produces scenarios your accountant can verify. You also need to know how much review it requires—and what happens when an import or calculation fails.

The most useful purchasing process is a controlled pilot with a defined decision, a synthetic test dataset, and written acceptance criteria. This guide explains what to request, how to test it, and when to accept, restrict, or reject a tool.

Start with the decision, not the feature list

Choose one recurring planning decision before inviting vendors to demonstrate their products. For a staffed medical practice, that might be hiring a provider, adding administrative coverage, or funding a second location.

Write a short pilot brief that specifies:

  • Decision: Whether a proposed hire fits within the practice's approved cash plan.
  • Outputs: Monthly staffing costs, receipts, ending cash, and location/entity views.
  • Reviewers: The practice manager checks operating assumptions; the accountant checks calculations and accounting treatment; the owner approves the decision.
  • Reference model: An accountant-checked spreadsheet built independently from the same inputs.
  • Acceptance rules: Required calculations, acceptable rounding differences, review workload, and failure conditions.

Set these rules before seeing the results. Otherwise, a visually appealing dashboard can distract from missing payroll costs or unsupported receipt assumptions.

Distinguish calculation reliability from forecast uncertainty. A tool can calculate a scenario correctly while the assumed collections never materialize. The pilot should test whether the tool handles assumptions faithfully—not whether it can guarantee future revenue.

Define the evidence each vendor must provide

Ask the vendor to work through your test rather than substitute a prepared demonstration. Treat every capability as unverified until you observe it or receive suitable documentation.

Requirement What to test Evidence needed for acceptance
Staffing-cost assumptions Change provider compensation, administrative support, benefits, employer costs, and start dates separately Editable inputs and a visible bridge from each assumption to monthly expense and cash payments
Entity and location structure Assign costs and receipts to the correct entity and location Separate reports, explicit shared-cost allocations, and a combined view without double counting
Editable calculations Change a receipt lag or hiring date Inspectable formulas or calculation rules that update consistently without hidden overrides
Scenario exports Export base, delayed-receipt, and delayed-hire cases Files showing periods, assumptions, scenario names, and results that can be independently reconciled
Input traceability Follow a forecast line back to its approved input Source file or report, version, period, mapping, and any manual adjustment
Review workflow Have the accountant investigate a discrepancy A practical way to identify, explain, correct, and recheck the result
Failure recovery Introduce a duplicate file, missing month, or failed refresh A visible warning and a repeatable route back to an approved state

A narrative explanation is not a substitute for a calculation. If the tool cannot expose a formula directly, require enough detail to reproduce the result independently. If neither is possible, it should not be the basis for a consequential hiring decision.

Build a controlled input package

Begin with synthetic data. Do not upload patient-identifiable information to a general-purpose AI tool. A hiring-plan test can use invented aggregate payer receipts, patient collections, staffing costs, and cash balances without patient records.

After the synthetic test passes, consider a limited pilot using approved practice-level financial information. Have the accountant reconcile the starting balances and identify unresolved items before forecasting from them. The IRS business recordkeeping guidance supports complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation; it does not validate any planning vendor.

The input package should identify:

  • The approved accounting period and whether each report uses cash or accrual accounting.
  • Opening cash by entity, including any restrictions relevant to the decision.
  • Aggregate payer receipts and patient collections, distinguished from billed charges.
  • Provider and administrative payroll assumptions, benefits, and other employer costs.
  • Location costs, shared overhead, and the approved allocation method.
  • Planned equipment payments, debt payments, owner distributions, and other cash movements where relevant.

Keep an assumption register with the input value, owner, source, and approval status. Require the tool to distinguish supplied assumptions from inferred values. Missing information should trigger a question or a visible exception—not an invented number.

Keep financial data boundaries explicit

Excluding patient information does not eliminate all data-handling concerns. Payroll files, bank details, and financial statements also require controlled access.

Request documentation explaining what data the vendor stores, who can access it, whether it is used for model training, which subprocessors receive it, and how export and deletion work. Confirm contractual terms rather than relying on a sales demonstration.

If a later workflow would involve protected health information, obtain a separate privacy and contractual review. HHS cloud guidance explains that a cloud service maintaining electronic PHI may be a business associate even if it cannot decrypt the information. Encryption alone does not settle those obligations. The procurement pilot should not introduce PHI merely to demonstrate forecasting.

Run a synthetic hiring-plan test

The following is an illustrative example using entirely invented figures, not a compensation benchmark or a prediction of practice performance.

Ask each candidate tool to model a provider hire with supporting administrative time over three months. Have the accountant independently check the reference spreadsheet before comparing results.

Approved test assumptions

Input Illustrative assumption
Opening unrestricted cash $120,000 in the employing entity
Existing operations Net cash movement of $0 each month, solely to isolate the hiring decision
Provider compensation $15,000 monthly, beginning in Month 1
Added administrative payroll $3,000 monthly, beginning in Month 1
Benefits and other employer costs $3,000 monthly combined; a fixed test assumption, not a statutory rate
Incremental overhead $2,000 monthly
One-time recruiting and setup payment $6,000 in Month 1
Incremental cash receipts $0 in Month 1, $10,000 in Month 2, and $20,000 in Month 3
Payment timing All listed costs are paid in the month shown

Recurring incremental cash costs equal $23,000 per month. The reference calculation is:

Ending cash = opening cash + existing-operation net cash movement + incremental receipts − incremental cash costs.

Expected reference results

All figures below belong to the same illustrative example.

Result Month 1 Month 2 Month 3
Incremental receipts $0 $10,000 $20,000
Recurring incremental costs $23,000 $23,000 $23,000
One-time payment $6,000 $0 $0
Incremental net cash movement −$29,000 −$13,000 −$3,000
Ending cash $91,000 $78,000 $75,000

The three-month cumulative cash reduction is $45,000. A tool that reports a different result must expose a reconcilable reason. Check especially for omitted employer costs, duplicated setup expenses, or recognition of billed charges as cash receipts.

Next, delay every incremental receipt by one month while leaving costs unchanged. Receipts within the test window become $0, $0, and $10,000. Expected ending cash becomes $91,000, $68,000, and $55,000. The tool should identify that the remaining receipts fall outside the displayed horizon rather than silently discard them.

Finally, delay the hire itself. Specify which costs move and which do not: a recruiting payment might remain scheduled even when compensation begins later. This reveals whether the model follows explicit assumptions or shifts every cost indiscriminately.

Test entity boundaries and shared costs separately

A correct combined forecast can conceal an incorrect entity-level cash position. For a multi-entity practice, add a separate synthetic test in which one entity employs staff while another pays shared overhead.

For example, use an illustrative $2,000 shared expense allocated $1,200 to one location and $800 to another. Require the tool to show:

  1. Which entity pays the original $2,000.
  2. How the allocation affects each location's P&L.
  3. Whether any reimbursement between entities is assumed.
  4. How the combined report avoids counting the expense twice.

An allocation is not automatically a cash transfer. Likewise, positive combined cash does not prove that the entity responsible for payroll has sufficient funds. Ask the accountant to approve the model's treatment of transfers and reimbursements; the tool should not invent them to resolve a shortfall.

Single-entity practices can use the same test for location reporting without adding unnecessary entity complexity.

Measure review effort and failure recovery

A fast first draft is not necessarily a low-effort planning process. Record the time spent preparing inputs, mapping accounts, checking outputs, correcting errors, and approving each scenario. Separate one-time setup from recurring review, and distinguish vendor assistance from work your own team can repeat.

Ask the practice manager—not only the vendor—to rerun an approved scenario. Then have the accountant trace selected results from source to output. Note unexplained differences, manual workarounds, and whether prior corrections survive a refresh.

Use deliberate failure tests:

Failure introduced Acceptable behavior to demonstrate
Duplicate receipt import Flags or prevents duplication before an output is treated as approved
Missing payroll month Identifies the gap rather than silently treating payroll as zero
Stale source file Shows the input period and makes the stale data visible
Incorrect entity mapping Allows correction and a clean rerun of affected outputs
Interrupted update Preserves access to the last approved result and identifies incomplete work
Unsupported AI explanation Allows reviewers to reject the explanation and inspect the underlying calculation

Save dated inputs, outputs, assumptions, and reviewer notes in a practice-controlled location. Confirm that an exported scenario remains understandable without continued vendor access. A PDF alone may be inadequate if reviewers cannot reconstruct the calculations.

Use a pilot scorecard with nonnegotiable gates

The following scoring framework is a suggested procurement method, not an industry standard. Score each dimension from 0 to 2: 0 means failed or unverified; 1 means usable with documented limitations; 2 means demonstrated and independently checked.

Dimension What the reviewer records
Calculation reliability Agreement with the reference model and explanation of differences
Staffing assumptions Complete, editable cost components and timing
Entity/location handling Correct boundaries, allocations, and combined reporting
Traceability Reproducible path from approved inputs to outputs
Scenario portability Useful exports with assumptions and calculation detail
Review effort Measured recurring workload and unresolved exceptions
Data handling Acceptable documented terms and access arrangements
Failure recovery Demonstrated correction, rerun, and recovery process

Do not let a total score override a critical failure. Before testing, designate mandatory gates such as correct reference calculations, acceptable data-handling terms, traceable inputs, and recoverable approved outputs.

Use the evidence to choose one outcome:

  • Accept for the tested use: Mandatory gates pass, and recurring review is manageable.
  • Accept with restrictions: Use only for a defined scope, such as a single-entity staffing forecast, with limitations documented.
  • Retest: A correctable defect has a named owner and requires a fresh demonstration.
  • Reject: Material errors remain unexplained, calculations cannot be checked, or data and recovery requirements are not met.

Make the purchase decision about verified usefulness

Compare the full operating burden: subscription and setup costs, internal preparation, accountant review, correction work, and the ability to leave with usable records. Do not assume the tool replaces financial oversight.

FAQs

Does a successful pilot prove future forecasts will be accurate?

No. It establishes that the tool handled the tested inputs and calculations acceptably. Actual receipts, staffing dates, and expenses can differ from assumptions. Require explicit downside scenarios and retain human approval for consequential decisions.

What should a practice do if the AI tool and spreadsheet disagree?

Pause approval and reconcile inputs, timing, formulas, and entity mappings. The spreadsheet should also be checked; it is not automatically correct. Accept a result only when the accountant can explain the difference and reproduce the approved calculation.

Can a practice evaluate a tool without sharing patient information?

Yes. Start with synthetic financial data, then consider approved aggregate inputs if needed. Hiring costs and receipt timing can be tested without patient-identifiable records. Review payroll and business financial data handling separately, and obtain appropriate privacy review before considering any workflow involving PHI.
