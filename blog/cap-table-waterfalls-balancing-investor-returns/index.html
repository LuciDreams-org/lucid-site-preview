Cap Table Waterfalls: Balancing Investor Returns

November 2, 2026 · 13 min read

Your ownership percentage doesn’t tell you what you’ll receive in a sale. I start with the cash available after debt, fees, and deal adjustments - then apply each holder’s payout rights. In the article’s hypothetical $20 million sale, just $16 million reaches equity holders, and investor preferences leave $4 million for owners and eligible employees.

Here’s what I check before relying on an exit waterfall:

  • Who owns the entity being sold: A practice sale and an MSO sale may pay different holders.
  • Who gets paid first: Signed documents set preference amounts, payment priority, conversion rights, and participation caps.
  • What each holder can receive: Employee awards depend on vesting, exercise costs, and transaction terms - not just fully diluted ownership.
  • When payment arrives: Closing cash, escrow, earnouts, and rollover equity need separate treatment.
  • Whether the numbers hold up: I compare low, middle, and high sale values, reconcile every dollar, and update the model as terms change.

The goal is simple: show <u>who gets paid, how much, and why</u> - with legal and tax review before treating estimates as final.

The Flow of Funds: How to Make Cap Tables Flow Like Water

Build the Water From Ownership and Deal Terms

Once the payout order is set, build the waterfall from the executed deal documents - not the informal cap table. Link every input to its source document.

Use the charter and amendments, stock purchase and financing agreements, investor rights agreements, option and warrant agreements, SAFE and convertible-note documents, board approvals, and merger or purchase agreement. Reconcile those documents with the entity-level general ledger, capitalization records, and closing statement.

Calculate Proceeds Available to Equity Holders

Start by checking what the headline figure represents: enterprise value, equity value, or total consideration. For an enterprise-value deal, use:

Equity proceeds = enterprise value + cash − debt − debt-like liabilities ± working-capital adjustments − transaction expenses

This calculation comes before escrow, holdbacks, and similar adjustments. Follow the purchase agreement’s definitions, including its working-capital target. If the headline figure already represents equity value, don’t apply the enterprise-to-equity calculation again.

Keep closing cash separate from escrow, holdbacks, earnouts, deferred consideration, and rollover equity. Model later payments separately using their contractual allocation rules, and count fees and debt only once.

For a multi-entity sale, allocate consideration, expenses, debt, and retained cash by entity before applying each entity’s waterfall. This establishes the pool available before preference terms apply.

Record Securities and Preference Terms

Use verified terms only. Track accrued dividends, redemption amounts, and similar entitlements in a separate schedule, and include them only when payable.

Reconcile issued shares, fully diluted capitalization, and securities entitled to distributions separately. Keep granted options separate from ungranted reserves. Document how options, warrants, notes, and SAFEs are exercised, canceled, or converted.

Set the Order of Payment

Group preferred equity into senior, pari passu, and junior tiers, subject to allowed conversion choices. Pay senior tiers first, followed by pari passu tiers, then junior tiers. Common stock receives the residual, with participating preferred sharing as its terms allow.

Pari passu means equal rank - not equal proceeds. If the available proceeds cannot cover a tier, apply the agreement’s pro rata shortfall formula rather than ownership percentages. Preferred liquidation preferences are equity rights, not creditor claims.

Calculate Preferences, Conversions, and Remaining Payouts

Once the payment order is set, calculate what each class receives at each exit value.

Compare Preferred Equity Payout Rules

Nonparticipating preferred receives its preference or its as-converted value - whichever is better - not both.

Preferred type Payment order Conversion behavior Residual participation Investor return and effect on common
Nonparticipating Receives its preference before junior securities, unless conversion pays more Takes the better permitted preference or conversion amount None when taking the preference Protects against downside while allowing upside through conversion
Participating, uncapped Receives its preference first, then shares in remaining proceeds on an as-converted basis Usually keeps preferred status to receive both payments Yes, without a cap Can materially reduce common holders’ proceeds
Participating, capped Receives its preference first, then participates until the contractual cap is reached Stops participating at the cap; conversion may pay more if permitted Yes, but limited Protects investors while setting a defined limit on their proceeds

Specify whether the cap includes the preference, dividends, or both.

Test Conversion and Divide Remaining Proceeds

Test all permitted conversion elections together. One class’s conversion changes both the preference claims and the shares eligible for remaining proceeds.

Apply automatic-conversion triggers, election requirements, and adjusted conversion ratios. Then pay valid preferences and divide the remaining proceeds among eligible shares only. When a class reaches its participation cap, stop its participation and exclude it from later allocations if the agreement requires.

Employee awards sit below the preferred stack and require separate eligibility checks. Review vesting, exercise rights, assumption, acceleration, cancellation, and cash-out terms. Deduct exercise costs where required, and use cashless settlement only when permitted. A fully diluted award doesn’t automatically qualify for a payout.

Check That Every Dollar Is Accounted For

Reconcile to zero: payouts must be nonnegative, debt and fees must be deducted only once, and no holder may receive incompatible preference and conversion payments. Allocation weights must total 100% within each funded distribution pool. Track retained and contingent amounts separately from closing cash.

Run these checks for every exit-value scenario so the allocation matches total proceeds. Investor protection, common upside, and transaction mechanics must all reconcile in the calculation.

Once the waterfall mechanics are set, test returns at different exit values. Scenario tables show where preferences protect investors and where conversion shifts more value to common holders.

Find Conversion Thresholds and Participation Caps

At higher exits, conversion can pay more than the preference. But test both classes’ elections together, rather than comparing ownership percentages alone. Series B’s 1.0x preference equals its as-converted value at $40.0 million in net equity proceeds. Series A reaches that point at about $26.67 million. Because B is senior, A cannot receive proceeds ahead of B unless the governing documents allow it.

A cap limits total proceeds. A threshold compares the preference with the as-converted value. Once a cap is reached, later residual proceeds can shift to common holders.

Keep the Waterfall Current and Review Its Inputs

Update Terms and Check Model Controls

Update the waterfall whenever ownership, payout rights, debt, or sale terms change. This includes financings, secondary sales, grants, conversions, repurchases, and amendments. Outdated inputs can misstate who gets paid first and how much investors, owners, and employees receive. Reconcile inputs with the current capitalization ledger, charter or operating agreement, financing documents, award records, and approvals. Give each version an as-of date and record when each change took effect.

Keep a change log that records the source document, old and new values, preparer, reviewer, and prior version. Lock formulas while leaving inputs editable. A second reviewer should independently check ownership totals, preference balances, and conversion calculations. They should also confirm that holder-level payouts reconcile to distributable proceeds under a documented rounding rule. Track the legal and tax assumptions alongside these controls.

At each update, label every input as a documented fact, assumption, or counsel/tax review required. Counsel should resolve unclear priority, participation, conversion, and change-of-control provisions that could affect investor protection and common upside. Before treating modeled proceeds as final, tax professionals should review withholding, equity-compensation treatment, and the tax consequences of an asset sale versus an equity sale.

Use Current Entity-Level Financial Reports

Keep these controls accurate with current financial data. Use the same reporting cutoff for entity-level balance sheets, trial balances, debt schedules, and transaction-cost estimates. Replace book debt with current payoff amounts, including accrued interest and applicable penalties. Check that intercompany balances aren't counted twice and that fees don't appear in both recorded expenses and closing estimates. Update these inputs monthly or quarterly, again at signing, and immediately before closing.

Conclusion: Balance Investor Protection and Owner Upside

Ownership percentages do not determine exit returns. Debt, expenses, and other adjustments reduce exit proceeds before the contract’s payment order determines equity payouts. Compare liquidation preferences, conversion values, and participation rights, then check that payouts add up to net proceeds.

Run downside, base-case, and upside exit scenarios to see how these rules affect payouts. Identify conversion thresholds and participation caps, and show when founders and employees start sharing in the proceeds.

Those thresholds shift as terms change, so keep the model current. Before a financing or exit decision, update it, check it against current documents, and get legal, tax, and accounting review. Present the results as conditional outcomes, showing each stakeholder what they could receive and why.

FAQs

How can I estimate the exit value needed to receive a payout?

Build a liquidation waterfall model using your fully diluted cap table, including common shares, options, warrants, and SAFEs/convertible notes. Apply your investors’ actual liquidation preferences - such as 1x non-participating or 2x participating - to determine who gets paid first and how much each holder receives.

Then test different exit valuations to find the lowest exit value that meets your target payout. Lucid Financials supports this modeling, so you can quickly compare multiple exit values.

Can my options cost more to exercise than my exit payout?

Yes. Exercising your stock options can cost more than you receive at exit. This can happen if your strike price is higher than the fair market value per share at exit. It can also happen if the distribution waterfall pays investors with liquidation preferences first, leaving little or nothing for common shareholders.

Lucid Financials can help you model these exit scenarios with real-time data so you can see your potential financial outcomes in advance.

Which financing terms should I negotiate to protect my exit proceeds?

Negotiate 1x non-participating liquidation preferences. Avoid 2x or participating preferences, which can cut founder payouts. Favor weighted-average anti-dilution over full ratchet.

Define drag-along and tag-along rights narrowly. Require a supermajority for drag-along, and structure tag-along rights to preserve founder control.

If an option pool is required, base its size on a realistic 12–24-month hiring plan. Favor post-money sizing to avoid the option pool shuffle.
