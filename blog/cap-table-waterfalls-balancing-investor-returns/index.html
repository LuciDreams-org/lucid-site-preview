Ownership Waterfalls for Medical Practice Distributions

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

An ownership percentage does not necessarily determine an owner’s distribution. A medical practice’s governing agreements may specify different rules for distributions, capital repayments, and proceeds from a sale or liquidation.

An ownership waterfall is a step-by-step calculation showing how an identified pool of money moves through those agreed priorities. For a practice owner, its purpose is practical: understand a proposed payment, explain why recipients receive different amounts, and identify questions that need adviser review before cash moves.

The model does not create economic rights. It traces rights that counsel has confirmed from the applicable agreements. Tax treatment and enforceability require separate review by licensed advisers.

First Identify the Distribution Event

Before calculating anyone’s share, identify what the practice is distributing and which entity is making the payment. A quarterly operating distribution is not automatically governed by the same provisions as a practice sale.

Event Financial starting point Agreement questions to resolve
Routine operating distribution Cash proposed for distribution after operating needs and reserves Which owners participate, in what proportions, and with what approvals?
Contractual tax distribution Amount calculated under the agreement’s specific provision Who qualifies, how is the amount determined, and does it offset later distributions?
Owner redemption or buyout Payment specified under the applicable buyout arrangement Is this a purchase of an interest rather than a distribution to all owners?
Sale or liquidation Proceeds available after transaction-specific obligations and retained amounts Does this event trigger a special waterfall, capital repayment, or other priority?

Do not infer a payment obligation from the name of an account. An entry labeled “owner capital” does not, by itself, establish a right to receive that balance before other owners.

Likewise, provider compensation, an owner loan repayment, and an ownership distribution are different categories. A physician’s compensation for services should not be folded into the ownership waterfall merely because that physician also owns the practice.

Reconcile Recipients to the Correct Entity

A practice may operate through one entity, several location entities, or separate operating and property entities. The payment model must follow the entity making the distribution—not the practice’s brand name or a investor-ready reports.

For example, an owner’s interest in the clinical operating entity does not establish an identical interest in a separately owned building entity. A location-level P&L helps explain performance, but a location is not necessarily a separate legal entity with its own owners.

Prepare a recipient schedule before modeling payments:

Record What to confirm Why it matters
Current ownership register Legal holder name, entity, interest class, units or percentage, and effective date Establishes who holds the relevant interest
Executed governing agreements and amendments Applicable distribution provisions and approval requirements Establishes the rules to be interpreted by counsel
Transfers, admissions, and redemptions Whether ownership changes became effective before the payment cutoff Prevents payments to an outdated holder list
Prior distribution records Amounts already paid and their contractual classification Prevents duplicate capital repayments or missed offsets
Owner loan schedule Creditor, balance, and documented repayment terms Keeps debt payments separate from equity distributions
Transaction documents, when applicable Sellers, consideration, retained amounts, and allocation provisions Connects the waterfall to the actual transaction

Use a consistent as-of date. If ownership changed during the period, ask counsel which cutoff or allocation convention applies rather than choosing one based on convenience.

Staff members and employed clinicians do not become distribution recipients simply because they work at the practice. Include them only when documented ownership or another applicable contractual right supports the payment, and classify compensation-related payments separately.

Separate Ownership Percentages From Payment Priorities

Ownership records answer who owns an interest. The governing agreements answer how a particular distribution is allocated. Those answers may differ.

An agreement might require a defined capital amount to be returned before remaining cash is shared. Another agreement might distribute all available cash in stated percentages without a capital-return tier. Neither approach should be assumed.

For each applicable provision, create a short rules schedule:

  • Trigger: Which event activates the provision?
  • Eligible recipients: Which holders or interest classes participate?
  • Payment basis: Is the amount fixed, balance-based, or percentage-based?
  • Priority: What must be paid before the next tier receives anything?
  • Shortfall treatment: How is insufficient cash divided within a tier?
  • Prior-payment treatment: Which earlier payments reduce the remaining entitlement?
  • Residual sharing: Who receives cash after the preceding tiers are satisfied?

Attach the relevant agreement section and counsel’s confirmed interpretation to each rule. If language is ambiguous, mark the affected result as pending adviser verification rather than embedding an unsupported interpretation in a formula.

Determine How Much Cash Is Actually Available

For an operating distribution, start with reconciled entity-level cash and a current cash forecast. Payer receipts and patient collections may have arrived, but the practice still needs funds for provider and administrative payroll, benefits, rent, vendors, debt payments, and other obligations.

A useful planning calculation is:

Proposed operating distribution pool = reconciled cash − identified obligations − approved operating reserves

This is a cash-planning calculation, not a legal test or an automatic authorization to distribute. Obtain the approvals and adviser review applicable to the entity and its agreements.

For a sale, use the transaction’s defined consideration and closing statement. Do not automatically treat a headline purchase price as cash available to owners. Establish whether it represents enterprise value, equity consideration, or another measure before making adjustments.

Separate closing cash from escrow, holdbacks, deferred payments, contingent earnouts, and rollover interests. Do not deduct debt or fees twice if the stated starting amount already reflects them. In an asset sale, proceeds received by the entity are not automatically the amount available for immediate owner distributions; have advisers identify the relevant obligations and reserves.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliations. Those records help establish reliable financial inputs; they do not determine contractual payout priorities.

Illustrative Sale Waterfall: Capital Return Followed by Residual Sharing

The following is an invented numerical example, not an actual practice transaction or a recommended ownership arrangement. It assumes counsel has confirmed that the hypothetical agreement applies to this sale and provides these rules:

  1. Available cash first repays specified outstanding contractual capital-return amounts.
  2. Those amounts rank equally. If cash is insufficient, the agreement requires allocation in proportion to the outstanding amounts.
  3. After that tier is satisfied, remaining cash is divided 50% to Owner A, 30% to Owner B, and 20% to Owner C.
  4. No other preference, offset, or participation provision applies in this example.

The contractual capital-return amounts below are assumed to have been reconciled to prior contributions and repayments under that agreement. They are not assumed to equal tax basis or accounting capital balances.

Step 1: Establish the Cash Pool

The illustrative closing schedule identifies cash consideration before the following separately stated deductions:

Illustrative closing item Amount
Cash consideration before listed deductions $1,500,000
Debt payoff ($200,000)
Transaction expenses ($50,000)
Escrow withheld from current distribution ($150,000)
Cash available for this waterfall $1,100,000

This simplified example assumes advisers have confirmed that no additional obligations or reserves reduce the current pool. The escrow is not distributed at closing and has no assumed future recovery value in this calculation.

Step 2: Apply the Agreement’s Tiers

Recipient Confirmed ownership and residual share Outstanding contractual capital return Residual allocation Total current distribution
Owner A 50% $200,000 $400,000 $600,000
Owner B 30% $100,000 $240,000 $340,000
Owner C 20% $0 $160,000 $160,000
Total 100% $300,000 $800,000 $1,100,000

The first tier consumes $300,000, leaving $800,000 for residual sharing. Owner A receives more than 50% of the current distribution because the example agreement provides a capital-return tier before percentage sharing.

If the applicable agreement instead required every dollar to be allocated by ownership percentage, this model would be wrong. The priority exists only because the hypothetical agreement expressly provides it.

Step 3: Test a Shortfall

Using the same illustrative agreement, compare smaller available-cash pools:

Illustrative available cash Owner A total Owner B total Owner C total Result
$150,000 $100,000 $50,000 $0 Capital-return tier only; allocated in the required two-to-one proportion
$300,000 $200,000 $100,000 $0 Capital-return tier fully satisfied; no residual
$1,100,000 $600,000 $340,000 $160,000 Capital-return tier plus residual sharing

An unpaid capital-return amount in a shortfall is not automatically a guaranteed future payment or an owner debt. Counsel must confirm how the agreement treats it.

Keep Operating Distributions and Later Sale Payments Separate

Do not reuse the sale model for routine operating distributions without checking the governing provisions. An agreement may apply percentage sharing to ordinary distributions but a different sequence to liquidation proceeds.

Prior payments can also matter. A routine distribution might reduce a later entitlement, or it might not. The agreement’s definitions control that treatment—not the bookkeeping label alone.

Maintain separate schedules for:

  • Operating distributions and any contractual offsets.
  • Capital-return payments and remaining entitlements.
  • Owner loan repayments.
  • Closing sale distributions.
  • Escrow releases and other later consideration.

When escrow is released, determine whether the agreement requires the waterfall to continue from prior payments or requires another allocation method. Do not assume the release should be divided by current ownership percentages.

Review the Model Before Authorizing Payment

A useful review connects the financial pool, contractual rules, and recipient totals.

Control Required check
Entity boundary Each cash pool belongs to the entity whose rights are being modeled
Recipient reconciliation Every recipient matches current ownership or another confirmed contractual entitlement
Financial reconciliation Cash, debt payoffs, fees, and retained amounts match supporting records
Tier sequence No later tier receives cash before required earlier payments are satisfied
Prior payments Previously satisfied entitlements are not paid again
Allocation weights Percentages total 100% within each applicable pool
Total payout Recipient payments equal available cash, with rounding differences resolved
Version control The model shows its as-of date, documents used, preparer, reviewer, and open questions (see our guide to financial planning systems)

Escalate unclear definitions of “available cash,” capital-return balances, event triggers, shortfall allocations, and treatment of departed owners to counsel, referencing your equity-splitting framework. Ask the CPA to review financial inputs and tax consequences separately. A pre-tax distribution schedule is not a take-home cash estimate.

Give Owners a Clear Decision Packet

Before approval, provide a concise summary showing the proposed event, entity, cash pool, deductions, governing provisions, recipient amounts, timing, and unresolved assumptions. Include a lower-cash scenario if material closing adjustments or reserve needs remain uncertain.

The decision is whether to authorize the proposed payment on the confirmed terms—not whether the spreadsheet produces an attractive outcome.

FAQs

Does a 30% ownership interest mean I receive 30% of every distribution?

Not necessarily. The applicable agreement may provide different priorities or allocation rules for different events. Reconcile the ownership interest first, then apply the adviser-confirmed provisions governing that particular payment.

Can we distribute the profit shown on our practice P&L?

Profit is not the same as available cash. Review reconciled bank balances, outstanding obligations, debt payments, and operating reserves. Then confirm the agreement’s distribution provisions and required approvals before authorizing a payment.

Is an accounting capital balance automatically repaid first in a sale?

No. Do not treat an accounting balance as a contractual priority without confirmation. Have counsel identify any capital-return provision and have the accounting team reconcile the amount using the agreement’s definitions and prior payments.

What if the agreement does not clearly explain the payment order?

List the ambiguous language and the allocations it could affect. Counsel should resolve the interpretation before the model is treated as final. Any interim scenarios should be labeled as alternatives, not approved payout instructions.

Does the waterfall show what each owner keeps after tax?

No. It shows allocations under the modeled terms. Tax treatment, withholding, and after-tax results require separate analysis by qualified tax advisers. Present gross distributions separately from any reviewed tax estimates.
