Medical Practice Exit Metrics: IPO vs. Acquisition

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

For a staffed private medical practice, exit readiness starts with a financial question: Can another party trace reported earnings to reliable records and understand how those earnings become cash?

An acquisition discussion may focus on the practice’s sustainable earnings, collection patterns and operating obligations. Participation in a larger platform pursuing an initial public offering, or IPO, adds another perspective: whether the practice’s financial information can support consistent reporting across a larger organization.

These are not equivalent paths. A direct IPO is not a routine exit route for an individual private practice. A platform’s possible future IPO is also not a guaranteed liquidity event for a participating owner. Transaction feasibility, ownership restrictions and tax outcomes require advice from appropriately licensed advisers.

This article focuses on the evidence owners and managers can prepare—not valuation promises or transaction structuring.

Two-Path Financial-Readiness Matrix

The underlying financial records matter in both paths. The difference is how those records will be used and how consistently they must fit into a broader reporting process.

Financial area Acquisition discussion Participation in a platform pursuing an IPO Evidence to prepare
Earnings quality Establish sustainable practice earnings and test proposed adjustments Support consistent earnings definitions across practices and reporting periods Monthly financial statements, general ledger and adjustment schedules
Revenue quality Explain payer receipts, patient collections, adjustments and revenue trends Support comparable revenue recognition and collection reporting across the platform Revenue-policy documentation and reconciliations between billing reports, books and bank activity
Cash conversion Show whether earnings produce cash sufficient for operations Explain cash generation and funding needs across entities Earnings-to-cash bridge, receivables trends and cash-flow reports
Working capital Establish ordinary operating needs and identify unusual balances Support repeatable working-capital reporting across locations and entities Receivables aging, payables, accrued payroll and other operating balances
Debt and commitments Identify financing obligations and recurring payment burdens Support consolidated visibility into debt, leases and other commitments Debt schedules, lender statements and commitment summaries
Concentration Assess dependence on particular payers, providers or locations Identify concentrated exposures within the broader group Consistently defined concentration schedules
Reporting readiness Produce a defensible diligence package Supply reliable inputs for the platform’s reporting requirements Close checklist, account reconciliations and entity/location reporting

There is no universal margin, growth rate or cash-conversion threshold that makes a practice ready for either path. Definitions, trends and supporting records are more useful than a headline benchmark borrowed from another industry.

Start With Revenue Quality, Not Just Growth

An increase in practice revenue does not explain why performance improved or whether it can continue. Separate growth caused by additional provider capacity, new locations, payer-rate changes, collection improvements and temporary items.

Compare like periods and show new locations separately from established operations. A full year of activity at a recently opened office can otherwise look like growth in an unchanged business.

Reconcile Billing Activity, Revenue and Receipts

Charges, recognized revenue and cash receipts are different measures. Gross charges alone do not establish collectible revenue. Payer receipts may relate to services delivered in earlier periods, while patient balances may remain outstanding after payer processing.

Ask the finance team to document:

  • The accounting basis used in the financial statements.
  • How contractual adjustments, refunds and collection estimates are reflected.
  • How billing-system reports connect to recorded revenue and receivables.
  • How deposits connect to bank activity, including timing differences and unidentified receipts.

If the books are maintained on a cash basis, label them accordingly. Do not present cash receipts as accrual revenue or subtract an accrual receivable movement from cash-basis earnings without a properly prepared conversion.

The IRS business-recordkeeping guidance supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those habits provide a useful foundation, although transaction reporting may require additional work.

Reconcile Reported Earnings to Supported Adjustments

Net income and EBITDA answer different questions. EBITDA means earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It does not measure cash available to distribute: debt principal payments, equipment purchases and working-capital needs still matter.

“Adjusted EBITDA” adds another layer of judgment. An expense should not be removed simply because an owner would prefer a higher earnings figure. Every adjustment needs a documented reason and evidence of what ongoing operations will actually cost.

Illustrative Earnings Reconciliation

The following invented example demonstrates the reconciliation process. It is not a practice benchmark, valuation estimate or actual adviser conclusion. All amounts cover the same annual period.

Reconciliation item Illustrative amount Evidence and review needed
Reported net income $180,000 Tie to the financial statements and general ledger
Add interest expense $20,000 Tie to debt schedules and expense accounts
Add income-tax expense recorded in the statements $10,000 Include only the relevant amount actually recorded; confirm treatment with the CPA
Add depreciation $30,000 Tie to the fixed-asset schedule
Add amortization $10,000 Tie to supporting schedules
Reported EBITDA $250,000 Confirm that nothing has been counted twice
Proposed owner-compensation normalization +$60,000 Compare actual duties and compensation with a supported ongoing compensation assumption
Proposed nonrecurring relocation expense +$25,000 Support with invoices and evidence that it does not recur
Ongoing administrator replacement cost omitted from the initial proposal −$40,000 Document duties, staffing needs and expected ongoing expense
Recurring maintenance omitted from the initial proposal −$15,000 Establish whether it is an operating expense and support the ongoing requirement
Illustrative adjusted EBITDA, if all adjustments are accepted after review $280,000 Preserve the full bridge and each reviewer’s conclusion

Owner compensation deserves particular attention. If an owner continues providing medical services or management work, removing all of that compensation would overstate earnings unless the ongoing work and its cost are properly addressed. Keep clinical and administrative responsibilities distinct when evaluating replacement costs.

Maintain separate columns for proposed, adviser-reviewed and counterparty-accepted adjustments. An owner’s proposed add-back is not automatically accepted by a buyer or suitable for a platform’s reporting. Potential future savings should also remain separate from historical practice performance.

Test Whether Earnings Convert Into Cash

A practice can report positive earnings while struggling to fund provider and administrative payroll. Collection delays, old patient balances, payer recoupments and accumulated unpaid expenses can all affect cash availability.

For accrual financial statements, build an earnings-to-operating-cash bridge that explains noncash items and movements in receivables, payables and other operating balances. Then show equipment spending and financing payments separately.

Metric or schedule What it helps explain Important limitation
Operating cash flow Cash generated or used by operating activity Review consistently prepared statements, not bank-balance changes alone
Operating cash flow divided by EBITDA Relationship between cash generation and reported earnings Define the period and EBITDA measure; the ratio is not useful when EBITDA is zero or negative
Receivables aging Whether unpaid balances are becoming older Aging categories do not establish collectibility by themselves
Collection trends by service period Whether revenue from a period is turning into receipts Separate collection timing from changes in service volume
Cash after equipment spending and debt payments Cash remaining after those demands State exactly which payments are included; it is not automatically distributable cash

Do not treat delayed vendor payments as an operational improvement. They may temporarily lift cash while leaving the practice with obligations that still need funding.

For acquisition discussions, explain unusual collection or payment timing around the reporting date. For a platform, use repeatable definitions so one location’s cash results can be compared meaningfully with another’s.

Define Working Capital and Liquidity Separately

Working capital describes operating balances; liquidity concerns the ability to meet obligations when due. A practice may have substantial receivables but insufficient available cash for its next payroll.

Prepare a monthly schedule of receivables, accounts payable, accrued payroll and benefits, patient credit balances, and other relevant operating accounts. Explain seasonal movements and unusual balances rather than selecting a favorable month-end snapshot.

A transaction-specific working-capital definition may differ from a standard accounting presentation. Have transaction advisers confirm what belongs in that calculation; do not assume cash, debt or every current account receives the same treatment.

Alongside historical reporting, maintain a short-term cash forecast covering expected payer and patient receipts, provider/admin payroll, rent, vendors and financing payments. Use realistic collection timing rather than assuming that all outstanding balances will arrive promptly.

Make Debt and Other Obligations Visible

Prepare a financing schedule by legal entity. Include outstanding principal, interest terms, payment requirements, maturity information and supporting statements. Identify equipment financing, credit facilities and related-party balances separately.

Keep leases and other material operating commitments visible even when their accounting presentation differs from conventional debt. Do not subtract debt payments from EBITDA and call the result operating profit.

For multi-entity practices, reconcile intercompany balances on both sides. A transfer between related entities is not new revenue for the combined business. Similarly, cash held in one entity should not automatically be treated as freely available to another; confirm restrictions and permitted uses with advisers.

These schedules help explain financial exposure. They do not determine transaction proceeds or resolve legal responsibility for an obligation.

Measure Concentration Without Oversimplifying It

Concentration analysis should show what earnings depend on—not merely identify the largest revenue category.

  • Payer concentration: Calculate shares using a consistent basis, such as net revenue or receipts. Do not mix gross charges with collections.
  • Provider concentration: Show revenue dependence alongside provider compensation and supporting staffing costs. Revenue alone does not show the earnings contribution at risk.
  • Location concentration: Compare per-location profit and loss statements using consistent shared-cost allocations.
  • Service concentration: Separate materially different service categories when doing so helps explain revenue, expenses or cash requirements.

For shared billing, management or administrative costs, document the allocation method and apply it consistently. Show material central costs separately so location profitability does not appear stronger simply because expenses sit elsewhere.

A platform may diversify some exposures while retaining others. Joining a larger group does not eliminate reliance on a major payer or a key provider.

Build a Reporting Package That Can Be Repeated

Readiness is a workflow, not a one-time spreadsheet. Assign an owner to each schedule and establish a review process for unresolved differences.

A useful package includes:

  1. Monthly financial statements by entity, with location detail where relevant.
  2. Reconciled bank, credit-card, payroll, receivable and payable balances.
  3. A reported-to-adjusted earnings bridge with supporting documents.
  4. Cash-flow, working-capital and debt schedules.
  5. Revenue and concentration analyses with written definitions.
  6. A list of open accounting questions and their potential effects.

Ask the prospective platform what accounting policies, reporting periods and review requirements it expects. Reliable internal books are a foundation, not proof that public-company reporting or audit requirements have been met.

Financial analysis should minimize patient-identifiable information. Use aggregate reports where possible and obtain privacy guidance before sharing records. HHS explains the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions; the appropriate workflow depends on the circumstances. Do not place patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool.

Turn the Metrics Into an Owner Action Plan

Start with the weakest evidence, not the most flattering metric. If reported revenue cannot be reconciled, resolve that before refining adjusted earnings. If earnings appear strong but cash remains tight, investigate collections and unpaid obligations. If location profits vary sharply, test the shared-cost allocations before drawing conclusions.
