AI-Assisted Geographic Expansion Analysis for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Choosing a geographic market for a new medical practice location is different from choosing a building. Before paying for detailed site diligence, owners need to know which markets deserve further investigation—and which depend on assumptions that are too weak to support a decision.

A useful geographic screen compares documented occupancy costs, local staffing costs, payer-related assumptions, referral concentration and overlap with existing locations. AI can help organize that evidence and expose inconsistencies. It cannot establish demand simply by producing a market score.

The goal at this stage is a defensible shortlist, not a complete new-location forecast. Keep financing timing, construction budgets and launch cash-flow planning for later work. First, answer: Which market has the strongest evidence-supported financial case for further diligence, and what could change that choice?

Define the Comparison Before Collecting Data

Start with the same operating concept for every candidate market. Otherwise, a lower-cost market may appear attractive only because its scenario assumes fewer staff, a smaller footprint or a different service mix.

Document the proposed provider coverage, administrative staffing, approximate space requirements and services under consideration. Separate items that must remain constant from items that legitimately vary by market.

For an owner with existing locations, also define the geographic objective. Is the practice trying to reach a distinct patient population, improve access within its current service area or diversify referral relationships? These objectives lead to different interpretations of location overlap.

Establish decision gates before calculating a score. Examples include:

  • Suitable occupancy options have reasonably comparable cost documentation.
  • Local hiring evidence supports the required provider and administrative roles.
  • Material payer participation questions have a named reviewer.
  • Referral assumptions do not depend entirely on one unconfirmed relationship.
  • Possible transfers from existing locations are distinguished from incremental practice activity.

A missing gate should produce “hold for evidence,” not an invented midpoint score.

Build an Evidence Packet for Each Market

Compare occupancy on a consistent basis

Ask a local broker for written information on available space and the costs included in each quote. Base rent alone may not be comparable when one listing includes operating expenses and another quotes them separately.

For this screening exercise, identify recurring occupancy costs, quoted exclusions, space suitability questions and the date of the information. Do not turn a preliminary listing into an assumed all-in cost or a construction estimate.

Use a range when material costs remain unresolved. A market with several suitable options may deserve more confidence than one whose apparent advantage rests on a single unconfirmed listing.

Use role-specific staffing inputs

Compare the staffing configuration the practice would actually need: provider coverage, medical assistants, front-desk staff and location management, where relevant.

Separate wage or salary inputs from employer payroll costs and benefits. Document whether each input comes from current practice payroll, a local recruiting discussion, a job posting or another identified source. A posted salary range is not proof that the practice can fill a role at its lower end.

Existing payroll is a useful reference, but it should not automatically become the assumption for another labor market. Also identify any shared administrative capacity that the new market would consume. “Supported by headquarters” does not necessarily mean “no additional cost.”

Treat payer assumptions as unresolved until reviewed

Keep payer participation, expected payer mix and expected collections per comparable service separate. Do not infer reimbursement from neighborhood income, competitor presence or a practice’s experience in a different market.

Use existing practice collections only as an internal reference, with attention to service mix and collection periods. Billing and contracting reviewers should assess whether those assumptions can reasonably transfer to the candidate market.

A useful screen records the question, the evidence available and the person responsible for confirming it. It does not present an assumed collection rate as a verified local benchmark.

Examine referral concentration and existing-location overlap

Referral evidence should distinguish established relationships from introductory conversations and general market impressions. Several possible referral sources do not necessarily represent independent opportunities if they belong to the same organization.

For overlap, examine aggregated patient-origin areas and existing location catchments using an appropriately controlled workflow. Consider travel patterns and whether the proposed market would mainly redistribute current appointments.

Transferred activity may improve access or capacity utilization, but it is not automatically incremental revenue for the practice as a whole. Evaluate the potential effect on both the new location and the existing location’s contribution.

Show When the Preferred Market Changes

A ranking is more useful when the owner can see how fragile it is. Test the uncertain inputs that could reverse the result rather than producing only a weighted score.

An illustrative comparison—not a location forecast

The following illustrative example uses a common monthly reference collections amount of $150,000 at an identical assumed activity and service mix. That reference is a controlled comparison input, not evidence that either market can generate that activity.

For this limited screen:

Screening balance = reference collections × collections factor − occupancy − loaded staffing − contribution displaced at existing locations.

The displaced contribution is the estimated practice-level effect of activity moving from existing locations, after considering costs that would actually change. It is not simply the amount of receipts transferred.

The preference changes below a 94% factor under these assumptions. That threshold identifies a diligence priority: determine whether Market B’s payer-related assumptions are supportable before treating its lower occupancy costs as decisive.

Other inputs can reverse the result too. In this example, a $3,000 increase in Market B’s monthly staffing cost eliminates its base-case advantage. So does an additional $3,000 of displaced contribution from existing locations.

Test plausible combinations as well as single variables. Do not describe these ranges as statistical confidence intervals unless an appropriate statistical method supports that description.

Give AI a Bounded Analytical Role

AI can assist with extracting terms from supplied documents, normalizing labels, assembling comparison tables and drafting questions for reviewers. Each extracted figure should remain traceable to its document and relevant passage, with a person checking it before use.

A practical workflow is:

  1. Create the evidence register. Record the market, document owner, source date, units and limitations.
  2. Ask for structured extraction. Require missing information to remain marked as unverified rather than inferred.
  3. Reconcile comparable inputs. Check monthly versus annual figures, quoted versus loaded compensation, and included versus excluded occupancy costs.
  4. Calculate in a controlled model. Verify formulas and reconcile totals independently of the AI narrative.
  5. Challenge the recommendation. Ask which unsupported assumption could reverse the ranking and which evidence would resolve it.

AI cannot validate demand from a polished summary, confirm payer terms without appropriate evidence or replace local broker, recruiting, billing, legal and financial review.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of general-purpose AI tools and ordinary collaboration messages. Aggregated information can often support geographic screening, but aggregation alone should not be treated as proof that a dataset is safe to share. Follow the practice’s approved data-handling process. HHS provides guidance on the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions and on cloud services handling electronic protected health information.

Turn the Analysis Into a Shortlist Decision

The final screening memo should identify a preferred market, an alternative and the evidence required before either proceeds. Use three outcomes:

  • Advance to detailed diligence: the comparative case is supportable and major uncertainties have clear review paths.
  • Hold for evidence: missing information could reverse the ranking.
  • Remove from the current shortlist: available evidence conflicts with the practice’s selection criteria.

Keep unallocated shared costs visible rather than assigning them arbitrarily to make one market look better. For multi-entity owners, identify which entity’s records support each internal baseline; consolidated totals alone may hide location differences.

FAQs

Can population growth establish demand for a new medical practice location?

No. Population information may help describe a market, but it does not establish that the practice can attract patients at its assumed service mix and collections level. Review access patterns, referral evidence, payer assumptions, provider capacity and overlap with existing locations before drawing a financial conclusion.

Should the lowest-cost market rank first?

Not necessarily. Lower occupancy costs may be outweighed by staffing costs, weaker collections assumptions or greater displacement of existing-location contribution. Compare consistent operating configurations and test the inputs that could reverse the ranking.

What if payer or referral evidence is incomplete?

Keep those inputs explicitly unverified and use ranges to show their effect. Assign a reviewer and a specific evidence request. If the preferred market changes within a plausible range, hold the selection until that uncertainty is better understood rather than presenting a precise score as a decision.

Is this analysis enough to approve a lease?

No. Geographic screening selects markets for further investigation. Site suitability, lease terms, operating feasibility, legal questions and a complete location financial plan require separate diligence before a commitment.
