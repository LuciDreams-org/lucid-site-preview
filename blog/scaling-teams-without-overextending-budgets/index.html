Growing Medical Practice Teams Without Overextending Budgets

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can need another employee before it can comfortably afford one. Full schedules, administrative backlogs and recurring overtime may justify evaluating a position, but they do not establish that cash will be available when payroll comes due.

The hiring decision needs two tests: Can the practice sustain the ongoing employment cost, and can it fund the transition before expected receipts arrive? A profitable practice can still face a cash squeeze when recruiting, onboarding and overlapping coverage precede collections.

For owners and managers of staffed private medical practices, the goal is not simply to minimize payroll. It is to choose a support plan the practice can fund without relying on uncertain collections or leaving existing obligations uncovered.

Start With the Specific Hiring Decision

Define the proposed role before estimating its affordability. An additional front-desk employee, a medical assistant and a new provider have different cost structures and different relationships to receipts.

Document:

  • Whether the position replaces someone or adds ongoing capacity.
  • The proposed start date, schedule and employing entity.
  • Which location will use the employee’s time.
  • What coverage is needed during recruiting and training.
  • Whether the role supports existing activity or enables additional appointments.
  • Who approves the commitment and reviews the results.

For a replacement, include any gap between the departing employee’s final payroll and the new employee’s start, along with temporary coverage and training overlap. For an added position, do not assume the existing payroll budget contains room for it.

Separate the operational need from the financial response. A recurring backlog may warrant permanent support, while a short absence may call for temporary coverage. Neither conclusion should rest on a generic staffing ratio.

Calculate the Fully Loaded Cost, Not Just the Wage

A salary or hourly rate is only the starting point. Build an estimate using actual benefit terms, payroll information and vendor quotes rather than a universal employment-cost multiplier.

Cost category What to include How to estimate it
Direct compensation Scheduled wages or salary, expected overtime and applicable incentives Proposed offer and payroll calculations
Employer payroll costs Employer-side payroll taxes and applicable employment-related insurance Payroll provider or CPA estimate
Benefits Employer health contributions, retirement contributions and other offered benefits Actual plan terms and eligibility timing
Recruiting Advertising, recruiter charges and relevant screening expenses Quotes and expected payment dates
Onboarding Paid training, trainer coverage, equipment and account setup Role-specific onboarding plan
Transition coverage Overtime, temporary support and overlap with existing staff Coverage schedule and quoted rates
Recurring role expenses Software seats, supplies and other incremental costs Vendor terms and purchasing estimates

Distinguish economic cost from additional cash outflow. A manager’s time spent training a new employee has an opportunity cost, but it is not automatically extra payroll. Include additional paid hours or replacement coverage as cash outflows when they are actually expected.

Similarly, do not count paid leave twice if it is already included in salary. Estimate any separate coverage cost when the employee is absent.

Create both a steady-state cost estimate and a dated startup-cost schedule. A sustainable recurring wage can still be unaffordable if recruiting, equipment and coverage payments cluster around the first payroll.

Forecast Receipts Around the Start Date

The relevant question is when money will reach the practice’s bank account—not when an appointment is scheduled or a charge is posted.

Use recent payer receipt patterns, patient collections and known payment issues to estimate cash timing. Separate existing receipts from any additional collections attributed to the hire.

Treat different roles differently

An administrative hire may improve workflow without producing separately identifiable revenue. In that case, the base forecast can assume no incremental receipts and test whether existing collections can support the position.

A role intended to support additional appointments needs a more detailed bridge:

Additional completed appointments → expected collectible amounts → expected receipt dates → related incremental costs.

Do not use gross charges as expected cash. Consider payer mix, contractual adjustments, patient balances and collection uncertainty. If a new provider’s start depends on payer enrollment or other readiness steps, reflect the confirmed timing rather than assuming collections begin with the first paycheck.

Also include expenses associated with additional activity. Extra receipts are not all available for staff costs if they also require provider compensation, supplies or other spending.

Model a slower ramp

Prepare a base case and a downside case. The downside should address specific uncertainties: a later start, longer training, slower schedule growth or delayed payer receipts.

If the hire is affordable only when every favorable assumption occurs, the approval depends on a fragile forecast. Consider a later start, staged hours or a defined temporary bridge instead.

Use a Hire-Approval Worksheet

Compare realistic alternatives over the same planning period. A short-term option may cost less because it covers fewer hours or fewer responsibilities—not because it is a better substitute.

Illustrative example: A practice is considering an additional administrative employee. The worksheet below uses three four-week periods, not calendar months. These invented assumptions are planning examples, not staffing benchmarks or market wage estimates.

Assumptions:

  • Full-time wages are $4,000 per four-week period.
  • Estimated employer payroll costs and benefits add $1,000 per period.
  • Recruiting and onboarding require $2,000 in cash.
  • Immediate hiring requires $1,000 of additional transition coverage.
  • Staged hiring starts in the second period, with $3,000 of overtime coverage in the first.
  • Overtime-only support costs $3,000 per period, including estimated associated employer costs.
  • Temporary support costs $4,000 per period plus a $500 setup charge.

Illustrative comparison of hiring options

Worksheet item Hire immediately Start in period 2 Overtime bridge only Temporary support only
Employee wages, employer costs and benefits $15,000 $10,000 $0 $0
Recruiting and onboarding $2,000 $2,000 $0 $0
Overtime or transition coverage $1,000 $3,000 $9,000 $0
Temporary support and setup $0 $0 $0 $12,500
Total additional cash outflow $18,000 $15,000 $9,000 $12,500
Assumed incremental receipts during the period $6,000 $2,000 $0 $0
Net additional cash requirement $12,000 $13,000 $9,000 $12,500

The example assumes incremental receipts of $0, $2,000 and $4,000 across the periods for immediate hiring, versus $0, $0 and $2,000 for a delayed start. Those receipts require a documented basis; they are not an automatic result of adding staff.

Although staged hiring has lower total outflows here, its net cash requirement is higher because assumed receipts arrive later. If those receipt assumptions are unsupported, remove them and compare the full outflows instead.

Overtime and temporary support are bridges, not necessarily equivalent long-term solutions. Confirm available hours, appropriate duties, applicable pay requirements and contract terms before treating either as feasible. Include minimum commitments and cancellation charges where relevant.

Test the Lowest Cash Point Before Approving

A total cost comparison can hide a payroll-week shortfall. Build a weekly cash forecast through recruiting, onboarding and the expected ramp-up, extending it if collections take longer to stabilize.

Use this structure:

Beginning cash + expected receipts − existing obligations − hiring-related outflows = ending cash.

Existing obligations should include current payroll, rent, debt payments, planned tax payments, vendor commitments and other expected disbursements. Show proposed owner distributions separately so the decision does not quietly assume they will disappear.

Set a practice-specific minimum operating cash balance based on payment timing and collection uncertainty. Do not treat a generic reserve target as proof of affordability.

Illustrative cash-floor test

Continuing the example, assume forecast ending cash without the hire is $40,000, $42,000 and $46,000 across the three periods, after existing obligations. The owner selects a $30,000 operating cash floor for this illustration.

For immediate hiring, additional outflows are $8,000 in the first period and $5,000 in each later period. With the assumed incremental receipts, ending cash becomes:

Illustrative period Cash without hire Cumulative net hiring cash requirement Cash after hiring
Period 1 $40,000 $8,000 $32,000
Period 2 $42,000 $11,000 $31,000
Period 3 $46,000 $12,000 $34,000

If none of the additional receipts arrive during the modeled period, ending cash instead falls to $32,000, $29,000 and $28,000. The proposal fails the illustrative cash-floor test in the downside case.

Even the base case needs a weekly check: payroll may clear before a large payer deposit arrives. Period-end balances alone cannot establish that every payment is fundable.

Assign the Cost to the Right Location and Entity

For a multi-location practice, identify where the employee works and use a consistent basis for shared costs, such as documented time. Review the location’s P&L alongside the cash forecast; allocation explains profitability but does not create cash.

For multi-entity owners, identify which entity employs and pays the employee. Group-wide cash does not automatically establish that the payroll-paying entity has sufficient funds. Have the finance team review any proposed transfers or shared-cost arrangements before relying on them.

Clean records matter here. IRS Publication 583 discusses complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices help establish a reliable starting point for the hiring forecast.

Record the Approval and Revisit Actual Results

The approval should specify the position, start date, compensation, expected benefits, one-time spending limit, temporary coverage end date and minimum cash condition. Record who can approve changes to the offer or an extension of coverage.

After payroll and receipts arrive, replace estimates with actuals rather than simply checking whether total spending is “close.”

Review item Compare Decision supported
Payroll Actual gross pay and employer costs against the worksheet Correct the recurring cost forecast
Onboarding Actual setup, training and coverage spending Update remaining commitments
Receipts Bank deposits against expected amounts and dates Revise collection timing or remove unsupported growth
Temporary coverage Actual invoices and remaining commitments End, extend or replace the bridge deliberately
Cash Updated weekly balances against the approved floor Adjust discretionary spending or future hiring

Review after the first payroll and during the regular monthly close, with closer cash checks when headroom is tight. Separate timing differences from lasting changes: a delayed deposit and a permanently higher benefit cost require different responses.

If results miss the plan, update future commitments before approving another hire. Avoid treating already-promised employment costs as expenses that can simply be switched off.

FAQs

How do I know whether my medical practice can afford another employee?

Estimate fully loaded recurring costs and one-time hiring outlays, then place them in a dated cash forecast alongside existing obligations. Test both expected and slower receipt timing. Approval should depend on sustained affordability and adequate cash at the lowest point—not just a profitable P&L or a busy schedule.

Is overtime or temporary support cheaper than hiring?

It depends on duration, hours, employer costs, agency terms and coverage needs. Compare options over the same period and state where their scope differs. A lower short-term outlay does not establish that a bridge can replace a permanent role. Revisit the comparison before extending temporary arrangements.

Should expected revenue from a new hire pay for the position?

Only include additional receipts when there is a defensible connection between the role, additional activity and collection timing. For administrative roles, a conservative base case may assume no incremental receipts. For capacity-expanding roles, estimate collectible amounts and related expenses, then test what happens if receipts arrive later than planned.

What should change after the first payroll runs?

Replace estimated wages, employer costs and benefits with actual amounts. Update recruiting and onboarding commitments, compare deposits with the receipt forecast, and rerun the cash-floor test. Use the revised forecast to decide whether to end temporary coverage, maintain the plan or defer additional hiring.
