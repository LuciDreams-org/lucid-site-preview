Can my practice pass credit card fees on to patients?

December 9, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 9, 2026

In most states, yes, on credit cards. Visa lets a practice add a surcharge of up to 3%, never more than the practice pays to take the card, once it gives its processor 30 days' notice and posts signs. You can't surcharge debit or HSA cards, and Connecticut, Massachusetts and Maine ban card surcharges.

Key points

  • Visa caps a US credit card surcharge at 3%, or at your own average credit card rate if that's lower.
  • Federal law stops card issuers from blocking a discount for patients who pay by cash or check (15 U.S.C. 1666f).
  • On $40,000 a month of card payments with 30% on debit and HSA cards, a 3% surcharge collects $10,080 a year (Example 2).

What's the difference between a surcharge, a convenience fee and a cash discount?

A credit card surcharge is an extra charge a practice adds because the patient paid with a credit card. A convenience fee is a flat fee a practice charges for paying through a channel it doesn't normally use, not for the card itself. A cash discount is a lower price a practice offers to every patient who pays by cash, check or a similar method.

Fee type What it is The rule Cap
Surcharge A percentage added to credit card payments Visa: credit cards only; 30 days' notice to your processor; signs at the entrance and at checkout; shown on the receipt Visa: your average credit card rate, never more than 3%
Convenience fee A flat fee for paying online or by phone when you usually take payment in person Visa: same fee for every way to pay in that channel; not on recurring or installment payments; never on top of a surcharge Visa: a flat amount, not a percentage
Cash discount A lower price for cash or check Federal law: card issuers can't stop you from offering it; Connecticut requires a posted notice No limit in the federal law

Each card brand sets its own rules. This post covers Visa's. Mastercard's rules weren't checked for this post, so check them and every other brand you take before you start.

Can a medical practice add a credit card surcharge?

Yes, in most states, if you follow Visa's steps. You tell your processor in writing 30 days before you start, you post the fee at your entrance and at checkout, and you show it on every receipt. You charge it only on credit cards, and never more than your own average credit card rate or 3%.

  1. Tell your processor in writing at least 30 days before you announce or start the surcharge.
  2. Post signs at your main entrance (32 point type or larger) and at every checkout desk (16 point or larger).
  3. At checkout, state the percentage, that your practice charges it, that it's for credit cards only, and that it isn't more than your rate.
  4. Online, post it where you first list the cards you take, and again at checkout.
  5. Show it as its own line on the receipt.
  6. Keep it at or below your average credit card rate over the last one or 12 months, and never above 3%.

If you pay 2.6% on credit cards, you can't charge 3%.

Which states ban credit card surcharges?

Massachusetts bans surcharges on credit cards, Maine bans them on credit and debit cards, and Connecticut bans them on every way of paying. All three still let you offer a discount for cash. Other states limit how much you can add or how you post prices, so read your own state's law before you start.

  • Connecticut: no surcharge on any transaction. A discount for cash, check or debit is allowed with a posted notice (Conn. Gen. Stat. 42‑133ff).
  • Massachusetts: no surcharge when a patient pays with a credit card instead of cash or check (M.G.L. c. 140D, s. 28A).
  • Maine: no surcharge on a credit or debit card; a discount from the regular price isn't a surcharge (9‑A M.R.S. 8‑509).

If your practice has offices in more than one state, follow each state's law at each office.

Can I surcharge debit, HSA or FSA cards?

No. Visa doesn't allow a surcharge on a debit or prepaid card. Visa HSA cards run on prepaid or debit card numbers, and Visa FSA cards are prepaid. Visa bans it even when the patient presses "credit" on your terminal, so you'll need to set your terminal to tell debit from credit.

Can my payer contracts stop me from charging insured patients a fee?

They may. Your insurance contracts, and Medicare if you take it, may limit what you can collect from an insured patient for a covered visit. Read each contract's section on patient charges, or ask the payer, before you add a surcharge to an insured patient's balance.

How do I find what I really pay in card fees?

Divide the total fees on your monthly processor statement by your total card payments for the month. That's your effective rate. You may never have seen that total, because your processor may take the fees out before each deposit, and you'll often find them split across dozens of lines.

Your effective rate is your total card fees for a month divided by your total card payments for that month. Interchange is the fee the patient's card bank charges on each payment, set in rate tables the card networks publish.

Read your processor statement this way:

  1. Find total card volume, the full amount patients paid by card.
  2. Add every fee: interchange, network fees, the processor's markup, and monthly, terminal and chargeback fees.
  3. Divide total fees by total volume.
  4. Check whether your processor takes fees out of each deposit or charges them once at month end.
  5. Split credit from debit. Debit cards from banks with $10 billion or more in assets often cost you less, because the Federal Reserve caps their interchange at 21 cents plus 0.05% of the payment, plus 1 cent for fraud prevention.

How should card fees show in my books?

Record the full amount patients paid as revenue and your card fees as their own expense line. If you book only your deposits, you'll understate revenue, lose sight of your fees, and miss it when they go up.

Example 1: the same month, booked two ways. A dental practice takes $40,000 in card payments in March. Its processor charges $840 on $28,000 of credit card payments and $300 on $12,000 of debit and HSA card payments. The processor takes the $1,140 out before each deposit, so the practice receives $38,860.

Booked from deposits Booked from the statement
Card payments (revenue) $38,860 $40,000
Card fees (expense) $0 $1,140
Cash in the bank $38,860 $38,860
Effective rate you can see None 2.85%

You end with the same cash either way. Only the second way shows you $1,140 a month, or $13,680 a year, as a cost you can compare and cut.

What does a 3% surcharge save a practice?

A 3% surcharge brings back 3% of your credit card payments only. On $40,000 a month of card payments, you'd collect $1,200 a month if every patient paid by credit card. You can't surcharge debit or HSA cards, so if 30% of your card payments come on those, you'll collect $840.

Example 2: what a 3% surcharge brings back. The same dental practice takes $40,000 a month in card payments: $28,000 on credit cards and $12,000 on debit and HSA cards. Its average credit card rate is 3%, so it can surcharge the full 3%. No patient switches how they pay.

Monthly card payments Surcharge collected a month A year
Credit cards $28,000 $840 $10,080
Debit and HSA cards $12,000 $0 $0
Total $40,000 $840 $10,080

The practice also pays its processor's 3% on the surcharge itself, so it keeps about $815 of the $840 a month, or about $9,780 of the $10,080 a year.

This is an example with round numbers, not a typical result. The practice still pays the $300 a month in debit and HSA card fees from Example 1. If some patients switch to debit to avoid the fee, the practice collects less again. For the other places your cash goes, see why a practice can be profitable but short on cash.

What to ask your processor before you add a fee

  • What's my average credit card rate over the last 12 months?
  • Will my terminal skip the surcharge on debit, prepaid and HSA cards?
  • Do you deposit my payments in full, or with fees taken out?
  • What do I need to send you in writing before I start?

Questions practice owners ask about credit card fees

This post is general information, not legal advice. Card network rules and state laws change, so check them with your processor and a lawyer before you add a fee.

Can a medical practice charge patients a credit card fee?

Yes, in most states, as a surcharge on credit cards. Visa requires 30 days' written notice to your processor, signs at your entrance and checkout, and the fee on the receipt. Connecticut, Massachusetts and Maine ban surcharges. Without a surcharge, your practice pays the whole fee.

What's the difference between a surcharge and a convenience fee?

A surcharge is a percentage you add because a patient paid by credit card. A convenience fee is a flat amount you charge for paying through a channel you don't usually offer, such as an online portal when you usually take payment at the desk. Visa requires the same convenience fee for every way to pay in that channel, cards or not.

Can I surcharge debit cards or HSA cards?

No. Visa doesn't allow a surcharge on debit or prepaid cards, and issuers put HSA cards on prepaid or debit card numbers. That's true even when the patient chooses "credit" on the keypad. If your terminal can't tell debit from credit, don't surcharge until your processor fixes it, or you'll charge patients a fee you can't charge.

What do practices usually pay in card processing fees?

It depends on your processor, your pricing plan and how many patients use credit versus debit. To find your own number, add every fee on one month's statement and divide by that month's card payments. In Example 1 the result is 2.85%. Compare that number across three months before you call your processor about a lower rate.

How should card fees show in my books?

Record the full payment as revenue and the fees as a separate expense, even when your processor deposits the net amount. If you collect a surcharge, give it its own line too. Ask your CPA whether surcharges count in your taxable sales if your state taxes any of what you sell.

Can I offer a cash discount instead?

Yes. Federal law stops card issuers from blocking a discount for paying by cash or check, and Connecticut, Massachusetts and Maine allow one too. Offer it to every patient and post it clearly; Connecticut requires a posted notice. Charging everyone a higher price and calling it a discount may count as a surcharge, so ask a lawyer how to set it up.
