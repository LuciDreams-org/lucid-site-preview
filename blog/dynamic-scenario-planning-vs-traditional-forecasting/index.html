Dynamic Scenario Planning vs. Forecasting for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice’s baseline forecast and its scenarios answer different questions. The baseline estimates expected financial performance. Scenarios test whether a decision still works under different conditions.

For an owner considering a lease renewal, the baseline might reflect expected payer receipts, patient collections, provider and administrative payroll, and current occupancy costs. Scenarios can test the proposed renewal alongside a delayed provider start, higher staffing costs, or slower collections—without turning every possibility into the official operating plan.

Neither method is inherently more accurate or universally better. The useful choice is how much planning a decision requires, who maintains it, and how the results will inform approval.

What Each Planning Method Does

A baseline forecast is the practice’s current best estimate of what will happen. It is not necessarily the annual budget: the budget may remain an approved spending target while the forecast changes to reflect expected results.

Dynamic scenario planning examines several plausible combinations of conditions and management responses. “Dynamic” means the cases remain available for review and decision-making as relevant conditions develop. It does not require constant recalculation after every payment or schedule change.

Dimension Baseline forecasting Dynamic scenario planning
Main question What do we currently expect? What would we do if conditions differ?
Purpose Coordinate expected operations and cash needs Evaluate choices and prepare conditional responses
Assumptions One approved set of expected assumptions Several explicitly different, internally consistent sets
Practice inputs Expected receipts, payroll, occupancy, shared costs, and other obligations Alternative collection timing, staffing availability, costs, and spending choices
Typical output Expected P&L and cash position over the planning period Comparison of decision options, cash exposure, and response conditions
Update cadence A regular cycle tied to reliable financial reporting Scheduled decision reviews and material trigger events
Governance Finance maintains the forecast; management approves key assumptions Management approves cases, decision criteria, and authority to act
Workload Recurring reporting and variance review Additional analysis concentrated on consequential decisions
Main limitation One expected path cannot describe every possibility Too many cases can consume time without improving a decision

Both can use the same financial model and underlying records. The distinction is the purpose and approval status of each output, not the software used.

Choose the Method Based on the Decision

When a baseline may be enough

Routine operations generally need a maintained baseline rather than a new scenario exercise for every expense. A manager reviewing ordinary supply purchases within an approved budget may only need to confirm expected cash availability and spending authority.

The baseline also provides a consistent reference for monthly results. If actual payroll exceeds expectations, management can investigate overtime, vacancies filled earlier than planned, or cost allocations without immediately creating several new scenarios.

When scenarios add useful information

Scenarios are more useful when a decision is difficult to reverse, uncertainty is material, and different conditions would lead to different actions. Examples include:

  • Renewing a lease or adding space.
  • Hiring a provider before the expected collection contribution is established.
  • Adding administrative coverage across locations.
  • Purchasing equipment while collection timing is uncertain.
  • Committing one entity’s cash when another entity bears the operating cost.

A simple test is: Could a plausible change in staffing, collections, or costs change the approval decision? If yes, a focused scenario comparison may be worthwhile.

Do not create a large scenario library simply because the model allows it. A small number of distinct cases is usually easier to interpret than many slightly different versions of the same assumption.

Coordinate Both Methods on One Planning Calendar

The baseline needs a dependable reporting rhythm. Scenarios need a schedule that matches the decision deadline. Those schedules should connect, but they need not be identical.

The following is an illustrative planning calendar, not a required reporting timetable.

Timing Baseline forecast work Scenario planning work Responsible participants
Annual planning cycle Establish the expected operating plan and distinguish it from the approved budget Identify major commitments requiring conditional analysis Owner, practice manager, finance lead
Monthly, after close Compare actual results with expectations and approve material assumption changes Check whether active decision cases remain relevant; avoid rebuilding unaffected cases Finance lead and operational input owners
Quarterly Reassess the broader outlook and location/entity assumptions Retire outdated cases and prioritize upcoming decisions Owner, manager, finance lead
Before a major commitment Confirm the current baseline version and cash available for the decision period Compare alternatives under selected staffing, collection, and cost conditions Decision owner, finance, relevant advisers
At a defined trigger Assess whether new evidence changes the expected outlook Review the affected case and proposed response Assigned trigger owner and approver
After approval Incorporate the approved commitment into the next baseline release Preserve the decision comparison and monitor unresolved conditions Finance lead and decision owner

A trigger should be meaningful enough to warrant attention: a provider start date becomes uncertain, proposed lease terms change, or a sustained collection delay threatens the practice’s chosen cash floor. An individual late payment does not automatically justify a new planning cycle.

Example: Evaluate a Scheduled Lease Renewal Without Losing the Baseline

Illustrative example: all amounts, timing, and conditions below are invented to demonstrate the planning process.

A staffed private practice must decide on a lease renewal before its current term expires. The owner is also considering an additional administrative role. Collections could arrive later than expected, and a provider vacancy remains a separate possibility.

The practice keeps its existing baseline clearly labeled. That version reflects expected operations, current occupancy costs, and existing staffing. It has not yet incorporated the proposed renewal increase or the optional hire. It is a reference for comparison—not a complete post-expiration occupancy plan.

The practice then evaluates the proposed renewal over the six months following its effective date. Its finance lead uses these illustrative inputs:

  • Opening operating cash: $240,000.
  • Baseline cash receipts over six months: $1,800,000.
  • Baseline cash payments over six months: $1,740,000, including current occupancy costs.
  • Additional renewal payments over the comparison period: $18,000.
  • Additional administrative-role payments, if approved: $60,000 over the same period.

Baseline ending cash is therefore $300,000 before the proposed changes. The comparison isolates the renewal and staffing decisions while showing how different collection conditions affect them.

The receipt delay is a timing assumption, not an assumed permanent loss. The vacancy case links lower payroll payments with lower collections rather than treating a vacancy only as a cost saving. Any later-period recovery or continuing effect belongs in the remaining planning horizon.

Use the comparison to make the decision

The ending balances are a screening tool, not sufficient approval evidence. Finance must also check the monthly cash path: payroll and lease payments could create a low point before receipts arrive. The review should extend far enough to examine the remaining lease commitment and any later collection recovery, rather than relying only on the six-month illustration.

Suppose the owner selects an illustrative minimum operating cash level of $150,000 based on the practice’s obligations. The delayed-receipts case ends below that level, so approving both commitments without further review would conflict with that decision criterion. Other cases still require a check of their lowest monthly balance.

Possible responses include evaluating renewal without the added role, staging the hire, or discussing different lease terms. None is automatically the right choice. A hiring delay also needs operational review; an apparent cash saving may create coverage problems or undermine collections.

The stronger-receipts case does not become the baseline merely because it makes the commitment easier to approve. Once management approves a decision, finance issues a new baseline incorporating that commitment and the current expected assumptions. The earlier baseline and scenario comparison remain separately identifiable.

Set Governance Before Debating the Numbers

Planning becomes confusing when different people edit assumptions without agreeing on what each version represents.

Use a short decision record that identifies:

  • The decision and deadline: what requires approval and when.
  • The baseline version: the dated forecast used as the comparison point.
  • Assumption owners: who confirms staffing dates, collection conditions, lease terms, and shared costs.
  • Approval criteria: cash limits, operational requirements, and acceptable exposure.
  • Decision authority: who may approve the commitment or authorize a conditional response.
  • Review conditions: what evidence requires another discussion.

For a multi-location practice, review both the affected location’s P&L and the cash impact on the entity signing the lease. A location can appear profitable while its entity has other cash obligations. Shared administrative costs should be allocated consistently across the baseline and scenarios so the comparison does not change merely because the allocation method changed.

Keep the Workload Proportionate

Start from reconciled financial records rather than maintaining separate, disconnected sets of numbers. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide a sound foundation; they do not make uncertain assumptions certain.

Reuse the same expense categories and timing conventions across cases. Show which assumptions changed and leave unaffected inputs alone. Compare the outputs that matter to the decision: lowest projected cash, expected operating results, committed payments, and the feasibility of any proposed response.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. Payer-level receipts, total patient collections, and role-level payroll assumptions can often support this analysis without patient-identifiable details. Ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools should not be used to share patient-identifiable information for planning.

Conclusion: One Expected Outlook, Several Conditional Choices

A baseline forecast coordinates what the practice currently expects. Scenarios help owners decide whether a commitment remains acceptable under other plausible conditions.

Keep the baseline separately identified, concentrate scenario work on consequential decisions, and agree on review dates and approval authority. The goal is not to produce the most versions. It is to make the decision, its financial exposure, and its conditions clear.

FAQs

Is dynamic scenario planning more accurate than forecasting?

Not inherently. A forecast estimates an expected outcome; scenarios describe conditional outcomes. Their usefulness depends on the assumptions, records, and decision process. A scenario can reveal exposure without being a prediction of what will happen.

Should every scenario become part of the baseline forecast?

No. Keep alternative conditions separate until management determines that an assumption represents the expected outlook or approves a commitment. Document the reason for a baseline change so an optimistic case does not silently replace the expected plan.

How often should a medical practice review scenarios?

Match reviews to the decision calendar and material triggers. A lease decision may need focused reviews before its approval deadline, while a standing staffing contingency may be reviewed during regular management meetings. Routine data changes do not require rebuilding every case.

What if the cash result supports a decision but staffing conditions do not?

Cash affordability is only one approval criterion. The owner and practice manager should assess coverage, workload, and implementation feasibility alongside the financial comparison. Revise or reject an option that depends on staffing conditions the practice cannot reasonably deliver.
