Cross-Border Payment Encryption for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A U.S. private medical practice may need to pay an overseas equipment supplier, office-furnishings vendor, or other business partner. Before sending money, the owner needs to understand how the payment provider protects financial information—and what that protection does not cover.

The useful question is not simply, “Does this provider use AES-256?” It is: What information is protected, where does that protection begin and end, who can decrypt it, and what evidence supports the answer?

Encryption helps protect information from unauthorized reading. It does not establish that an invoice is legitimate, confirm that a vendor’s bank details are correct, or decide who may release a payment. Those require separate controls.

This guide explains TLS and AES at an owner-appropriate level and provides a questionnaire for evaluating a provider. It is not a cryptographic design guide or a compliance certification checklist. Have qualified technical specialists assess implementation details, and route cross-border legal questions to appropriate counsel.

Start With the Payment Workflow, Not an Algorithm Ranking

Before requesting security documents, identify the payment route your practice will actually use. A provider’s mobile app, browser portal, file-upload service, and bank connections may have different protection boundaries.

Write down:

  • Which practice entity owns the vendor obligation and funds the payment.
  • Which employee prepares the transaction and who approves it.
  • What information is entered, uploaded, downloaded, or retained.
  • Which provider, banks, and intermediaries receive the information, to the extent known.
  • Where payment confirmations and reconciliation records will be stored.

Keep patient information out of an ordinary vendor-payment workflow. A furnishings payment generally needs business contact details, an invoice reference, beneficiary details, amount, and currency—not patient names, account histories, or clinical records.

If a proposed workflow would involve protected health information, obtain a separate privacy and security review. HHS explains that business-associate responsibilities can arise when service providers handle PHI. A payment provider’s encryption claim does not resolve those responsibilities.

TLS and AES: Two Different Protection Questions

TLS protects a connection while information travels

Transport Layer Security, or TLS, protects information moving over a network connection. HTTPS uses TLS to protect communication between a browser and a website.

Properly configured TLS provides confidentiality and integrity for that connection and normally authenticates the server through certificates. It does not, by itself, authenticate the employee approving a payment or validate the commercial purpose of an invoice.

The boundary matters. A protected connection from your browser to the payment provider does not prove that every later connection—from the provider to an intermediary or recipient bank—has equivalent protection. Information may be decrypted and processed between connections.

Ask for documented protocol support, including where TLS 1.2 or TLS 1.3 is used, whether older protocols remain enabled, and whether any exceptions affect your payment route. A technical reviewer should assess versions, configurations, certificate handling, and exceptions rather than relying on a version label alone.

AES can protect stored information

The Advanced Encryption Standard, or AES, is an encryption algorithm used to protect data, including stored information. “AES-256” identifies AES with a particular key length; it does not describe a complete security system.

A provider might encrypt storage disks, databases, selected fields, or backups. These are different protection layers. Storage encryption may protect against exposure of underlying storage, but it does not necessarily prevent an authorized application or compromised user account from reading records.

Ask which records and storage locations are covered. Payment databases, backups, attachments, logs, and exported files may not all receive the same treatment.

Also ask who controls the keys needed to decrypt the information. Strong encryption provides limited protection if the wrong person can obtain the keys or use an application that decrypts records for them.

Practice-owner question Relevant concept What the answer does not establish
Can someone read payment details while they travel to the provider? TLS and other transport protections That the invoice or beneficiary is legitimate
Are stored payment records protected? Encryption at rest, potentially using AES That all staff, applications, or support personnel are blocked from reading them
Who can decrypt records? Key management and access controls That access is appropriate without reviewing permissions and oversight
Is protection continuous across intermediaries? Documented encryption boundaries That a portal’s HTTPS connection covers the entire payment chain
Are downloaded confirmations protected? Practice-controlled storage and device safeguards That provider-side encryption follows a file after download

Separate Encryption From Other Payment Controls

Encryption belongs in a layered payment-control process. It cannot replace the other layers.

Control Main purpose Medical-practice application
Encryption Protect information from unauthorized reading Protect beneficiary details in transit and stored payment records
Authentication Establish the identity of a user or system Require individual accounts and appropriate multifactor authentication
Access controls Limit what an authenticated user can do Let an administrator prepare payments without granting unrestricted release authority
Payment authorization Approve a specific movement of funds Have an authorized owner or manager approve the vendor, amount, currency, and funding entity
Fraud prevention Detect or prevent deceptive transactions Independently verify changed bank instructions using a previously trusted contact method
Reconciliation Confirm what actually happened financially Match the invoice, bank debit, fees, conversion details, and payment status

A fraudster can send false bank instructions through an encrypted channel. A compromised employee account may also submit an unauthorized payment through a securely encrypted portal. In both cases, encryption can work as designed while the practice still loses money.

When staffing permits, separate payment preparation from approval. Where that separation is difficult, establish an explicit owner review rather than treating portal access as blanket permission to pay.

Provider Questionnaire: Request Answers and Evidence

Send this template to the provider before onboarding or materially changing an overseas payment route. Ask for written answers tied to the service your practice will use—not just the provider’s general security page.

For each answer, record the supporting document, its date, any exceptions, and the person responsible for follow-up. Do not request actual encryption keys, passwords, or confidential system secrets.

A useful response might explain that storage keys are managed by a hosting provider while the payment company controls application access. That answer is more informative than “military-grade encryption,” but it still needs review in context.

How to Evaluate the Evidence

Check scope before accepting a badge

An independent report is useful only to the extent it addresses the service, systems, and period relevant to your practice. Ask whether the portal you will use is included and whether material processing partners are covered or excluded.

Different documents answer different questions. An assurance report may assess specified controls over a period. A penetration-test summary may address technical testing within a limited scope. Neither automatically proves that every transfer is encrypted correctly or that fraud cannot occur.

Give detailed evidence to a qualified IT or security reviewer. The owner’s role is to ensure that unanswered questions and significant exceptions receive a documented decision—not to personally validate cryptographic settings.

Identify the gaps your practice owns

Provider encryption may not protect a confirmation downloaded to an unmanaged laptop, copied into an email, or retained in an unrestricted shared folder. Review where staff store records and who can access them.

Use only the information needed for financial work. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack conversations or a general-purpose AI tool. If PHI becomes relevant, seek guidance on the applicable workflow; HHS’s minimum necessary guidance includes important exceptions and should not be reduced to a universal rule.

Make a documented decision

Classify each unresolved item as acceptable with an explanation, requiring remediation, or requiring specialist review before use. Missing evidence is not proof of insecurity, but it is not proof of adequate protection either.

If the provider cannot explain a material protection boundary or key-management responsibility, pause onboarding or the affected payment route until the issue is resolved. Do not substitute an unreviewed email or file-sharing workaround simply to meet an invoice deadline.

Hypothetical Example: An Overseas Furnishings Payment

Illustrative scenario: A two-location private medical practice buys waiting-room furniture from an overseas supplier. The invoice contains no patient information.

The practice manager prepares the payment from the entity responsible for the purchase. Before release, the owner confirms the invoice, currency, beneficiary, and funding account. Any changed bank instructions are verified through an established contact method, not a phone number supplied only in the change request.

The provider supplies documentation covering its browser connection and stored payment records, but its description of a downstream transfer is unclear. The practice’s technical reviewer requests clarification. The manager separately records the provider’s urgent payment-support and security contacts.

After the review is resolved and the payment is approved, the bookkeeper retains the invoice, approval, confirmation, bank debit, fees, and available foreign-exchange details. The accounting team records the transaction to the appropriate entity and location, with any shared-cost allocation documented separately.

This final step is financial control, not encryption. IRS Publication 583 supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. A secure transmission does not establish that the books reflect the payment correctly.

Assign Responsibilities Before the Next Payment

The owner or designated manager should approve the provider and payment authority. An IT or security specialist should assess technical evidence. Legal counsel should address applicable cross-border legal obligations. The CPA or bookkeeping team should handle accounting records and reconciliation.

FAQs

Is AES-256 enough to approve a payment provider?

No. The label does not explain which records are encrypted, who can decrypt them, how keys are managed, or whether exported copies remain protected. Evaluate the documented implementation and its boundaries alongside access, authorization, and fraud controls.

Does HTTPS mean an overseas payment is encrypted all the way to the recipient bank?

No. HTTPS protects the connection to the website you are using. Later transfers may involve separate systems and connections. Ask the provider to explain protection at each relevant handoff and identify where information is decrypted for processing.

Can encryption prevent payment to a fraudulent bank account?

Not by itself. Encryption can protect an instruction containing the wrong bank details. Independently verify beneficiary changes, restrict payment permissions, and require appropriate transaction approval.

Does encryption establish HIPAA compliance?

No. HHS explains in its cloud-computing guidance that a cloud service maintaining ePHI can have business-associate responsibilities even without the decryption key. Encryption alone does not settle applicable agreements or safeguards. Keep patient information out of ordinary vendor payments and obtain qualified review if PHI is involved.
