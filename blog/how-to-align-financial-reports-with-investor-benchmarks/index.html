Aligning Medical Practice Investor Reports With Agreed Benchmarks

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An investor asks for the practice’s EBITDA margin against an agreed target. The practice manager sends a report based on collections, while the investor expects accrual revenue. One calculation includes owner-provider compensation; another substitutes an agreed compensation expense. Both carry the same label, but they do not measure the same result.

For a staffed private medical practice, benchmark alignment starts with agreement on definitions—not adjustments designed to reach a target. Before distributing reports, owners and their finance team need to establish which period, entities, accounting basis, compensation treatment, and adjustments apply to both actual results and the comparison figure.

This guide addresses that reporting workflow. It is not a guide to selecting clinical or operational benchmarks, determining a practice’s value, or establishing ownership arrangements. The goal is a report investors can trace, understand, and compare consistently.

Start With the Investor’s Reporting Request

Ask the investor for the calculation behind each requested measure, not just its name. “Profit margin,” “provider compensation,” and “practice revenue” can mean different things across reporting packages.

Obtain the reporting requirements in the applicable agreement, approved budget, or written reporting instructions. Then identify any unresolved definitions before preparing the comparison. If a formal agreement governs the calculation, a management preference should not silently replace it.

For each requested measure, confirm:

  • The formula, including the denominator for any margin or ratio.
  • Whether the comparison is an internally approved target, a prior-period result, or an external comparator.
  • The period and reporting deadline.
  • The included legal entities and locations.
  • The treatment of owner compensation, shared costs, and adjustments.
  • Who can approve a definition or a subsequent change.

A useful opening question is: “Could someone reproduce this measure from our financial statements and the attached reconciliation?” If not, the definition needs more work.

Build a Benchmark-Definition Crosswalk

A crosswalk connects the investor’s requested measure to the practice’s accounting records. Maintain it as a controlled reference alongside the reporting package.

The following is a suggested structure, not an industry standard. Complete the final column with the practice’s actual agreements.

Measure and formula What does the metric include? Exact numerator, denominator, account mapping, and exclusions
Reporting period Is the request monthly, quarterly, year-to-date, or trailing twelve months? Start and end dates; whether prior periods are comparable

Resolve cash versus accrual before comparing margins

Payer receipts are not automatically the same as revenue recognized for the reporting period. Collections may include payment for earlier services, while current-period patient balances may remain unpaid.

A bank reconciliation supports cash accuracy; it does not establish accrual revenue. An accrual-based report also needs support for receivables, applicable revenue adjustments, unpaid expenses, and payroll accruals.

Do not compare cash-basis actuals with an accrual-based target under a single unlabeled heading. If the practice needs both views, present them separately and reconcile material differences. If reliable accrual information is unavailable, disclose that limitation rather than implying the conversion is complete.

Define owner compensation separately from distributions

Owner-provider compensation can materially affect reported earnings. Specify whether the measure includes actual compensation expense or substitutes a documented amount for the owner’s work.

If a substitution is permitted, explain what it covers: clinical work, management duties, employer payroll costs, benefits, or another agreed set of components. An agreed replacement amount is not automatically evidence of market compensation.

Owner distributions are generally equity transactions, not compensation expense on the P&L. Do not add them back to earnings simply because cash left the practice. Have the CPA review unusual owner-related classifications before reporting.

Keep location and entity boundaries consistent

A location P&L may exclude expenses carried centrally, such as administrative payroll, accounting, or shared technology costs. Comparing that location’s margin with a fully burdened practice-wide target would be misleading.

For multi-entity reporting, document how intercompany charges and transfers are handled. A management fee may be an expense in one entity and income in another; combining both without appropriate eliminations can distort the group presentation.

Use Targets That Match the Definition

An internally approved budget is often a useful comparison because the practice and investor can agree on its underlying assumptions. Preserve the approved version and calculate actual results using the same definition.

External comparators require more caution. Before using one, record its source, reporting period, population, and methodology. Check whether it matches the practice’s specialty, entity scope, accounting basis, and owner-compensation treatment.

If the published methodology is insufficient, label the comparator as limited or omit the numerical comparison. Do not fill gaps with an assumed “industry norm.”

Changing a definition can change the reported outcome without changing the practice’s underlying performance. That is why the definition should be agreed before results are evaluated.

Worked Reconciliation: One Practice, Two Documented Definitions

Illustrative example only. All amounts, compensation assumptions, and targets below are invented to demonstrate the reporting method. They are not industry benchmarks or evidence of market compensation.

Assume a practice reports a quarter on an accrual basis across the same two locations. Its ledger shows:

Ledger-based starting point Illustrative amount
Net revenue $1,000,000
Net income $85,000
Interest expense $10,000
Depreciation and amortization $25,000
Income tax expense in this example $0
Unadjusted EBITDA $120,000

For this example, EBITDA is net income plus interest, income tax expense, depreciation, and amortization. It does not automatically exclude one-time costs or owner compensation. Those require separately defined adjustments.

Two documented reporting definitions produce different results:

  • Definition A—unadjusted EBITDA margin: Retains actual owner compensation and all expenses included in EBITDA.
  • Definition B—adjusted EBITDA margin: Replaces $180,000 of recorded owner compensation expense with an agreed $160,000 expense and excludes a documented $20,000 location-opening consulting charge. Both definitions use the same revenue, period, and entity scope.

The compensation amounts cover the same expense components. The consulting charge is assumed to be separately identifiable and eligible under the agreed adjustment policy; calling an expense “one-time” would not be sufficient by itself.

Reconciliation Definition A Definition B
Unadjusted EBITDA $120,000 $120,000
Add back recorded owner compensation — $180,000
Deduct agreed replacement compensation — ($160,000)
Add back approved location-opening consulting expense — $20,000
Result under the documented definition $120,000 $160,000
Revenue denominator $1,000,000 $1,000,000
Resulting margin 12% 16%

The $40,000 difference is a definition bridge, not an improvement in collections, staffing efficiency, or cash availability.

Suppose the internally agreed target in this illustrative example is 14%. Definition A is two percentage points below that target; Definition B is two percentage points above it. Only the definition attached to the approved target determines the valid comparison. Switching definitions after seeing the result would undermine the report.

Present the unadjusted result and the adjustment bridge together. Adjusted EBITDA should not be presented as cash available for distributions: debt principal payments, capital spending, and working-capital needs can still consume cash.

Run a Pre-Distribution Review

A repeatable review is more useful than rebuilding definitions every reporting cycle.

1. Close and reconcile the underlying books

Reconcile bank accounts, review payer receipts and patient collections in aggregate, confirm provider and administrative payroll, and check period-end entries relevant to the reporting basis. Resolve unexplained balances before preparing the investor comparison.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those practices provide a foundation for reporting; they do not, by themselves, validate an adjusted earnings measure.

2. Tie each measure to supporting schedules

Keep a schedule connecting the report to the final P&L, balance sheet, and relevant ledger accounts. For shared costs, retain the allocation calculation. For adjustments, retain the expense detail and approval.

Avoid unsupported spreadsheet overrides. A reviewer should be able to follow each material number back to its source without relying on the preparer’s memory.

3. Review adjustments consistently

Apply the agreed policy to unfavorable and favorable items. Do not exclude an unusual expense while leaving an equivalent unusual gain unexamined.

Ask whether an expense described as exceptional has appeared repeatedly. Continuing recruiting, routine repairs, or recurring implementation work should not be excluded merely because management would prefer a higher margin. Any permitted adjustment needs a clear rationale and consistent treatment.

4. Check the target and comparison columns

Confirm that actuals, budget, and prior-period figures use compatible periods and definitions. If a new location changes the scope, distinguish total-practice performance from any separately defined same-location comparison.

Separate business drivers from definition effects in the commentary. A compensation adjustment does not explain an operational improvement; a change in payer receipt timing does not necessarily establish revenue growth.

5. Approve and preserve the issued package

Assign preparation and review responsibilities. Record the definition version and approval before distribution. Preserve the issued report, reconciliation, and supporting schedules together.

If a material error is found later, issue a clearly identified correction with an explanation of what changed. Do not silently replace a previously distributed report.

Report Footnote Template

Use a footnote that makes the calculation understandable without requiring the investor to reconstruct it from email correspondence:

Basis and scope: Results cover [start date–end date] for [included entities and locations] on a [cash/accrual] basis. [Describe exclusions and intercompany treatment.]
Measure: [Metric] is calculated as [formula], using [revenue or other denominator definition].
Compensation and allocations: Owner compensation is treated as [actual expense/agreed substitution], covering [components]. Shared costs are allocated using [method].
Adjustments: Adjustments total [amount] and comprise [itemized amounts and reasons]. The reconciliation begins with [ledger-based result].
Comparison: The target is from [approved budget/version or external source and period] and uses [definition]. [Describe comparability limitations.]
Governance: Definition version [identifier], effective [period], approved by [role/date]. Changes from the prior report are [none/details]. [Identify estimates, unresolved items, and any review or assurance limitations.]

Keep Reporting Definitions Stable—and Changes Visible

Review definitions when an investor request changes, a location opens or closes, an entity is added, or the accounting basis changes. Document the reason and effective period. Recalculate comparative periods where practical; otherwise, identify the break in comparability.

FAQs

What if an investor requests a different profit definition from the practice’s P&L?

Keep the P&L intact and prepare a separate reconciliation to the requested measure. Document the formula, scope, compensation treatment, and adjustments. If the request conflicts with an existing reporting agreement, resolve that conflict before presenting it as the official comparison.

Can a practice report adjusted EBITDA above its agreed target?

Yes, if the approved target uses the same adjusted definition and the calculation is supported. Show the unadjusted result and the adjustment bridge alongside it. Do not switch from unadjusted to adjusted EBITDA after seeing that the original measure missed the target.

What should an owner do when benchmark definitions cannot be reconciled?

Present separately labeled measures and explain the specific differences or missing information. If a reliable comparison is not possible, say so. An explicit limitation is more useful than a precise-looking variance calculated from incompatible figures.
