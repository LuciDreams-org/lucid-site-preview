What Established Medical Practice Investors Need in Exit Plans

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An established medical practice’s exit plan should do more than describe a possible sale or ownership transition. It should give existing capital partners a supported account of what the practice earns, what it owes, how dependent it is on departing owners, and whether its management team can sustain operations through a transition.

For owners and managers of staffed US private practices, the central task is making the plan verifiable. Every important statement should connect to a record, a responsible person and any work still needed. A polished presentation cannot substitute for reconciled accounts, documented obligations or an executable management handoff.

This article explains how to assemble that evidence. It does not establish a practice valuation, recommend transaction terms or determine whether a particular ownership structure is legally permitted. Those questions belong with qualified valuation, transaction, tax and healthcare legal advisers.

Define Exactly What the Exit Plan Covers

Start with a scope sheet identifying the proposed sale or ownership transition under review. Distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions that advisers still need to evaluate.

For a multi-location practice, the operating footprint and legal entity structure may not match. One entity might operate several offices, while a separate owner-controlled entity holds real estate. A plan that describes only the combined practice can obscure which assets, obligations and expenses belong where.

Document:

  • The entities and locations included in the analysis.
  • Ownership records to be reviewed by counsel.
  • Related entities providing space, staff, equipment or administrative services.
  • The reporting period and latest completed financial close.
  • The owner’s current clinical and administrative responsibilities.
  • Questions about transaction scope that remain unresolved.

Use this scope consistently across financial statements, debt schedules and staffing records. If something is excluded, explain how the practice depends on it and whether an ongoing arrangement would need review.

Substantiate Earnings Quality, Not Just Reported Profit

Existing investors need to understand whether reported earnings reflect ongoing operations. That requires explaining how revenue, expenses and cash receipts relate—not simply presenting a favorable profit figure.

Reconcile the financial foundation

Begin with closed monthly financial statements, supporting balance-sheet schedules and reconciled bank accounts. Identify the accounting basis and any changes in revenue recognition, expense classification or estimates that affect comparisons.

The IRS business recordkeeping guidance supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. These are useful foundations for exit preparation, but they do not establish that a practice has completed a formal quality-of-earnings review.

For a medical practice, the earnings explanation should address:

  • Payer receipts and patient collections, including unusual collection activity.
  • Accounts receivable aging, write-offs and credit balances, where applicable.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, benefits and accrued obligations.
  • Owner compensation, distributions and related-party expenses, kept distinct.
  • Significant equipment costs and the difference between capital purchases and operating expenses.
  • Shared costs allocated among locations or entities.

Cash-basis revenue may rise because older balances were collected, not because current-period activity improved. Accrual-basis revenue requires support for receivables and relevant estimates. Neither accounting basis eliminates the need to explain collection timing.

Support every proposed earnings adjustment

Do not treat an expense as removable merely because an owner approved it or because it occurred only once. Document what happened, why an adjustment is proposed, whether the cost could recur and what replacement spending may be necessary.

Illustrative example: A practice proposes adding back $80,000 of owner administrative compensation. The owner currently handles recruiting, payroll approval and vendor oversight. If replacement management would cost $60,000, the plan should show that continuing expense rather than implying that the entire $80,000 disappears. These figures are illustrative, not compensation benchmarks or a valuation recommendation.

Maintain a bridge from reported results to each proposed adjusted measure. Show both the adjustment and its supporting evidence. Ask the CPA or transaction adviser to assess the methodology; do not present management’s adjustments as buyer-accepted earnings.

Make location economics explainable

Provide location-level results when they are relevant to the proposed transition. Show direct expenses separately from shared administrative costs and document the allocation method.

A location that appears profitable before shared payroll, rent or central administration may tell a different story after allocation. Investors should be able to distinguish an operating change from a change in allocation policy.

Make Debt and Other Obligations Traceable

A balance-sheet debt total is only the starting point. Build an obligation schedule that ties recorded balances to supporting documents and identifies items requiring further review.

Obligation category Records to assemble Question the plan should answer
Bank and equipment debt Agreements, statements, payment schedules and ledger detail Do recorded balances reconcile, and what remains unconfirmed?
Owner or related-party loans Agreements, transfer records and account reconciliations Which amounts are loans rather than contributions or distributions?
Leases Executed leases, amendments and payment schedules Which locations or assets depend on continuing agreements?
Payroll and benefits Payroll registers, accrual schedules and supporting statements Are earned compensation and benefit obligations reflected consistently?
Taxes and unresolved notices Filed returns, payment records and adviser correspondence What requires CPA review before the plan is considered complete?
Other commitments Vendor agreements, guarantees and dispute records What obligations may not be obvious from the debt accounts?

Have counsel and relevant advisers review guarantees, security interests, consent provisions and possible transaction-related requirements. A management schedule should flag these issues, not declare that an obligation will transfer or that an owner will be released.

Keep practice operating cash needs separate from any discussion of sale proceeds. Payroll, rent and vendor payments continue while a potential transaction is being evaluated.

Demonstrate What Happens When the Owner Steps Back

Owner dependence has both an earnings component and an execution component. A practice may have experienced staff but still rely on one owner for critical decisions.

Separate clinical contribution from administrative work

Document the owner’s provider activity at an appropriately aggregated level, compensation structure, administrative duties and involvement in important business relationships. Do not assume that other providers can absorb the owner’s workload without changes in staffing, capacity or cost.

For each responsibility, identify a proposed successor or an unresolved staffing need. Label replacement-cost assumptions clearly and have the appropriate advisers review them. The financial plan should acknowledge a missing replacement rather than burying it in a general transition statement.

Test management continuity

An organization chart alone does not demonstrate that management can operate independently. Look for evidence that managers already perform the work: completed close checklists, reviewed payroll, documented purchasing authority and routine operating reports.

Responsibility Evidence of current capability Gap to resolve
Payroll preparation and approval Payroll calendar, review checklist and named backup Owner is still the only authorized approver
Monthly financial review Reconciliations, reporting package and review notes Manager cannot yet explain location variances
Vendor and facilities oversight Contract register and escalation responsibilities Critical renewal information is held only by the owner
Administrative staffing Coverage plan and assigned supervisory duties No backup exists for a key administrator
Collection oversight Aggregate aging reports and assigned follow-up review Only the owner investigates unusual receipt patterns

The goal is not to claim that the owner is unnecessary. It is to show which responsibilities can continue, what the handoff requires and which dependencies remain.

Build an Exit-Readiness Evidence Matrix

Use one matrix to connect investor questions to records, accountability and unresolved work. The following is a working template, not a representation that any practice has completed these tasks.

Can we rely on reported earnings? Monthly statements, bank reconciliations, general ledger and balance-sheet schedules Controller or external CPA Unreconciled accounts, missing periods or unsupported estimates
Are receipts consistent with the revenue explanation? Aggregate payer and patient receipt summaries, receivables aging and deposit reconciliations Finance lead with billing manager Unexplained collection spikes, aged balances or timing differences

Add a status, target completion date and evidence location to the working version. Useful statuses include supported, under review, missing evidence and adviser decision required. Avoid marking an item complete merely because someone uploaded a file.

Prepare for Buyer Questions Without Overstating Readiness

Before presenting the plan to capital partners, perform an internal evidence review. Ask someone other than the original preparer to trace selected statements back to their supporting records.

Use a repeatable response process

For each diligence question:

  1. Record the question and assign a response owner.
  2. Identify the supporting records and reporting period.
  3. Reconcile the answer to the current financial package.
  4. Obtain CPA, operational or legal review where appropriate.
  5. Record any limitation and retain the reviewed response.

If an answer changes, explain why and identify which schedules also need updating. This prevents one spreadsheet from showing a different debt balance or earnings measure than the investor presentation.

A preliminary answer can still be useful if its limitations are explicit. “Pending reconciliation” is more credible than a precise number with no support.

Minimize patient information in financial evidence

Use aggregate financial reports when they answer the question. Ordinary investor summaries generally do not need patient names or other patient-identifiable details.

When more detailed information is requested, have the practice’s privacy lead and advisers determine appropriate handling. HHS minimum necessary guidance describes the standard and its exceptions; requirements depend on the circumstances. Do not place patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack channels or general-purpose AI tools.

Present a Plan That Distinguishes Evidence From Assumptions

The final investor package should contain a concise scope statement, financial reporting package, earnings-adjustment bridge, obligation schedule, owner-dependence assessment, management continuity plan and evidence matrix.

Close with unresolved items and the decision each could affect. Missing replacement-management costs may limit the earnings analysis. An unreconciled owner loan may leave the obligation schedule incomplete. An undocumented related-party lease may require legal review before transaction scope is settled.

An evidence-based readiness decision—proceed with adviser review, complete specified work first, or pause preparation—is more useful than an unsupported closing date or promised outcome.

FAQs

Does a medical practice need a formal quality-of-earnings review before preparing an exit plan?

Not necessarily. Management can first organize reconciled financial statements, supporting schedules and an earnings-adjustment bridge. A CPA or transaction adviser can help determine whether a formal review is appropriate for the proposed process. Do not describe ordinary bookkeeping or management-prepared adjustments as an independent quality-of-earnings review.

How should owner compensation appear in the plan?

Separate compensation for clinical work, administrative responsibilities and ownership distributions. Explain which duties would continue after the transition and support any replacement-cost assumptions. Owner compensation should not automatically be treated as an expense that disappears.

What if important records are missing?

Identify the missing evidence, assign a responsible person and explain which conclusions remain unsupported. Reconstruct records where feasible and have the relevant adviser review the result. Disclose unresolved limitations rather than presenting an assumption as a confirmed fact.

What makes an exit plan credible to existing capital partners?

Credibility comes from consistency and traceability: the same scope, reporting periods and definitions across documents; records supporting material claims; named owners for follow-up; and a clear account of unresolved work. A credible plan does not guarantee a sale or a particular price. It makes the practice’s readiness and remaining risks assessable.
