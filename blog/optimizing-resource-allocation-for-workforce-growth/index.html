Allocating Resources for Medical Practice Workforce Growth

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Approving a new position is only the first financial decision. A private medical practice also needs to fund recruiting, equipment, onboarding, supervision, workspace, and payroll while the new employee becomes established.

Those resources often come from the same people and cash reserves that keep the practice running. A practice manager training two new employees may have less time for payer follow-up. Equipment purchases may compete with an already-approved office project. Payroll may begin before any related improvement in collections reaches the bank.

The owner's task is therefore specific: decide which resources to commit, when to commit them, and what must be ready before each approved hire starts.

This is not a guide to predicting headcount demand or optimizing daily schedules. It is an implementation and funding framework for hires already under consideration. Staffing qualifications, clinical supervision requirements, worker classification, and employment-law decisions belong with the appropriate clinical, HR, and legal professionals.

Start With a Resource Commitment Sheet

For each proposed hire, document the role, employing entity, work location, proposed start date, and person responsible for onboarding. Then identify the resources required before and after that date.

Use information supplied by your practice: compensation offers, benefit estimates, vendor quotes, payroll-provider estimates, workspace availability, and the manager's assessment of training time. Do not substitute a generic industry percentage for a cost you can obtain directly.

Separate the commitments into four categories:

Resource category Examples in a staffed medical practice Decision to make
One-time cash costs Recruiting charges, workstation purchases, initial training fees What must be purchased, and when is payment due?
Recurring cash costs Wages, employer payroll costs, benefits, software seats What ongoing monthly commitment will the practice assume?
Internal capacity Manager training time, provider orientation, administrative support Who can provide the time without leaving essential work uncovered?
Cash protection Funding for payroll and other obligations before expected receipts arrive How much cash must remain available after launch spending?

A salary approval does not resolve the other three categories. Keep them visible until the owner has approved the complete resource package.

Build a Fully Loaded Hiring-Resource Worksheet

Create one worksheet per hire, then combine the worksheets into a practice-wide cash plan. Include both the amount and its payment timing. A recruiting invoice due before the start date affects cash differently from an annual subscription paid several months later.

The following worksheet is a template, not a set of cost assumptions.

Keep three totals, not one

The worksheet should produce three different outputs:

  1. Launch cash: incremental payments needed to recruit, equip, and onboard the employee.
  2. Recurring cash commitment: ongoing compensation, employer costs, benefits, and other recurring expenses.
  3. Internal capacity commitment: training and supervision hours that existing staff must supply.

A fourth output—the required cash reserve—comes from the timing of those payments and expected receipts.

Do not combine everything into a single “cost per hire” and lose the distinctions. Existing manager wages may already be in the baseline budget, while overtime coverage is new spending. Cash spent on equipment may also receive different accounting treatment from its immediate cash-flow treatment; have the CPA determine the appropriate treatment.

Check Dependencies Before Releasing Funds

Some resources have to be ready before others are useful. Paying for a workstation does not solve a lack of trainer availability, and reserving payroll does not make an unfinished workspace usable.

Use a dependency checklist to identify the earliest feasible start date.

Dependency Evidence to obtain Budget consequence if unresolved
Workspace and equipment Confirmed delivery and setup dates Potential rescheduling or additional setup costs
Administrative access and orientation Completed internal readiness checklist Paid time may be consumed before normal work can begin
Training capacity Named trainer and agreed training hours Coverage spending or delayed existing work
Payroll and benefits setup Confirmation from responsible administrators Risk of rushed corrections and unplanned administrative work
Role-specific prerequisites Confirmation from appropriate HR or clinical leaders Start or work assignments may need adjustment
Funding approval Cash plan and owner authorization Commitments may exceed the available reserve

For provider hires, ask the responsible team to confirm any applicable enrollment or credentialing dependencies. Do not count expected payer receipts merely because a provider's payroll has started.

The purpose is not to make the finance team decide whether someone is qualified. It is to prevent financial commitments from moving ahead of operational readiness.

Compare Simultaneous and Staggered Starts

Starting employees together can consolidate orientation and make added capacity available sooner. Staggering starts can spread cash payments and reduce the peak demand on supervisors. Neither approach is automatically better.

Compare both using the same compensation, launch-cost, and receipt assumptions. Change only the start sequence unless you have a documented reason to change another input.

Illustrative example: two administrative hires

All figures below are invented for illustration, not practice benchmarks or a client case study. Assume a practice is considering two approved administrative hires with these inputs:

  • Each employee has a fully loaded recurring cash cost of $6,000 per month, including assumed wages, employer payroll costs, and benefits.
  • Recruiting and equipment cost $4,000 per employee.
  • Incremental paid training coverage costs $1,000 per employee.
  • Each hire requires 20 hours of existing supervisor time during the start month.
  • For this example, all launch costs are paid in the start month, and each employee starts at the beginning of that month.
  • The practice has $60,000 earmarked for hiring, after reserving cash for baseline obligations and its chosen minimum operating balance.
  • No incremental collections are credited to the hires during the comparison period.

Thus, each hire requires $5,000 in launch cash plus $6,000 in recurring monthly cash. Existing supervisor time is tracked separately because its wages are already in the baseline budget. The $1,000 coverage charge is additional spending and is included in launch cash.

Staggering requires $12,000 less cash through Month 3 because the second employee works two fewer months. It does not reduce the assumed recruiting or equipment costs, and it does not lower the eventual recurring monthly commitment.

The trade-off is two months without the second employee's contribution. The owner must compare that delayed benefit with the value of preserving cash and reducing the supervisor's peak training load.

If the practice assigns an illustrative internal value of $60 per supervisor hour, the 40 total training hours represent $2,400 of internal time under either plan. Do not add that amount to cash spending unless it creates additional pay or another actual cash consequence. Instead, identify what work the supervisor would otherwise perform and whether it needs coverage.

Fund Payroll Runway From Cash Timing

A positive practice P&L does not guarantee that cash will be available on the next payroll date. Payer receipts and patient payments may arrive later than the expenses associated with the work.

Build the hiring plan into the practice's broader cash forecast:

Projected ending cash = beginning available cash + expected receipts − baseline payments − incremental hiring payments.

Compare that ending balance with the owner's minimum operating cash requirement. Avoid counting the same reserve once as baseline protection and again as hiring funding.

For the illustrative example, neither three-month result proves long-term affordability. Both approaches eventually require $12,000 per month in additional recurring cash. The practice must extend the forecast far enough to assess how that commitment will be supported, including a downside case for slower receipts or extended onboarding.

Useful tests include:

  • What happens if anticipated payer receipts arrive later than assumed?
  • What if training coverage is needed longer than planned?
  • What existing commitments—rent, debt payments, supplies, or owner distributions—compete for the same cash?
  • Can the practice meet its minimum cash balance without relying on an unapproved financing arrangement?

Use specific practice assumptions rather than a universal number of months of runway. If additional borrowing or owner funding is needed, evaluate and approve it before treating it as available cash.

Allocate Costs to the Right Location and Entity

For a multi-location practice, show where each hire's costs belong. Charge directly attributable expenses to the relevant location. Allocate genuinely shared supervision, recruiting, or administrative costs using a documented basis that reflects how the resource is used.

For example, a shared trainer's time might be allocated using recorded onboarding hours. That does not mean every shared expense should use the same allocation method.

Keep the total practice view alongside location-level P&Ls. Moving a cost between locations does not reduce the practice's total spending.

For multi-entity owners, identify the employing entity, paying account, and any proposed shared-cost arrangements. Have the CPA review accounting treatment and the appropriate professionals review legal questions. Do not assume that a consolidated cash balance is freely available to every entity.

The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, keeping business accounts separate from personal accounts, and reconciling bank records. Reliable records make the hiring budget easier to compare with actual payments.

Approve in Phases and Review Actual Commitments

Use spending gates rather than releasing the entire budget without conditions:

  1. Before recruiting commitments: approve the resource worksheet, funding source, and responsible owner.
  2. Before confirming the start date: verify workspace, equipment, training capacity, and applicable prerequisites.
  3. Before launch purchases: confirm vendor amounts, payment dates, and remaining cash capacity.
  4. After the employee starts: compare actual payroll, launch spending, and training hours with the plan.
  5. Before the next start: update the forecast using actual spending and receipts, not the original estimate alone.

Track committed but unpaid costs as well as payments already made. Otherwise, the available budget can look larger than it really is.

Make the Start-Date Decision With the Full Commitment Visible

A workable hiring budget funds more than compensation. It identifies launch payments, recurring obligations, trainer capacity, dependencies, and the cash needed while the practice absorbs the change.

Choose simultaneous starts when the practice can support both the cash commitment and the onboarding workload. Choose staggered starts when sequencing protects implementation capacity or cash—and the cost of waiting is acceptable. In either case, document what has been approved, what remains conditional, and what must be reviewed before the next commitment.
