Debt and Owner Equity Scenarios for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Opening another location or adding providers creates two funding needs: paying for the expansion itself and keeping enough cash available while collections catch up. Choosing between debt and owner equity affects both needs—but in different ways.

Debt creates repayment obligations. Owner equity puts owners’ capital at risk and may change economic or decision-making rights. Neither option removes the underlying risk that a new location will cost more or collect more slowly than expected.

For a staffed private medical practice, the useful question is not simply which funding source looks cheaper. It is whether the proposed structure can support provider and administrative payroll, existing operations and expansion costs through a slower-than-planned ramp-up.

This article explains how to compare the cash and management consequences. It does not determine who may own a medical practice, interpret ownership agreements or recommend a particular investment. Have qualified advisers review the proposed structure and documents before making commitments.

What Debt and Owner Equity Mean for a Practice

A practice might finance an expansion with a business loan, additional capital from current owners, capital from an incoming owner, or a combination. These sources should be modeled separately because they create different obligations.

Funding source Immediate cash effect Ongoing cash obligation Management questions
Business loan Provides proceeds, potentially subject to fees and funding conditions Principal, interest and any other contractual charges Can the practice meet payments during a weak collections period? What restrictions or guarantees apply?
Additional capital from current owners Increases practice cash without creating loan debt No loan-style repayment schedule solely because it is equity; agreements may create other obligations Do contributions change ownership interests? How will future distributions and capital needs be handled?
Capital from an incoming owner Adds cash if paid into the practice Economic rights, distributions or future payments depend on the arrangement Which ownership, voting and exit rights change? Is the money going to the practice or an existing owner?
Loan from an owner Adds practice cash but remains debt if structured as a loan Repayment and interest depend on documented terms How does it rank against other obligations, and can the practice afford repayment?

An owner loan is not interchangeable with an equity contribution. Labeling both as “owner funding” can conceal repayment obligations and distort the comparison.

Also distinguish capital paid into the practice from money paid to an existing owner for an ownership interest. A transaction between owners may change ownership without supplying cash for equipment, payroll or working capital.

Separate Profitability From Cash Available for Repayment

A location can appear profitable while the practice struggles to make a loan payment. Accounting profit and available cash measure different things.

Loan proceeds are financing, not patient-service revenue. Principal repayments reduce cash and the loan balance; they are not operating expenses on the P&L. Interest is generally recorded as an expense, but its tax treatment requires separate review. Capital contributions and owner distributions also need to remain separate from operating revenue and expenses.

For financing decisions, review three views together:

  • Location P&L: Is the expansion producing an operating return after appropriate shared costs?
  • Cash forecast: When will payer receipts and patient collections arrive relative to payroll, rent and debt payments?
  • Balance sheet: How much debt, cash and owner capital supports the practice?

Do not select a financing option based only on a favorable P&L forecast. Build a cash schedule that includes principal payments, capital purchases and planned owner distributions.

Build the Expansion Budget Before Choosing the Funding Mix

Start with the project’s total cash requirement, not the amount a lender offers or an owner is willing to contribute.

Separate opening costs from the operating reserve

Opening costs may include buildout, equipment, deposits and pre-opening payroll. The operating reserve covers the period after opening when payroll and occupancy costs may run ahead of collections.

Budget timing matters. Equipment deposits may be due before financing is available. New provider capacity may not immediately translate into payer receipts. Patient balances may be collected later than the services that generated them.

Budget component What to establish Why it changes the funding decision
Buildout and equipment Payment milestones, delivery timing and contingency Large early outflows can exhaust funds before opening
Provider and administrative staffing Start dates and payroll commitments Payroll may begin before collections ramp up
Payer and patient receipts Expected collection timing, not just billed charges Revenue forecasts can overstate near-term cash availability
Shared support costs Incremental billing, management and other support costs Existing operations may subsidize the new location
Operating reserve Cash needed through the modeled low point A fully funded opening can still leave an underfunded ramp-up
Financing costs Fees, payment start dates and repayment terms Net usable proceeds may differ from the stated loan amount

Protect the existing practice

Identify how much cash the established location needs for its own payroll, vendors and other obligations. Do not treat its entire bank balance as available expansion capital.

If the plan assumes ongoing support from the existing location, show those transfers explicitly. Otherwise, an expansion may appear self-sufficient while quietly consuming the cash that supports current operations.

Illustrative Example: Three Funding Structures for the Same Expansion

All figures in this section are illustrative assumptions, not lending quotes, recommended reserves or ownership valuations.

Assume a practice needs $300,000: $180,000 for opening expenditures and $120,000 in operating cash remaining after those expenditures. Compare three funding structures for that same project.

Assumption Debt-heavy Blended funding Owner-equity funded
Loan proceeds $240,000 $120,000 $0
Owner equity contributed to the practice $60,000 $180,000 $300,000
Total funding $300,000 $300,000 $300,000
Opening expenditures $180,000 $180,000 $180,000
Operating cash after opening expenditures $120,000 $120,000 $120,000
Assumed monthly principal and interest payment $5,000 $2,500 $0

The payment amounts are modeling placeholders. An actual comparison must use proposed loan terms, including fees, payment timing, variable-rate provisions and any balloon payment.

Compare cash after debt service

Assume the expansion produces $9,000 of monthly operating cash before debt service once activity develops. In a downside month, assume it instead consumes $6,000 before debt service. These are also illustrative assumptions.

Monthly cash measure Debt-heavy Blended funding Owner-equity funded
Base operating cash before debt service $9,000 $9,000 $9,000
Base cash after debt service $4,000 $6,500 $9,000
Downside operating cash before debt service -$6,000 -$6,000 -$6,000
Downside cash after debt service -$11,000 -$8,500 -$6,000
Cash after one downside month, starting with $120,000 $109,000 $111,500 $114,000

This comparison excludes distributions and other unmodeled cash flows. It shows the immediate trade-off: debt preserves more owner cash at the outset but increases monthly cash demands. Equity reduces debt service but commits more owner money to the business.

The equity-funded case is not cost-free. Owners bear the risk of losing their contributed capital and may expect future distributions. If a new owner supplies the equity, the economic and management consequences depend on the negotiated arrangement.

Funding percentages are not ownership percentages

A project financed with a particular mix of debt and equity does not automatically produce the same split of ownership. Ownership depends on the existing practice’s value, the transaction structure and the agreed terms—not simply the percentage of expansion spending supplied by each source.

Keep the funding schedule and ownership schedule separate. Model cash contributions and loan balances in one; document economic and decision-making rights in the other.

Stress-Test the Obligations, Not Just the Opening Budget

Use the same operating assumptions across financing options so that differences reflect the funding structure rather than a more optimistic revenue forecast.

Test a base case, a slower-collections case and a delayed-opening case. If several setbacks could reasonably occur together, include a combined downside case rather than testing each in isolation.

Stress case Change to model Decision it helps answer
Payer receipts arrive later Shift collections into later periods Can the practice meet payroll and loan payments without emergency funding?
Provider starts later Delay receipts and adjust only costs that can actually be postponed How much cash is committed before productive capacity is available?
Buildout costs rise Increase opening expenditures Does the project still retain a workable operating reserve?
Existing-location collections soften Reduce cash available from established operations Can the expansion proceed without destabilizing the original location?
Variable borrowing costs rise Recalculate payments using a higher assumed rate How sensitive is the cash plan to financing terms?

For each case, record the lowest projected cash balance, when it occurs and the funding needed to restore the practice’s chosen reserve. Set review triggers before committing—for example, delaying discretionary purchases or reassessing hiring if collections fall behind the modeled schedule.

Do not assume owners can always make another contribution. Confirm both their willingness and their capacity to provide additional funds.

Compare Management Consequences Alongside Cash

Debt and equity create different responsibilities even when both fund the same project.

With debt, review the constraints

Keeping existing ownership does not necessarily mean unrestricted control. Loan documents may address collateral, guarantees, reporting, additional borrowing or distributions.

Ask advisers to identify the relevant obligations and explain what happens if the practice falls short. Include any required payments and restrictions in the decision summary rather than treating them as paperwork to review after selecting a lender.

With equity, clarify expectations

An equity contribution generally does not create a scheduled principal payment, but owners still need agreement about how the business will operate. Resolve questions such as:

  • Will additional contributions change economic or voting rights?
  • How will working-owner compensation differ from ownership distributions?
  • Who approves budgets, borrowing and expansion decisions?
  • How will future capital needs be addressed?
  • What process applies if an owner wants to leave?

These are issues for professional review, not assumptions to fill in with a generic template. The financial model should reflect the agreed arrangement without attempting to determine its legal validity.

Keep Location and Entity Reporting Clear

A multi-location practice needs to see whether the new site is improving or simply receiving support from established operations. A multi-entity group also needs to identify which entity receives financing, incurs costs and makes payments.

Maintain a financing schedule showing the borrower or contributing owner, recipient entity, outstanding balance and payment obligations. Record transfers between entities according to their actual purpose rather than treating every cash movement as revenue or expense.

Use a documented method for allocating shared administrative costs. Review both the new location’s performance and the consolidated cash position: a location P&L alone will not reveal all financing demands.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Reliable records are the foundation for comparing financing scenarios and checking actual results against the plan.

Turn the Comparison Into an Owner Decision

Prepare a concise decision summary supported by the detailed forecast. It should show:

  1. Total project funding and how much reaches the practice after financing costs.
  2. Owner cash required and whether it is debt or equity.
  3. Monthly debt service and any later lump-sum obligation.
  4. The lowest cash balance under each scenario.
  5. Proposed ownership, distribution and decision-making changes.
  6. Conditions that would cause the practice to pause or resize the expansion.

FAQs

Is contributing owner cash the same as lending money to the practice?

No. An equity contribution and an owner loan create different accounting and economic relationships. A loan has repayment terms; equity represents capital at risk in the business. Document the intended arrangement and have the books reflect it consistently.

Does equity financing mean giving up ownership?

Not necessarily. Existing owners may contribute additional equity without admitting anyone new. Whether ownership percentages or other rights change depends on the arrangement. Bringing in a new owner requires a separate evaluation of economic rights, management responsibilities and applicable requirements.

Can a profitable medical practice still struggle with debt payments?

Yes. Collections may arrive after expenses are incurred, and principal repayments use cash without reducing operating profit. Review a cash forecast alongside the P&L, including existing debt, capital purchases and distributions.

How much expansion reserve should a practice keep?

There is no single amount established by this comparison. Model the cash low point under realistic delays and cost overruns, then consider the obligations and uncertainty of both the expansion and existing operations. The reserve should follow that analysis rather than a generic benchmark.

Should a practice use a mix of debt and owner equity?

A blended structure is worth comparing, but it is not automatically preferable. It reduces borrowing relative to an all-debt approach while requiring less owner cash than an all-equity approach. Its suitability depends on repayment capacity, available owner funds, contractual terms and the owners’ agreed responsibilities.
