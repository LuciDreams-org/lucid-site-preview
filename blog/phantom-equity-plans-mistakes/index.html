5 Phantom Equity Plan Mistakes for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A phantom equity proposal can sound straightforward: give a practice administrator or employed physician a financial stake in the practice’s future value without issuing actual ownership. The difficult decisions sit underneath that promise. What value is being shared? Which entity owes the payment? What happens if the employee leaves—or the practice owes money before sale proceeds arrive?

For a staffed private medical practice, these are compensation-design and cash-planning decisions, not just document-drafting details. A future payout may compete with provider and administrative payroll, rent, debt service and operating reserves.

Before approving grants, owners should resolve five preventable gaps with legal, tax, payroll and accounting advisers. This article provides a review framework, not legal clauses or a determination of tax treatment.

1. Confusing Cash Participation With Ownership

A cash-settled phantom equity arrangement generally provides a contractual payment right tied to a defined measure of business value. It is not itself an issuance of shares or membership interests. Calling a recipient a “partner” or promising “a percentage of the practice” can obscure that distinction.

The decision at risk

The owner must decide what economic participation to offer without accidentally communicating a different bargain. A participant may interpret a percentage as a share of annual profits, sale proceeds or actual ownership, even when the proposed plan means something else.

For a multi-entity practice, the boundary matters even more. Participation linked to the operating practice does not necessarily include a separately owned building or another location held in a different entity.

Records to gather

  • Entity organization chart and current ownership records.
  • Existing employment agreements, bonus arrangements and offer letters.
  • Proposed participant communications, including emails describing the award.
  • A list of the entities and assets intended to be included or excluded.

Questions to resolve before approval

Ask counsel to distinguish the proposed payment right from actual ownership, governance rights and any separate information rights. Identify the entity making the promise and the entity expected to fund it.

Then test the explanation with plain language: What can the participant receive? What must happen first? What rights are not being granted? Counsel should also review applicable professional-practice restrictions rather than assuming that a cash-based arrangement avoids every ownership or compensation concern.

Approval checkpoint: The plan, grant documents and employee explanation describe the same economic benefit and entity scope.

2. Leaving Valuation Definitions Ambiguous

“Practice value” is not a complete payout formula. An appraisal of the operating business, a formula based on earnings and cash received in a sale can produce different amounts. Full-value participation and appreciation-only participation also create different obligations: the latter measures growth above a defined starting value rather than sharing the entire measured value.

The decision at risk

Owners must choose what value the award tracks and how that value will be determined. Leaving the method open until payment creates uncertainty for both the practice and the participant.

Medical-practice financial statements require particular care here. Cash collections can fluctuate because of payer receipt timing and patient-balance collections. A location’s apparent profitability can also change when shared administrative costs are allocated differently. Neither movement necessarily represents an equivalent change in business value.

Records to gather

Collect reconciled financial statements, available valuation work, debt schedules, accounts receivable summaries and the proposed calculation. For multiple locations or entities, include per-location P&Ls and documented shared-cost allocations. Gather support for proposed adjustments, such as owner compensation or unusual expenses, rather than treating every adjustment as agreed.

Questions to resolve before approval

Definition needing a decision Why it matters
Included business and assets Establishes whether other entities, real estate or newly opened locations affect the award.
Full value or appreciation only Determines whether the calculation shares existing value or growth above a starting amount.
Valuation method and measurement date Prevents different methods or dates from being substituted without a defined process.
Debt, cash and transaction expenses Clarifies the bridge from business value to the payout base.
Earnings and shared-cost adjustments Reduces disagreement about owner compensation, central overhead and unusual expenses.

Ask the valuation adviser which method fits the intended economics and what documentation it requires. Do not assume that a generic valuation report answers the plan’s specific payout question.

Approval checkpoint: An adviser can reproduce a sample payout from the proposed definitions without inventing missing assumptions.

3. Failing to Model Payout Funding and Timing

A valuable practice is not necessarily holding enough available cash to settle an award. Receivables are not bank balances, and transaction proceeds may include amounts received later or noncash consideration.

The decision at risk

The owner must determine whether the practice can fund the proposed obligation when due—not merely whether the award seems affordable at its estimated value today.

Model all outstanding and proposed awards together. A manageable individual grant can become a material obligation when several participants qualify at once.

Records to gather

Use entity-level bank reconciliations, a short-term cash forecast, payroll schedules, debt payments, planned capital spending and the complete proposed award register. For a potential sale, obtain the draft proceeds schedule, including expected holdbacks and deferred amounts.

Questions to resolve before approval

If the model reveals a gap, return the proposal to advisers. Do not assume the practice can postpone an existing payment obligation or informally change settlement terms after grants have been made.

Approval checkpoint: The forecast identifies the funding source, any shortfall and the action needed before the proposed obligation is approved.

4. Overlooking Departure and Transaction Provisions

Vesting, valuation and payment timing answer different questions. Vesting concerns the participant’s earned entitlement under the plan; it does not, by itself, answer when cash is due.

The decision at risk

Owners need to decide how the award works when employment or the business changes. Broad phrases such as “paid on exit” leave too much unresolved: an employee’s exit and an owner’s sale are different events.

Records to gather

Review employment and separation agreements, proposed vesting schedules, existing grants, leave policies and any transaction documents. Identify earlier promises that could conflict with the new proposal.

Questions to resolve before approval

Walk through resignation, retirement, termination, death and disability separately with counsel. For each, ask what happens to vested and unvested amounts, how value is measured, and whether any payment right survives departure. Avoid assuming that labels such as “good leaver” settle those questions.

For transactions, distinguish a full sale from a partial sale, recapitalization, merger or sale of one location. Ask how the proposal treats buyer equity, delayed proceeds and contingent payments. Determine whether any accelerated vesting is intended and how it interacts with the settlement schedule.

A practice considering an additional location should also ask whether that location enters the value calculation automatically or only through an approved change.

Approval checkpoint: Advisers can explain the result of each departure and transaction scenario without relying on informal owner discretion to fill gaps.

5. Failing to Coordinate Legal, Payroll, Accounting and Tax Review

A signed document is not an implementation plan. The legal agreement, financial model and payroll instructions must describe the same arrangement.

The decision at risk

Owners must decide whether the proposal is ready to launch. That requires more than receiving separate comments from advisers who have reviewed different versions.

Records to gather

Prepare one controlled review packet containing the proposed plan, grant schedule, valuation definitions, sample calculations, cash forecast, employment agreements and a list of unresolved questions. Identify the version and responsible reviewer for every document.

Questions to resolve before approval

  • Legal: Are the rights, obligations, amendment limits and approval requirements documented consistently?
  • Tax: What rules apply to this arrangement, including any deferred-compensation requirements? What events require action, and when?
  • Payroll: What information, processing steps, withholding instructions and remittances will be needed, as determined by the advisers?
  • Accounting: How should the arrangement be recognized, measured and presented under the practice’s reporting framework? Which entity records the obligation and expense?
  • Finance: Does the forecast include the full funding requirement without double-counting payroll amounts?

The IRS’s business recordkeeping guidance supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and reconciled bank records. Those practices help establish reliable financial inputs; they do not validate a phantom equity plan’s design.

Approval checkpoint: Each adviser has reviewed the same final proposal, and an identified person owns every implementation step.

Make the Approval Decision Before Making the Promise

A sound review ends with either a documented approval or a specific list of unresolved decisions. Do not launch grants while the valuation base, paying entity or funding source remains unclear.

FAQs

Does phantom equity make a practice employee an owner?

A cash-settled phantom award is generally a contractual payment right rather than an issuance of ownership. Have counsel confirm the actual documents and ensure employee communications do not imply governance or ownership rights that are not being granted.

Can a practice pay an award only when it has enough cash?

Do not assume cash availability permits a delay. Payment obligations depend on the approved arrangement and applicable rules. Model funding before approval and have advisers review proposed timing rather than informally changing it later.

What happens if a participant leaves before a practice sale?

That depends on the plan and grant terms. Review vested and unvested rights, valuation dates, surviving participation and payment timing for each departure scenario before issuing awards.

Who should determine the tax treatment?

The practice’s qualified tax adviser should evaluate the specific arrangement in coordination with legal counsel and payroll. A payout worksheet does not establish tax timing, withholding requirements or the availability of any particular tax treatment.
