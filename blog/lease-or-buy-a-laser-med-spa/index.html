Should my med spa lease or buy a laser?

December 9, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 9, 2026

Buy the laser, with cash or a loan, if you'll book enough treatments to cover the payment and you want to own it after five years. Lease it if you need to keep your cash or you plan to upgrade. Compare the total you'll pay, not the monthly payment, and don't buy only for the tax deduction.

Key points

  • In Example 1, you pay $149,460 for a $120,000 laser on a 5‑year loan at 9%, $138,000 on a 60‑month lease you return, and $153,000 on a fair‑market‑value lease you buy out.
  • If you own the laser and it's installed and ready to use in 2026, you can generally deduct the full cost that year under Section 179, up to $2,560,000.
  • In Example 2, you need 14 treatments a month at $300 to cover the loan payment and service plan, and 25 once you count the room's share of rent and staff.

What do the terms on a laser lease or loan quote mean?

  • An operating lease is a rental: you pay to use the laser for a set term, and the leasing company owns it at the end.
  • A fair‑market‑value lease is an operating lease that lets you buy the laser at the end for what it's worth then. Leasing companies often call it an FMV lease.
  • A capital lease, now usually called a finance lease in your books, is a lease that works like a loan: you'll usually own the laser at the end, often for $1, or your payments add up to nearly its full price.
  • Residual value is what the laser is still worth at the end of the term.
  • Bonus depreciation is a second tax rule that lets you deduct the cost of most equipment in its first year, with no dollar cap.

How do leasing and buying a laser compare?

When you buy, you pay more each month or up front, you own the laser, and you deduct its cost through depreciation. When you lease, you usually pay less each month, the leasing company owns it, and you deduct the payments as you make them.

Example 1: how do I compare a loan and a lease on a $120,000 laser?

Add up every payment plus any buyout, then subtract what the laser will be worth at the end if you'll own it. Here, you pay the least by returning the operating lease and the most by buying out the FMV lease. Once you count what you can sell the laser for, the loan costs least.

Example numbers, not market rates. The $120,000 laser is installed in January, so you make 12 payments in the first tax year. The loan is 9% for 60 months with no down payment. Both leases are $2,300 a month for 60 months. The FMV buyout and the laser's residual value are both $15,000. You'd pay the same service plan either way, so it's left out. You keep your books the same way as your tax return, so each lease payment is one expense on your P&L.

The loan's $2,833 is $2,000 of depreciation, if you spread the full $120,000 over five years in your books, with nothing left at the end, plus $833 of average monthly interest. Its $129,992 deduction, if your business income covers it, is the $120,000 cost under Section 179 plus the $9,992 of interest you pay in the first 12 months. On each lease, you deduct the 12 payments, $27,600.

At an example combined tax rate of 35%, the loan's first‑year deduction cuts your tax by about $45,500, while you've paid $29,892 on the loan. In years 2 to 5, you keep paying $2,491 a month but deduct only the interest, so you'll pay more tax in those years than you would on the lease.

Example 2: how many treatments a month do I need to cover a laser?

Divide what the laser costs you each month by what you keep from one treatment after its own costs. In this example, you need 14 treatments a month at $300 to cover the loan payment and service plan, and 25 once you also cover the room's share of rent and staff.

Example numbers, not market prices. One treatment is $300 and takes 45 minutes. You pay $12 for tips and gel, $40 to the laser tech and a 3% card fee. Your overhead comes to $150 per booked room hour. The service plan is $650 a month on either option.

Can I deduct the whole laser in the year I buy it?

Usually, if you own it and it's installed and ready to use by December 31. For 2026, Section 179 lets you deduct up to $2,560,000 of equipment, and you can use bonus depreciation for 100% of most equipment acquired after January 19, 2025. You can't take either one on an operating lease.

  • The income limit. Your Section 179 deduction can't be more than your business's taxable income for the year, and you carry the rest to next year. The $2,560,000 limit shrinks once you place more than $4,090,000 of equipment in service.
  • Bonus depreciation. You can take it with no business income limit, and you get it unless you elect out.
  • The form. You claim either one on Form 4562.
  • Your state. Many states don't follow the federal rules, so your state deduction may be smaller.
  • Selling it later. If you sell or trade in the laser, you may have to report part of the deduction as income.

You can't count the delivery date. In Publication 946, the IRS treats a machine delivered one year but not running until the next as placed in service the next year. If your installer sets up the laser on January 6, you'll deduct it in the new year. For other equipment and the rest of your deductions, see tax deductions for medical practice owners.

What should dermatology and plastic surgery practices check?

In a dermatology practice, keep cosmetic and medical laser revenue on separate lines so you can see which side covers the payment. In a plastic surgery practice, add the payment to your cash plan before you sign, since you won't get that cash back through the deduction.

What to ask your CPA before you sign

  • Will you treat this lease as rent or as a purchase on my return?
  • Do I have enough business income this year to use Section 179, or should I use bonus depreciation?
  • Does my state follow the federal deduction?
  • What happens to the deduction if I sell or trade in the laser?

Questions med spa owners ask about leasing or buying a laser

Is it better to lease or buy medical equipment?

Buy if you'll use the equipment past the loan term and can book enough treatments to cover the payment. Lease if you expect to replace the model before it wears out, or you need to keep cash for the first months. Ask for both quotes on the same term so you can compare the totals.

How many treatments a month does a laser need to pay for itself?

Divide your monthly laser cost, the payment plus the service plan, by what you keep from one treatment after tips, tech pay and card fees. In Example 2, that's 14 treatments at $300. Count the room's share of rent and staff too, and you need 25.

What's the difference between a capital lease and an operating lease?

A capital lease, or finance lease, usually ends with you owning the equipment, often for $1, so your CPA will usually treat it like a loan on your return and you depreciate the laser. An operating lease ends with the leasing company owning it, and you deduct each payment as rent. Read the buyout terms in your contract to see which one you have.

Can I deduct a laser in the year I buy it?

Yes, in most cases, if you own it and it's installed and ready to use before your tax year ends. You can use Section 179, up to your business income, or 100% bonus depreciation. You can deduct the full cost even if you financed it and have paid only part of the loan.

What happens at the end of a laser lease?

On an operating lease, you return the laser, renew the lease or lease a newer model. On an FMV lease, you can also buy it for what it's worth then, and the leasing company usually sets that price. On a $1 buyout lease, you pay $1 and own it. Check the notice terms, since some leasing companies renew month to month unless you give written notice.

Should I buy equipment in December for the tax deduction?

Only if you'd buy it anyway. When you buy in December, you move the deduction into this year, but you still spend the cash and you'll have less to deduct later. You'll also get the deduction this year only if the laser is installed and ready to use by December 31.
