How do I read my practice's profit and loss statement?

December 2, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 2, 2026

To read your practice's P&L, start at the top and work down. Gross charges minus contractual adjustments gives you net revenue, the money you can expect to collect. Take away operating expenses such as staff, supplies, rent and billing fees, then owner pay, and you reach net income. Then compare each line with net revenue and with last month.

Key points

  • Judge your practice by net revenue, not gross charges, because gross charges include money your insurers will never pay.
  • In Example 1 below, a three-provider practice keeps 43.7% of net revenue before owner pay and 9.5% after it.
  • Divide every expense line by net revenue to compare one month with another.
  • On the accrual method, your P&L can show a profit in a month when your bank balance falls. You'll see how in Example 2.

What is a profit and loss statement?

A profit and loss statement (P&L) is a report of what your practice earned and spent over a period, usually one month, and what was left. It's also called an income statement (SEC, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements). Your balance sheet is a different report: it shows what the practice owns and owes on one day.

Six terms to know:

  • Gross charges are what your practice bills at its own fee schedule, before any insurer discount.
  • A contractual adjustment is the part of a charge your payer contract says you won't collect.
  • Net revenue is gross charges minus contractual adjustments: what you can expect to be paid.
  • An operating expense is a cost of running the practice, such as staff pay, supplies, rent and software.
  • Net income is what's left after every expense, including owner pay. It's the bottom line.
  • Accrual is a way of keeping books where you record revenue when you give the care and expenses when you get the bill, not when you get or pay the cash (IRS, Publication 538).

What's on a medical practice P&L, line by line?

A medical practice P&L comes in three parts. At the top, you'll see what you billed and what you can expect to collect. In the middle, you'll see what it cost to run the practice. At the bottom, you'll see what's left for the owners and for the practice. Below is one month for a three-provider primary care practice.

Sample P&L: a three-provider primary care practice, October 2026. All three owners see patients and take a salary through payroll. The numbers are an example to show the arithmetic, not typical results.

Line Amount What this line tells you
Gross charges $310,000 What you billed at your own fees. Good for tracking volume, not money.
Contractual adjustments ($152,000) What your payer contracts say you won't collect. Watch its share of charges.
Net revenue $158,000 What you can expect to be paid. Judge every other line against it.
Staff wages and benefits $46,000 Front desk, medical assistants, nurses and billers. The biggest cost here.
Medical supplies $9,500 Supplies used in patient care. It should rise and fall with visits.
Rent and utilities $15,000 A fixed cost. It stays the same in a slow month.
Billing company fees $7,900 If your biller charges a percent of collections, this grows with revenue.
Software, insurance and other $10,600 EHR, malpractice cover, phones, marketing. Ask for detail if it grows.
Total operating expenses $89,000 What it cost to run the practice before the owners were paid.
Income before owner pay $69,000 What the practice earned for its owners.
Owner pay, three owners $54,000 Salaries for the owners who see patients.
Net income $15,000 What the practice kept after everyone was paid.

If you own an S corp and take a salary through payroll, that salary is a wage, and it's an expense on the P&L (IRS, Paying yourself; IRS, S corporation employees, shareholders and corporate officers). Draws and distributions aren't expenses, so they don't appear on it at all.

Your bookkeeper names these lines in your chart of accounts. For a starting list, see the chart of accounts a medical practice should use.

How do I read my P&L from top to bottom?

Read it in the same order every month. Check net revenue first, then each expense line as a percent of net revenue, then income before owner pay, then net income. Compare each number with last month and with the same month last year. When a line moves more than revenue does, that's the line to ask about.

  1. Check the period and the method. Make sure it's one full month, and find out whether it's on the cash or accrual method.
  2. Check the adjustment share. Divide contractual adjustments by gross charges.
  3. Read net revenue. Compare it with last month and the same month last year, since many practices have busy and slow seasons.
  4. Divide each expense line by net revenue. That turns dollars into shares you can compare from month to month.

Example 1: reading the October sample, top to bottom. The numbers come from the sample P&L above.

Measure Arithmetic Result
Adjustment share $152,000 ÷ $310,000 49.0%
Staff cost share $46,000 ÷ $158,000 29.1%
Supplies share $9,500 ÷ $158,000 6.0%
Overhead share $89,000 ÷ $158,000 56.3%
Margin before owner pay $69,000 ÷ $158,000 43.7%
Net margin $15,000 ÷ $158,000 9.5%

The owners earned $69,000, or 43.7% of net revenue, before their own pay. They paid most of it to themselves as salary, so the practice kept $15,000. If staff cost was also 29.1% of net revenue in September, the staff line is steady. If it was 26%, they'd ask why staff cost grew faster than revenue.

Why does the same month look different on cash and accrual?

On the accrual method, your P&L counts care in the month you gave it, even if the insurer pays you weeks later. On the cash method, it counts money in the month it reaches your bank. So the same October can show a profit on one method and a loss on the other, as you'll see in Example 2.

Example 2: the same practice, October 2026, on both methods. In October, insurers paid the practice $141,000, mostly for September visits. The practice also paid a $7,500 supply invoice from September.

Line Accrual Cash
Net revenue $158,000 $141,000
Operating expenses $89,000 $96,500
Owner pay $54,000 $54,000
Net income $15,000 ($9,500)

On the accrual P&L, the owners see what October's work earned. On the cash P&L, they see why the bank balance fell. If you see a profit on your P&L and less money in the bank, ask your bookkeeper how much insurers still owe you, and how long they've owed it.

How can I tell if staff or supply costs are too high?

Look at each cost as a percent of net revenue, and compare it with your own last six to twelve months. A cost that grows faster than net revenue is the one to question. You can only do that if your bookkeeper splits the lines, so staff, supplies and billing fees each get their own row instead of one lump.

Published overhead figures vary by specialty and by whether they count owner pay. You'll find the figures with named sources in what overhead percentage is normal for a medical practice.

Ask your bookkeeper about any of these five signs:

Warning sign Line to check What to ask
Net income up, bank balance down Net revenue against deposits How much do insurers owe us, and since when?
Adjustment share jumps Contractual adjustments Did a payer cut its rates, or did our payer mix change?
Staff cost grows faster than revenue Staff wages and benefits Is it new hires, overtime or raises?
Supplies swing month to month Medical supplies Do you record bulk orders when they arrive or when we use them?
A large "other" line Software, insurance and other Can you split this into its own lines?

For primary care practices and cash-pay practices

Primary care practices often get capitation or membership payments as lump deposits. Ask your bookkeeper to put each kind on its own revenue line, so you can see what each one brings in. See bookkeeping for primary care practices.

A cash-pay practice, such as a med spa, has few or no contractual adjustments, so net revenue sits close to what you charge. The line to watch instead is cost of goods sold. Cost of goods sold is what you paid for the product you used in treatments or sold, such as injectables and skincare.

What to ask your bookkeeper or CPA about your P&L

  • Is my monthly P&L on the cash or accrual method?
  • Can you show each expense line as a percent of net revenue, next to last month and the same month last year?
  • Can you split payroll into owners, other providers and support staff?
  • Is the month closed, or could these numbers still change?
  • How should my own pay show up on the P&L?

Questions practice owners ask about their P&L

What shows on a profit and loss statement for a medical practice?

A medical practice P&L shows gross charges, contractual adjustments and net revenue at the top, then operating expenses such as staff, supplies, rent, billing fees and software, then owner pay and net income. Some practices also have other income, such as interest or rent from a subtenant. Your bookkeeper should list it on its own line, below operating expenses, so it doesn't make the practice look busier than it is.

How do I read a P&L if I'm not an accountant?

Start with three numbers: net revenue, total operating expenses and net income. Divide the last two by net revenue, then compare them with last month. You don't need the accounting terms to spot a change. Circle any line that moved and ask your bookkeeper why.

What is a good profit margin for a medical practice?

There isn't one number, because your margin depends on your specialty and on whether owner pay counts as an expense. A practice that pays its owners a salary shows a much smaller margin than one where owners take whatever is left. Track your own margin before and after owner pay each month, and compare it with published figures only when the definitions match.

Why doesn't my P&L match my bank balance?

Your P&L is a record of what you earned and spent, and your bank balance is cash. The two differ because insurers pay weeks after a visit, because loan principal payments leave the bank without being expenses, and because you record big equipment purchases as assets over several years. Ask your bookkeeper for a cash flow statement, so you can see the gap line by line.

Should owner pay be on the practice P&L?

A salary you take through payroll is an expense, so it belongs on the P&L. Draws and distributions aren't expenses, so they don't. If your owners only take draws, add a fair salary for each owner before you compare your practice with published figures. Otherwise your practice will look more profitable than it is.

How often should I review my P&L?

Review it once a month, after your bookkeeper has closed the books and reconciled the bank accounts, so the numbers won't change after you read them. Once a quarter, read the last 12 months side by side to spot seasonal patterns. Before a big decision, like hiring a provider or signing a lease, ask for a P&L for the year so far.
