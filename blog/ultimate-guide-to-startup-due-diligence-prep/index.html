Preparing a Medical Practice for Financial Due Diligence

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

Selling a private medical practice or seeking outside capital puts your financial records under a different kind of scrutiny. Reviewers need to understand not just what the practice earned, but how those earnings connect to bank activity, payroll, receivables, debt and the owner's role in daily operations.

The seller's preparation task is to make those connections clear before outside questions arrive. That means resolving discrepancies, documenting proposed adjustments and assigning responsibility for incomplete records—not simply collecting files.

This guide provides a seller-side financial preparation workflow for owners and managers of staffed US private practices. It is not a formal audit checklist, legal review or valuation opinion. The objective is a defensible financial explanation, not a promise that a transaction will close or achieve a particular price.

1. Agree on the Financial Scope Before Collecting Records

Start with your CPA and transaction advisers by defining the periods, entities and accounting basis under review. Ask prospective reviewers to confirm their requested historical periods rather than assuming a universal requirement.

Write a short scope note covering:

  • Entities: Identify the operating practice, any separate property entity and entities that employ staff or pay shared expenses.
  • Locations: Identify which offices are included and whether location-level results are available.
  • Periods: Establish the historical reporting periods and the latest completed month to be provided.
  • Accounting basis: State whether statements use cash, accrual or another basis, including material departures or changes.
  • Transaction scope: Distinguish a practice sale from financing, a partial ownership transaction or another capital arrangement.
  • Responsibilities: Assign one coordinator and identify who approves accounting corrections, owner-related explanations and external responses.

For a multi-entity practice, separate legal-entity accounts from management reporting. A combined P&L may help explain operations, but reviewers also need to see which entity owns the cash, owes the debt and pays the employees. Document intercompany balances and eliminate internal charges when preparing combined results so they are not counted twice.

2. Reconcile Statements to Supporting Schedules

Use one controlled set of monthly financial statements as the starting point. Label each version with its reporting period, accounting basis and preparation date. Avoid distributing several spreadsheets that show different results without explaining why.

The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. For diligence preparation, extend that discipline to the schedules supporting material financial statement balances.

Financial area Supporting records Reconciliation or explanation needed
Cash Bank statements and completed reconciliations Tie statement balances to the ledger; investigate outstanding or duplicate items
Revenue and collections Aggregate payer and patient collection reports, deposit summaries and ledger detail Explain timing differences, refunds, fees, recoupments and non-revenue deposits
Accounts receivable Aggregate aging and adjustment summaries Tie to the ledger where receivables are recorded; explain exclusions and collectibility assumptions
Payroll Payroll registers, employer-cost summaries and payment records Reconcile gross wages, employer costs, cash payments and unpaid liabilities
Accounts payable and accruals Vendor statements, unpaid invoices and accrual schedules Explain recorded obligations and material items not yet recorded
Debt and equipment Lender statements, agreements and fixed-asset schedules Separate principal from interest and tie balances to the books
Owner and related-party activity Distribution, contribution, loan and related-party expense schedules Distinguish operating expenses from equity movements and financing

Explain the difference between receipts and revenue

A practice's deposits do not automatically equal revenue for the same period. Payer receipts may relate to earlier services, patient balances may remain uncollected, and deposits may include loan proceeds or owner contributions. Payment processing fees can also cause net deposits to differ from gross collections.

Prepare a reconciliation between aggregate collection reports, deposits and recorded revenue. Identify the reporting dates and basis used by each report. If statements are cash basis but the receivables report tracks outstanding balances, explain that distinction rather than forcing the two reports to match.

Use aggregated reports without patient-identifiable details. A financial reviewer generally needs to understand the amount, timing and composition of collections—not individual patient records.

Make location results reproducible

For multiple offices, show directly attributable revenue and expenses separately from shared administrative costs. Document the allocation method for centralized payroll, rent, management and other shared costs.

Consistency matters more than making every location appear profitable. If an allocation method changed, quantify its effect and explain the reason. Do not present a location's margin before shared costs as though it were its fully allocated contribution.

3. Build a Documented Earnings-Adjustment Bridge

Owners often want reviewers to consider earnings without unusual expenses or owner-specific arrangements. These proposed adjustments require support, and they are not the same as accounting corrections.

An accounting correction fixes the historical records. A proposed normalization adjustment presents an adviser-supported view of ongoing earnings. Keep them separate. If an expense was recorded twice, correct the books through the appropriate process. If an expense was valid but allegedly nonrecurring, retain it in historical results and evaluate it separately.

Illustrative example: reported earnings to proposed adjusted earnings

The following invented amounts demonstrate the structure only. They are not benchmarks, a valuation or evidence that a reviewer would accept an adjustment.

Bridge item Amount Evidence and interpretation
Reported net income $300,000 Starting amount tied to the illustrative financial statements
Interest expense +$40,000 Ledger and lender support; included to calculate EBITDA
Income tax expense recorded in the statements +$10,000 Supported statement amount; entity treatment requires CPA review
Depreciation and amortization +$50,000 Tied to supporting schedules
Calculated EBITDA $400,000 Arithmetic subtotal, not a valuation conclusion
Proposed owner-compensation adjustment +$50,000 Recorded compensation of $200,000 less an adviser-estimated ongoing replacement cost of $150,000
Proposed nonrecurring relocation expense +$18,000 Invoices establish the expense; whether it is nonrecurring remains a judgment
Proposed additional ongoing administrative cost -$30,000 Adviser estimate of a role currently performed by the owner without separate compensation
Proposed adjusted EBITDA $438,000 Subject to review, negotiation and validation of assumptions

The invoices and payroll records establish what was paid. They do not establish that the expense should be removed from ongoing earnings.

For each proposed adjustment, record the ledger accounts, dates, amount, supporting documents, rationale, expected future treatment and adviser conclusion. Mark its status as proposed, accepted for internal presentation, disputed or withdrawn.

Owner compensation deserves particular care. If the owner provides clinical care and manages the business, identify both functions and the expected post-transaction arrangement. Removing compensation without allowing for necessary replacement work can overstate earnings. Also check for overlap: an administrative replacement cost should not already be embedded in another compensation adjustment.

4. Assemble Debt, Lease and Payroll Schedules

These schedules explain obligations that a P&L alone cannot show. They should reconcile to the same reporting date as the financial statements.

Debt and leases

For each borrowing arrangement, list the borrowing entity, lender, outstanding principal, payment amount, interest terms, maturity, collateral and any guarantees identified in the agreement. Attach the executed agreement, amendments and recent statements. Distinguish the ledger balance from a lender's transaction-specific payoff amount.

For premises and equipment leases, summarize the tenant entity, location or asset, payment terms, renewal options, deposits and relevant amendments. Flag related-party leases separately. Ask counsel to interpret assignment, consent and other transaction provisions rather than treating a financial summary as legal clearance.

If the owner also owns the building, show actual rent paid and any proposed ongoing rent assumption separately. A proposed rent adjustment needs support; ownership of the property does not make the practice's occupancy cost disappear.

Payroll and staffing costs

Prepare aggregate payroll summaries by entity, location and role group, such as providers, clinical support and administration. Separate wages, bonuses, employer payroll taxes, benefits and contractor costs.

Reconcile these summaries to payroll expense and liabilities. Explain differences caused by pay-period timing, accrued bonuses, corrections or payments made by another entity. Identify owner and family-member compensation separately for adviser review without circulating unnecessary personal details.

Payroll totals alone may not explain the ongoing cost structure. Note vacancies, temporary coverage, owner-performed duties and material staffing changes. Support historical amounts with records and label future staffing costs as assumptions.

Keep the underlying employment and tax records available under an adviser-confirmed retention schedule. A diligence request period is not permission to discard older records.

5. Explain Unusual Transactions Before Reviewers Ask

Create an exception log for significant items that could distort period comparisons or raise questions. Relevant examples include payer recoupments, delayed deposits, settlement payments, equipment disposals, relocation costs, owner loans and transfers between entities.

For each item, prepare a concise explanation:

  1. What happened: Describe the transaction without promotional language.
  2. Where it appears: Identify the entity, period, account and supporting schedule.
  3. What supports it: Reference the agreement, invoice, bank record or other evidence.
  4. How it was recorded: Explain the accounting treatment and any correction.
  5. What remains uncertain: Identify missing evidence or an unresolved adviser judgment.
  6. What happens next: Assign an owner and the next action.

Do not characterize an expense as one-time merely because it was inconvenient or unusually large. A recurring need can generate irregular payments. Likewise, an unusual payer receipt may reflect collection timing rather than stronger underlying performance.

When records are incomplete, state the limitation directly. A documented unresolved difference is more useful than an unsupported explanation presented as fact.

6. Manage Requests With a Single Tracker

Use a tracker to coordinate preparation and prevent conflicting responses. It can be a spreadsheet; its value comes from ownership, version control and clear completion criteria, not specialized software.

Add request identifiers, agreed due dates, document references, reviewer questions and approval history to the working tracker. Avoid marking a request complete merely because a file was uploaded. Completion should mean the file answers the question and reconciles to the approved financial package.

7. Control Responses and Protect Patient Information

Before releasing records, have the coordinator check that statements, schedules and written answers use consistent periods and definitions. Keep a response log so subsequent answers do not contradict earlier explanations. When information changes, identify what changed and distribute the revised version deliberately.

Start with aggregate financial records and exclude patient-identifiable material from the preparation package. Remove patient names, account identifiers and identifying narratives from exports and explanations. Check attachments as well as visible spreadsheet tabs.

The HHS explanation of the minimum necessary standard describes requirements and exceptions that depend on context. If a request appears to require protected health information, pause and involve appropriate privacy and legal advisers to determine what may be disclosed and under what arrangements. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Make the Financial Story Defensible

Before outside review begins, walk through the package with your advisers. Can someone trace reported earnings to the ledger, understand collections versus revenue, identify obligations and distinguish historical facts from proposed adjustments?

Prioritize unresolved differences that affect earnings, cash, debt or ongoing costs. Keep less material open items visible rather than silently dropping them. Preparation is complete enough for the next step when advisers understand both the evidence and its limitations—not when every number tells the most favorable story.

FAQs

Is financial due diligence the same as an audit?

No. Transaction-related financial due diligence examines financial questions relevant to a proposed deal. An audit is a separate professional engagement with its own scope and reporting requirements. Reconciled books support review, but they do not constitute an audit opinion or guarantee a transaction outcome.

Should the practice correct its books or use an earnings adjustment?

Correct errors through the CPA-approved accounting process and preserve an explanation of the change. Use a separate adjustment schedule for proposed views of ongoing earnings, such as replacement compensation or a potentially nonrecurring expense. Do not use an adjustment to conceal an unresolved accounting error or count the same correction twice.

What if the practice has cash-basis statements?

Disclose the basis clearly and prepare supporting schedules for collections, receivables, unpaid obligations and timing differences. Ask the advisers what additional analysis the transaction requires. Do not relabel cash-basis results as accrual results without the necessary accounting work.

How much patient detail should be included?

Use aggregate financial information and exclude patient-identifiable material from the preparation package. If a reviewer requests more detail, involve privacy and legal advisers before disclosure. A request for evidence does not by itself establish that patient-level information is necessary or appropriate to share.
