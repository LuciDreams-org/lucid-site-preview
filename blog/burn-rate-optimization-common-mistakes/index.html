Medical Practice Cash Burn: Common Optimization Mistakes

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A declining bank balance does not automatically mean your medical practice spends too much on daily operations. Cash may be tied up in payer receivables, used for equipment, paid toward loan principal or distributed to owners. Each cause requires a different response.

The dangerous mistake is treating every dollar of reserve depletion as an expense problem. Cutting administrative coverage can slow collections. Delaying essential payments can make cash look healthier while obligations accumulate. Borrowing can conceal an operating shortfall without fixing it.

Before approving cuts, answer three questions: Where did the cash go, which causes are recurring, and would the proposed action improve cash without weakening the practice?

Define Cash Burn Before Trying to Reduce It

Burn terminology varies, so document what your practice includes. For this diagnostic, use these definitions consistently:

Measure Definition What it tells the owner
Gross operating cash burn Cash paid for operations during the period, before subtracting operating receipts The practice’s operating cash outflow, including provider/admin payroll, benefits, rent, supplies and interest
Net operating cash burn Operating cash payments minus operating cash receipts Whether operations consumed cash during the period; a negative result means operations generated cash
Total cash depletion Beginning cash minus ending cash across the accounts being reviewed The overall balance decline, including capital spending, financing, debt principal and owner transactions

Use actual payer receipts, patient payments and other operating collections—not charges submitted or accrual revenue—in the net operating burn calculation.

Keep equipment purchases, loan principal, borrowing and owner distributions separate from operating burn. Interest belongs in operating payments for this management view. Owner compensation paid through payroll is different from an owner distribution; have your accountant classify each correctly.

Gross burn alone is not a diagnosis. A busy practice can have substantial operating payments and still generate cash. Net operating burn is more useful, but even that needs context: a collection delay and an ongoing operating loss can produce similar short-term cash results.

Mistake 1: Cutting Costs Before Reconciling the Cash Decline

Start with reconciled records, not a screenshot of one bank account. The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations.

For the period under review, gather:

  • Reconciled balances for operating, payroll, savings and other practice cash accounts.
  • A P&L and balance sheet prepared on a consistent accounting basis.
  • Aggregate payer and patient receivable aging, collection reports and adjustments.
  • Accounts payable, payroll liabilities and credit-card balances.
  • Equipment purchases, debt statements, owner transactions and transfers.

Define the account and entity boundaries first. Transfers between accounts included in the same review do not create or consume combined cash. A transfer between separate entities matters to each entity’s liquidity, even if it disappears in a combined view.

An illustrative reconciled cash-depletion bridge

All figures below are hypothetical and illustrate one month at an established practice. The practice starts with $300,000 and ends with $250,000 across the same accounts.

Cash movement Effect on cash
Beginning cash $300,000
Payer and patient operating receipts +$240,000
Operating cash payments, including interest −$250,000
Net operating cash movement −$10,000
Equipment purchase −$45,000
Debt principal payments −$12,000
Owner distributions −$18,000
New loan proceeds +$35,000
Total cash movement −$50,000
Ending cash $250,000

The subtotal is not an additional outflow: $240,000 minus $250,000 produces the $10,000 operating cash use. Including the remaining movements produces the $50,000 decline.

Calling the entire $50,000 “monthly operating burn” would misstate the problem. Conversely, focusing only on the $10,000 operating shortfall would overlook other demands on cash. Without the new borrowing, total depletion would have been $85,000.

Reconcile the bridge to the books before using it to authorize cuts. An unexplained difference is a reason to investigate missing accounts, duplicate entries or misclassified transfers—not to add a balancing expense.

Mistake 2: Confusing Collection Delays With Operating Losses

A practice can report an accrual profit while using operating cash. Revenue may have been earned but not collected, while payroll and rent have already been paid.

Continuing the same hypothetical example, assume the practice’s accrual P&L and balance-sheet changes show:

Profit-to-operating-cash reconciliation Amount
Accrual net income $20,000
Add back noncash depreciation +$5,000
Increase in net accounts receivable −$40,000
Increase in operating accounts payable +$5,000
Net operating cash movement −$10,000

This assumes no other reconciling items. Accrual revenue is $280,000, while collections are $240,000. Expenses are $260,000, including $5,000 of depreciation; the increase in operating payables brings operating cash payments to $250,000.

The arithmetic explains the difference, but it does not prove the receivables will be collected. Review aging, payer delays, denials, unresolved balances and adjustment patterns. Determine whether the increase reflects ordinary timing or amounts that may need write-downs.

Also test whether current services support their costs. An apparently profitable month may include estimates that need correction. A billing-system charge total is not a substitute for appropriately measured revenue.

Match the response to the cause: address collection workflow when collectible receipts are delayed; review service economics and recurring costs when the underlying operation loses money. Neither problem is solved merely by renaming it “burn.”

Mistake 3: Improving Cash by Letting Obligations Accumulate

Not paying a bill preserves cash today, but it does not necessarily reduce cost. Growing payables, unpaid payroll obligations or an increasing credit-card balance can make bank-based burn appear better while financial pressure worsens.

Distinguish among:

  • Expense elimination: A service is canceled and the obligation genuinely ends, subject to its contract.
  • Agreed payment timing: A vendor accepts revised terms, changing when cash leaves.
  • Unpaid obligations: The practice retains cash while overdue amounts accumulate.

These are different outcomes. Show liabilities alongside cash so a temporary payment delay is not presented as recurring savings.

Review essential vendors, payroll and required remittances before delaying anything. Where timing needs to change, seek appropriate advice and agreement rather than silently missing payments. Track the amount deferred and its revised due date separately from expense reductions.

Mistake 4: Cutting Staff Without Testing Capacity and Collections

Payroll deserves careful review, but a blanket reduction can damage the activity that produces receipts.

Before reducing provider or administrative coverage, examine appointment capacity, provider schedules, overtime, open positions, claim follow-up workload and collection backlogs. Ask whether the proposed change removes unused capacity or creates a bottleneck.

A smaller payroll is not an improvement if it produces a larger decline in collectible revenue or shifts work into overtime and replacement labor. Financial analysis should also respect essential service and operational requirements; a spreadsheet cannot determine appropriate clinical staffing.

An illustrative action-review worksheet

Every amount and proposed action below is hypothetical, not a recommended staffing level or savings target. Use actual practice evidence before making a decision.

Proposed action Initial cash estimate Evidence and offset to review Decision and follow-up
Remove an administrative shift $6,000 monthly payroll reduction If lost coverage reduces collections by $8,000 monthly, cash worsens by $2,000 before other effects Validate workload and collection impact before approval; assign the practice manager to review results
Cancel duplicate software $1,000 monthly reduction after cancellation takes effect Check termination terms, necessary functionality and replacement work Approve only if no essential function is lost; verify charges stop
Delay a vendor payment $9,000 retained temporarily The liability remains; review agreed terms and service consequences Record as a timing change, not recurring savings
Pause discretionary owner distributions $18,000 retained in the month Review owner commitments and the entity’s circumstances with its advisers Record separately from operating improvement
Draw on a credit line $35,000 cash inflow Record new debt, interest and repayment obligations Label as financing, not reduced burn

For each action, record the responsible person, implementation date, expected cash effect, evidence required and review date. Measure both the intended saving and any deterioration in capacity, collections or liabilities.

Mistake 5: Treating Financing or Capital Spending as Operating Performance

Borrowing increases cash; it does not increase operating collections. Equipment purchases reduce cash; they are not necessarily an immediate operating expense. Debt principal reduces cash without appearing as an expense on the P&L.

Keep these distinctions visible when comparing months. A financed equipment purchase can make one period look cash-rich and another look cash-poor even if operating performance is unchanged.

Before describing results as improved, ask:

  • Did operations generate more cash, or did a lender provide it?
  • Did spending genuinely fall, or was an equipment purchase postponed?
  • Did liabilities decline, or were bills shifted onto a card?
  • Did distributions change, rather than practice profitability?

For multi-location practices, review both location results and entity-level cash. Apply shared administrative costs consistently when comparing location P&Ls, and show direct and allocated costs separately where useful. An allocation changes reported location profitability; it does not itself change total practice cash.

Mistake 6: Using Runway as a Countdown to Failure

The familiar calculation is:

Runway = available cash ÷ monthly cash burn

It is only a rough indicator when the numerator is genuinely available and the denominator represents a reasonably repeatable cash-use rate.

In the hypothetical example above, dividing the $250,000 ending balance by the $50,000 decline produces five months. That is not a reliable survival estimate: the decline includes equipment spending, owner distributions, principal payments and borrowing. Dividing by the $10,000 operating shortfall is also incomplete because it ignores other future obligations and assumes the collection shortfall persists unchanged.

Runway becomes especially misleading when:

  • A positive operating cash flow makes the denominator zero or negative.
  • A single equipment purchase dominates the period.
  • Receivables or payables moved unusually.
  • Borrowing replenished the bank account.
  • Cash belongs to another entity or is otherwise unavailable for the obligations being assessed.

Use runway as a prompt for investigation, not an automatic trigger for layoffs or an arbitrary reserve target. Review available cash against actual commitments and the diagnosed causes of depletion.

Make the Review a Decision Process

At each financial review, reconcile cash, classify its movements and identify which causes have continued across periods. Then select actions that address those causes without merely moving the pressure elsewhere.

The owner’s decision record should distinguish recurring operating improvement, temporary timing changes, investment decisions, financing and owner transactions. Compare subsequent results with the expected effects and investigate differences.

FAQs

Can a profitable medical practice still burn cash?

Yes. An accrual profit can coexist with delayed collections, equipment purchases, debt principal payments or owner distributions. Reconcile profit to operating cash first, then explain the remaining investing, financing and owner movements. A cash-basis P&L requires a different reconciliation; confirm the accounting basis with your accountant.

Should owner distributions count as medical practice cash burn?

Include them in total cash depletion, but show them separately from operating burn. Reducing distributions preserves practice cash without necessarily improving operating profitability. Owner payroll and distributions are different categories and should be classified correctly.

How do I know whether a cost reduction actually worked?

Compare the realized cash saving with related changes in receipts, overtime, replacement costs and unpaid obligations. Confirm that the expense ended rather than moved to another account or a later payment date. Assign an owner and review date to each action so apparent savings are checked against actual results.
