Managing Energy Price Risk in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An energy renewal is a budget decision, not just a rate comparison. Before signing, a medical practice owner needs to know which charges can change, who controls the purchasing decision, and what happens if the practice moves or changes its operating hours.

A fixed supply rate does not necessarily mean a fixed utility bill. Consumption, delivery charges, demand charges, and landlord allocations may still change. For a practice with several locations, those exposures can differ even when the offices have similar staffing and schedules.

The practical goal is to build a defensible energy budget and choose an arrangement that fits the practice’s expected use and occupancy plans—not to predict energy markets. Start with actual bills, the current energy agreement, and relevant lease terms.

1. Establish What the Practice Pays and Controls

Gather a full year of electricity and gas bills where available, along with current contracts, renewal notices, and landlord utility statements. A longer history can help explain seasonal differences, but a newly opened location may need a provisional budget until it has comparable operating data.

Map each service separately. Electricity and gas may have different account holders, billing structures, and renewal dates.

Do not assume that every location can choose an energy supplier. Confirm available options with the utility or a qualified energy adviser. If the landlord purchases energy, the practice’s immediate decision may concern lease charges and documentation rather than supplier selection.

For shared meters, ask how the landlord allocates costs: submetered usage, occupied area, a stated percentage, or another method. A change in your practice’s consumption may not produce a matching change in the amount allocated to it.

2. Separate Consumption From the Price of Energy

A higher bill does not, by itself, show that energy prices increased. It may reflect more usage, a longer billing period, a different tariff, or a lease reconciliation.

Break the bill into components before comparing periods or quotes.

Component What can change What to verify
Consumption-based energy charges Units used and price per unit Which units and rates apply to each charge
Time-based charges Usage during differently priced periods Whether the practice has meaningful exposure to those periods
Demand charges, if applicable Billed demand and the applicable rate How billed demand is calculated, including any minimums or historical provisions
Fixed customer or meter charges Stated recurring amount Whether the charge is fixed only for a period or subject to revision
Delivery and tariff adjustments Rate schedules, riders, and other utility charges Which charges remain outside the supplier’s quoted price
Lease pass-throughs Building costs and the contractual allocation method What the lease permits and what supporting statements are available

For electricity, consumption is commonly shown in kilowatt-hours, while demand may be measured in kilowatts. They are different measures. Lower monthly consumption does not automatically mean a lower billed demand charge.

Compare similar billing periods and note operational changes. Extended evening sessions, additional staff, new equipment, or newly occupied space can affect consumption. Weather can also affect the comparison. Avoid treating every year-over-year difference as a procurement problem.

An all-in cost per unit can flag a change, but it is not a substitute for reviewing bill components. Fixed charges spread over fewer units can make that average rise even when the underlying energy rate is unchanged.

3. Build Usage and Price Scenarios Separately

Forecast monthly units before converting them into dollars. Start with each location’s history, then document expected changes in hours, occupied space, and equipment. Preserve seasonal patterns rather than dividing the annual total equally across months.

Next, apply the relevant rates and model other charges separately. For a landlord-billed location, use the lease allocation method instead of pretending the practice has a direct-meter contract.

Illustrative example: usage changes versus price changes

The following invented scenario demonstrates the arithmetic; it is not a market quote or an expected result for a medical practice.

Assume one office uses 10,000 kWh in a month. Its modeled consumption-based charge is $0.10 per kWh, with $300 of other charges held constant solely to isolate the effects of usage and price. Applicable taxes and any additional adjustments are excluded.

Illustrative scenario Monthly usage Modeled price per kWh Consumption charge Other charges Modeled total
Baseline 10,000 kWh $0.10 $1,000 $300 $1,300
Price increases only 10,000 kWh $0.12 $1,200 $300 $1,500
Usage increases only 11,000 kWh $0.10 $1,100 $300 $1,400
Both increase 11,000 kWh $0.12 $1,320 $300 $1,620

In this example, the combined increase is $320. That includes the higher rate applying to the additional consumption—not merely the sum of the two isolated changes.

Actual delivery, demand, and landlord charges may also change. Add those exposures to the practice’s working model rather than assuming the $300 remains constant.

Translate the scenario into a cash decision

Roll monthly scenarios into each location’s budget and the responsible entity’s cash forecast. Keep peak-cost months visible. Compare the additional cash requirement with scheduled provider and administrative payroll, rent, and other committed payments, using realistic timing for payer receipts and patient-balance collections.

There is no universal reserve percentage for energy risk. Set a budget allowance based on the practice’s modeled downside and available liquidity. If a plausible increase would create a cash shortfall, that deserves attention before renewal—even if energy is a relatively small annual expense.

4. Compare Renewal Terms, Not Just Headline Rates

Ask each party to describe exactly what its quote covers. Compare offers using the same expected usage, service period, and charge categories. A lower supply rate is not enough to establish a lower total cost.

A fixed-price arrangement may reduce uncertainty for specified charges while leaving usage and other costs exposed. A variable or indexed arrangement leaves the applicable rate open to change; it should be evaluated against the practice’s capacity to absorb that change. Neither structure guarantees a lower bill.

A longer term also creates a longer commitment. If a location may close, relocate, or consolidate, quantify the termination exposure before accepting a longer agreement. Ask for written clarification of ambiguous terms and obtain qualified contract advice where needed.

The owner’s decision should be based on total expected cost, downside exposure, and operational flexibility—not an attempt to guess the lowest future market price.

5. Clarify Responsibility for Facility Improvements

Before budgeting for lighting, controls, or HVAC work, establish who owns the equipment, who approves changes, and who pays. In leased space, the party funding an improvement may not be the party receiving the resulting reduction in utility charges.

Ask the landlord to identify responsibility for maintenance, replacement, access, and any end-of-lease obligations. For a proposed improvement, request a documented cost estimate and an explanation of the usage assumptions behind any projected benefit. Treat projected savings as estimates, not guaranteed offsets to a higher energy rate.

Evaluate the expenditure against the remaining occupancy period and the practice’s actual billing arrangement. An upgrade that reduces building consumption may have a different financial effect on a directly metered tenant than on a tenant paying a fixed allocation.

Do not change equipment schedules or facility settings solely to pursue a utility target. Have the appropriate facility personnel assess operational requirements before making changes.

6. Prepare Questions for the Responsible Party

Send questions before the renewal decision, with the relevant bill or contract provision attached.

Ask the utility or supplier

  • Which rate schedule applies, and where is it identified on the bill?
  • Which charges can change during the proposed term?
  • Can you explain demand calculations and provide available usage history?
  • What charges or obligations would remain if the location closed or moved?

Ask the landlord

  • Which lease provisions authorize the utility allocation or adjustment?
  • Can you provide the underlying statement and calculation for the practice’s share?
  • How are vacant space, common areas, and shared equipment treated?
  • Who is responsible for proposed facility improvements and their ongoing maintenance?

Ask a qualified energy adviser

  • Are the quotes comparable on usage, term, and included charges?
  • Which risks remain outside the quoted rate?
  • How are you compensated, and is that compensation included in the comparison?
  • What assumptions would make the recommendation unsuitable if the practice’s plans change?

7. Make Energy Review Part of Location Reporting

Assign a manager to maintain the worksheet, check bills against the agreement, and track renewal requirements. Investigate unexplained changes rather than automatically increasing next year’s budget by the same percentage.

Keep direct location costs distinct from shared allocations. Where one entity pays on behalf of another, have the finance team review the accounting treatment so entity-level and location-level reports remain understandable. Avoid counting the same utility cost both through a landlord statement and a direct bill.

Keep bills, agreements, and supporting calculations with the financial records. IRS Publication 583 provides general guidance on maintaining complete business records; it does not determine energy contract terms.

FAQs

Does a fixed energy rate fix our medical practice’s total bill?

Not necessarily. The agreement may fix only specified supply charges. Usage, delivery, demand, other adjustments, and landlord allocations may remain variable. Request a written explanation of what is included and model the remaining exposure separately.

What if the landlord handles all energy purchasing?

Review the lease and the landlord’s allocation statements. Establish how charges are calculated, what supporting documents are available, and who pays for facility improvements. Your decision may be about budgeting and lease obligations rather than choosing a supplier.

What should be ready before approving a renewal?

Prepare the location’s usage history, a component-level bill comparison, usage-and-price scenarios, and a summary of duration, renewal, and termination terms. Resolve material questions about pass-through charges and occupancy changes before signing.
