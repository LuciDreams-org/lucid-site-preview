How do I pay myself from my medical practice?

December 3, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 3, 2026

It depends on how the IRS taxes your practice. As the only owner of a PLLC taxed by default, you take draws. Partners take guaranteed payments and draws. An S corp owner takes a salary through payroll, then distributions. A C corp owner takes a salary and dividends. Nobody withholds tax from draws, so you pay quarterly estimates.

Key points

  • As the only owner of a PLLC taxed by default, you can't pay yourself a W‑2 salary. You take draws and pay 15.3% self‑employment tax on most of your profit.
  • An S corp owner who works in the practice takes a reasonable salary through payroll before any distributions.
  • You owe income tax on your share of profit even if you leave it in the practice.

What do the words for owner pay mean?

An owner's draw is profit you move from the practice to yourself, with no tax withheld. A guaranteed payment is a set amount a partnership pays a partner for their work, whatever the practice earns. A distribution is profit an S corp or partnership pays its owners, separate from wages. Self‑employment tax is the 15.3% Social Security and Medicare tax you pay on your own business earnings. A reasonable salary is the W‑2 pay an S corp owner takes for their work in the practice. Basis is your tax investment in the practice: what you put in plus your share of profit, minus losses and what you've taken out.

Which way can you pay yourself, by type of practice?

It depends on how the IRS taxes your practice. By default, the IRS taxes a one‑owner PLLC like a sole proprietorship and a PLLC with two or more owners like a partnership. A PLLC can elect corporate tax, and a professional corporation is a C corp unless it elects S corp tax.

How the IRS taxes your practice How you pay yourself Tax taken out when you're paid What reports it
One‑owner PLLC, taxed by default Owner's draw None; you pay quarterly estimates Schedule C and Schedule SE on your Form 1040
PLLC with two or more owners, taxed by default Guaranteed payments and draws None; you pay quarterly estimates Schedule K-1 (Form 1065)
PLLC or professional corporation taxed as an S corp Salary through payroll, plus distributions Payroll tax and income tax on the salary only Form W‑2 and Schedule K-1 (Form 1120‑S)
Professional corporation taxed as a C corp Salary through payroll, plus dividends Payroll tax and income tax on the salary only Form W‑2 and Form 1099‑DIV

How do you pay yourself from a one‑owner PLLC?

You move money from the practice account to your own and record it as a draw. A draw isn't an expense, and nobody withholds tax from it. You pay self‑employment tax and income tax on the whole profit, however much you draw, through quarterly estimates.

The IRS says that as a sole proprietor, "you are not an employee of the business" (IRS Publication 334). So you can't put yourself on payroll.

You pay self‑employment tax on 92.35% of your profit (IRS Publication 334): 12.4% for Social Security on the first $184,500 of earnings in 2026, and 2.9% for Medicare on all of it, plus 0.9% over $200,000 if you're single (IRS, Self‑employment tax).

You'll generally pay estimated tax if you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding. The 2026 payments are due April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15, 2027 (IRS Publication 505). To keep the penalty off, see estimated tax safe harbors for practice owners.

How do partners in a practice pay themselves?

Partners usually take guaranteed payments for their work and draws against their share of profit. The partnership deducts guaranteed payments. Nobody withholds tax from either, and no partner gets a W‑2. Each partner gets a Schedule K-1 and pays estimates (IRS Publication 541).

If partners take different amounts, write down why in your operating agreement: each guaranteed payment, how you split the rest, and when you draw it.

Partners who work in the practice generally pay self‑employment tax on their guaranteed payments and their share of profit (Instructions for Schedule SE). You generally don't pay tax again on a cash draw up to your basis; anything over it is a gain.

How do you pay yourself from an S corp?

You put yourself on payroll at a reasonable salary, with Social Security, Medicare and income tax withheld like any employee. Then you can take the rest of the profit as distributions, with no Social Security or Medicare tax. You still owe income tax on the whole profit. (IRS, S corporation compensation)

If your accountant says you're an S corp but you've never run payroll for yourself, fix that this year. The IRS says officer payments "must be treated as wages to the extent the amounts are reasonable compensation" (Instructions for Form 1120‑S, lines 7 and 8), and it can reclassify distributions as wages (IRS, S corporation compensation).

The IRS doesn't publish a percentage for a reasonable salary. To set and document yours, see how to set a reasonable S corp salary.

You don't pay tax again on distributions up to your stock basis; you report anything over it as a capital gain (Instructions for Form 7203). With several owners, you pay distributions in proportion to shares (Instructions for Form 2553). Before you settle your salary, read the QBI deduction for practice owners.

What if your practice is taxed as a C corp?

You take a salary through payroll, and the practice deducts it. Profit you take out beyond that is usually a dividend. The practice pays 21% tax on its profit (IRS Publication 542) and can't deduct dividends, and you pay tax on the dividends again (IRS, Corporations).

How much tax do you pay on $180,000 of owner pay?

If you take $180,000 of profit as draws from a one‑owner PLLC, you pay $25,433 of self‑employment tax. As an S corp with an assumed $100,000 salary, you and the practice pay $15,300 of Social Security and Medicare, and you pay none on the $72,350 distribution. You owe income tax either way.

Example 1: $180,000 taken as draws. You're the only owner of a PLLC taxed by default, single, with no other wages. The practice earns $180,000 of profit in 2026 and you draw $15,000 a month. Income tax isn't shown.

You add income tax to each estimate and deduct half the self‑employment tax, $12,716.60, on your return.

Example 2: the same $180,000 as an S corp. Your practice is taxed as an S corp and earns $180,000 before your pay. Your CPA sets a $100,000 salary; that's an assumption for the arithmetic, not a suggested salary. You take the rest as distributions.

The distribution is $180,000 minus the salary and the practice's $7,650 share. That's $10,133 less Social Security and Medicare than Example 1, before the cost of payroll and Form 1120‑S.

How do you keep owner pay clean in your books?

Pay yourself on a schedule, from the practice account only, and record each transfer by type: draw, guaranteed payment, payroll or distribution. Draws and distributions go to an equity account, not an expense. Then each month, compare what you've taken with profit so far this year. Your monthly checklist:

  1. Pay yourself on the same day each month. Don't pay personal bills with the practice card.
  2. Record each transfer by type. As an S corp, run your salary through payroll first.
  3. If you have partners, pay each one the amounts your operating agreement sets.
  4. Move money for your quarterly estimates into a separate savings account the same day.

Questions practice owners ask about paying themselves

This guide is general information, not tax advice for your practice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and your numbers. Talk to a CPA before you act on it.

Can I pay myself a salary from my PLLC?

Only if your PLLC is taxed as a corporation, usually after an S corp election on Form 2553. Taxed by default, you're not its employee, so you take draws or guaranteed payments. A fixed monthly draw still has no tax withheld.

What's the difference between an owner's draw and a salary?

A salary is wages: the practice withholds Social Security, Medicare and income tax and reports it on a W‑2. A draw is a transfer of profit with nothing withheld, so you pay the tax later through estimates. A salary is a business expense; a draw doesn't lower the practice's profit.

How do partners in a medical practice get paid?

Most practices pay each partner a guaranteed payment for their work and split the remaining profit by the operating agreement, with draws against that share during the year. At year end, each partner gets a Schedule K-1 showing both, and pays tax on the full amount even if some stays in the practice.

How much should I pay myself from my practice?

Start with what the practice can spare after its bills, loan payments, tax set‑asides and a cash cushion for slow months. If you're an S corp, set your salary first with your CPA, then take distributions from what's left and review it each quarter.

Do I pay tax on money I leave in the practice?

Yes, unless your practice is a C corp. As a sole owner, a partner or an S corp owner, you pay income tax on your share of the profit whether you take it out or not. Profit you leave in raises your basis, so you usually won't pay tax on it again later.

How do I record owner pay in my books?

Record draws and distributions in an equity account for each owner, never as an expense. Record an S corp salary as wages through payroll, and guaranteed payments as their own expense line. Your P&L then shows real profit, and each equity account shows what that owner took.
