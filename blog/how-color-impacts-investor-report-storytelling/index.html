How Color Impacts Investor Report Storytelling

February 28, 2025 · 6 min read

Color can shape how investors perceive financial information and make decisions. Using the right colors in reports boosts readability, trust, and even company valuations. Here’s what you need to know:

  • Colors influence decisions: Reports with strategic color use can increase revenue by 1% and improve stock valuations by up to 70%.
  • Emotional effects: Blue builds trust, red warns against risks, and green signals growth. These associations directly impact risk-taking and confidence.
  • Accessibility matters: Use color schemes that are readable for everyone, including colorblind users, and maintain proper contrast ratios.
  • Practical tips: Stick to brand colors, limit to 6–8 colors, and highlight key data with accent shades like green for gains or red for losses.

Want to make your financial reports stand out? Thoughtful color choices can improve clarity, professionalism, and investor trust.

Research: How Colors Shape Investor Decisions

Reading and Memory Impact

Using color effectively can improve how investors process and remember information. Research shows that color boosts brand recognition by 80%, makes advertisements 42% easier to read, and enhances the ability to recognize visual details by 5% compared to grayscale. These benefits extend to how colors influence emotions and perceptions of risk.

Risk and Emotion Effects

Color doesn't just aid memory - it also affects how investors perceive risk. For example, when financial losses are shown in red rather than black, investors reduce risk-taking by about 25%. William Bazley, an assistant professor of finance at the University of Kansas, highlights the implications:

"Our findings suggest the use of color deserves careful consideration when it's to be used on financial platforms, such as brokerage websites or by retirement service providers... For instance, the use of color could lead to investors avoiding the platform or delaying important financial decisions, which could have deleterious long-term consequences."

Different colors evoke distinct emotional reactions that influence financial decisions:

Color Emotional Association Impact on Financial Decision-Making
Blue Trustworthiness, Stability Builds confidence in data presentation
Red Danger, Warning Discourages risk-taking
Green Growth, Prosperity Promotes a positive outlook
Yellow Optimism, Energy Draws attention to key metrics
Purple Luxury, Confidence Enhances professional credibility

Trust Through Color Design

Establishing trust through color requires careful planning. Experts suggest using no more than 6–8 colors in financial reports to avoid overwhelming the viewer. Specific color schemes can make reports easier to understand. For example:

  • Use blue tones for inputs and assumptions.
  • Highlight calculations with orange or purple.
  • Indicate losses with red and gains with green.
  • Organize section headers with muted gray tones.

Accessibility is also essential. Hamid emphasizes the need to accommodate color blindness:

"A last important point regarding colors is to make sure to cover for color blind people and replace green by blue and red by orange/yellow."

Consistent and thoughtful color use not only improves readability but also enhances the professionalism and clarity of financial reports.

Color Guidelines for Financial Reports

Selecting Color Combinations

Begin with neutral shades and add accent colors for emphasis. For example, pairing navy blue with light gray can give core financial statements a polished and professional look. Soufyan Hamid, a Finance Presentations Coach at SouFBP, emphasizes the importance of aligning with your brand:

"Another important element that I always enforce when training teams is to use the company's color palette and not the standard Excel colors. It will show your business implication, people will recognize them and feel more confident and it will be standardized."

Using Color for Key Data

Colors can help convey meaning and prioritize information. Use blue to represent trust and revenue, green for growth metrics, and purple for projections. This creates a clear visual hierarchy, making it easier to focus on essential data.

Global and Access Standards

Good design goes beyond looking appealing - it must also be accessible to everyone. To ensure financial reports are effective and inclusive:

  • Maintain a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for larger elements.
  • Include alternative indicators like patterns or labels to support color-based cues.
  • Test your designs in grayscale to confirm they remain clear and understandable.

Dana Randall, Head of Accessible UI Design at Level Access, stresses the importance of accessibility in design:

"We fail at our jobs when we create things that are difficult to use or unpleasant to engage with."

Conclusion: Better Reports Through Color

Building on the role of color in shaping perception and decision-making, these insights offer practical ways to improve investor communications.

Key Takeaways

Color influences how investors interpret financial information, affecting their choices and risk assessment. Studies show that color preferences and their effects on trading decisions differ across cultures. For example, a color that signals caution in one region might symbolize opportunity in another. This makes it crucial to tailor color choices to the audience's cultural context.

Practical Steps

Here are some ways to apply these findings for more effective financial reports:

  • Stick to your brand's colors: Soufyan Hamid emphasizes the importance of using a company's color palette instead of generic options. As he explains:

"Another important element that I always enforce when training teams is to use the company's color palette and not the standard Excel colors. It will show your business implication, people will recognize them and feel more confident and it will be standardized."

  • Ensure accessibility: Use a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 to meet WCAG Level AA standards. For colorblind users, swap out red/green indicators for blue/orange combinations.
  • Create a clear visual hierarchy: Use darker shades for data-heavy sections and accent colors sparingly to highlight key metrics.
