Checklist for Reviewing AI Credit Scoring in Medical Practice Financing

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An AI credit score may appear in a lender’s application process, a financing adviser’s report or a service that estimates your practice’s borrowing readiness. Before relying on it, establish what it measures, which records it uses and how errors can be corrected.

For a staffed private medical practice, the important question is not whether a tool uses advanced technology. It is whether its assessment accurately represents the borrowing entity’s finances—and whether a person can review circumstances the score misses.

A score is neither financing approval nor a complete borrowing decision. It does not replace reviewing repayment capacity, loan terms, collateral requirements or the effect of new debt on provider and administrative payroll.

This checklist is for US practice owners and managers evaluating services that assess the practice as a business borrower. It does not cover scoring patients or deciding whether to extend patient credit.

1. Establish the Score’s Intended Use

Ask the lender, adviser or service provider to explain the score’s role in writing. A readiness estimate, an adviser’s screening result and a score used in underwriting are different things.

Question to ask What a useful answer should clarify
Who produces the score? The organization responsible for the model and the party supplying the underlying data
Who will use it? Whether it stays with the adviser or goes to a lender or other recipients
What does it measure? The outcome being estimated, the period covered and the meaning of the scale
Which borrower is assessed? The specific legal entity, rather than an unexplained combination of related practices
What role does it play? Preliminary screening, one underwriting input or another stated purpose
What falls outside its scope? Missing records, unsupported business types and circumstances requiring manual review

A multi-location practice should distinguish location reporting from entity reporting. Two offices belonging to one borrowing entity are not the same as two separately owned entities with shared administrative costs. Ask how the service handles each structure before submitting records.

If the provider cannot explain what the score is intended to do, do not treat its output as a reliable financing recommendation.

2. Reconcile the Source Data Before Scoring

Start with a closed reporting period and reconciled records. A fast score based on incomplete bank feeds is not a substitute for an accurate financial package.

IRS Publication 583 supports maintaining complete business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. Those practices also provide a useful foundation for checking financing inputs.

Assign your bookkeeper or CPA to compare the proposed input file with the general ledger, bank reconciliations and financial statements.

Input area Practice-specific check
Payer and patient receipts Confirm all relevant collection accounts are included; distinguish receipts from billed charges and outstanding balances
Revenue definition Identify whether the figure represents cash collections, accrual revenue or another measure
Transfers and financing deposits Check that account transfers, loan proceeds and owner contributions are not mistaken for operating revenue
Payroll Confirm provider and administrative payroll are represented consistently, including related payroll costs where applicable
Existing debt Reconcile balances and payment obligations to statements and the debt schedule
Entity boundaries Confirm accounts belong to the borrower and that related-entity activity is identified separately
Shared expenses Explain allocations for central administration, rent or other shared costs
Reporting dates Match the score’s measurement period to the supporting records

Do not force every difference to zero without understanding it. Cash collections and accrual revenue can legitimately differ. The goal is to document the definition and reconcile comparable figures—not to make unlike measures appear identical.

3. Require an Explanation You Can Investigate

Ask for more than a label such as “moderate risk.” You need enough information to identify which inputs influenced the result and whether they are correct.

Request:

  • The score’s date, reporting period and available report or version identifier.
  • The main factors affecting the assessment.
  • The source and date of the data behind those factors.
  • How missing, stale or conflicting records are treated.
  • An explanation of the score’s scale and limitations.
  • A contact who can review a factor that appears wrong.

For example, if the report flags declining receipts, ask which accounts and months were compared. If it identifies high debt payments, ask which obligations were included. These are questions about your assessment, not requests to reverse-engineer a lender’s proprietary algorithm.

An explanation should help your finance team investigate a result. It should not merely suggest changing financial behavior to chase a higher score.

4. Ask for Validation Evidence Relevant to Business Borrowers

A demonstration shows how a report looks. Validation evidence addresses whether the assessment performs meaningfully for its stated purpose.

You do not need to become a model developer. Ask the provider or lender’s responsible team for a plain-language summary covering:

  • Population: Was the assessment evaluated on business borrowers comparable to the intended users, or on a materially different population?
  • Outcome: What result was tested, and over what period?
  • Testing: Was performance checked on data separate from the data used to develop the model?
  • Limitations: How does the service handle sparse records, new locations, acquisitions or unusual cash flows?
  • Ongoing review: Who checks whether performance changes as data and business conditions change?
  • Fairness review: What review addresses potentially unfair outcomes or inappropriate use of information?

A headline accuracy percentage is not enough without its definition, testing context and limitations. Evidence from consumer lending does not, by itself, demonstrate usefulness for a private practice business borrower.

If detailed evidence is confidential, ask what summary can be shared and who is accountable for reviewing the model. Record unanswered questions rather than interpreting a polished presentation as proof.

5. Review Consent and Information Handling Before Connecting Accounts

Before uploading files or authorizing access, identify exactly what information will be collected and why. Review the actual authorization and service terms—not just a salesperson’s description.

Ask whether access is one-time or ongoing, which accounts it covers, who receives the information and whether it will be used for purposes beyond the financing assessment. Clarify any owner-level credit inquiry separately, including whose information is involved and what authorization is requested.

Your review should also cover:

  • Whether access is read-only and how it can be revoked.
  • Access permissions for the practice, adviser, lender and service provider.
  • Subcontractors or other recipients involved in processing data.
  • Retention, deletion and export procedures, including stated exceptions.
  • Whether practice information may be used for model training or other secondary purposes.
  • The process for reporting a security incident or unauthorized disclosure.
  • Whether a narrower document submission can meet the same need.

These are due-diligence questions, not assumptions that a particular service offers these controls.

Keep patient information out of the financing workflow where possible

Prefer aggregate payer receipts, patient-balance totals and financial statements over patient-level exports. Inspect transaction descriptions and supporting schedules for patient identifiers before sharing them.

HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities when protected health information is involved. Its minimum necessary guidance also provides context for limiting information, subject to applicable exceptions.

If a proposed workflow involves protected health information, obtain appropriate privacy and legal review before proceeding. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack conversations or a general-purpose AI tool.

6. Confirm the Correction Process—and Test It

Before relying on a score, find out how the service handles inaccurate source data, incorrect classifications and disputed interpretations. These are different problems and may require different reviewers.

Ask who accepts correction requests, what documents are needed, how receipt is acknowledged and how status can be tracked. Also ask whether a corrected input triggers a new assessment and how recipients of the earlier report are notified.

Illustrative example: an omitted payer account

The following figures are hypothetical and illustrate a review workflow, not an actual practice’s results.

A practice applies for equipment financing. Its score report lists quarterly operating receipts of $240,000 and identifies weak receipts as a contributing factor. The practice’s reconciled records show $300,000 in operating cash receipts for the same quarter.

The finance team finds that the scoring submission included the main collection account but omitted a separate payer-deposit account. Both accounts belong to the borrowing entity.

Comparable input for the same quarter Score submission Reconciled records
Main account operating receipts $240,000 $240,000
Separate payer-account operating receipts $0 included $60,000
Total operating receipts $240,000 $300,000

The finance team confirms that the additional receipts are not interaccount transfers and that neither total represents billed charges or outstanding patient balances. The discrepancy concerns account coverage, not a difference between cash and accrual accounting.

Assemble a correction packet

Submit only the relevant supporting information through an agreed channel:

  1. Cover note: Identify the borrower, application reference, score date, disputed field and requested correction.
  2. Discrepancy schedule: Show the reported amount, supported amount and explanation of the difference.
  3. Bank support: Include relevant statements and completed reconciliations for both accounts.
  4. Accounting support: Provide the matching general-ledger receipt detail and financial report on the stated accounting basis.
  5. Entity confirmation: Document that both accounts belong to the borrower, without adding unrelated entity records.
  6. Requested resolution: Ask for confirmation that the input was reviewed, any corrected assessment and notice to recipients who received the earlier version.

Remove unnecessary patient information and unrelated sensitive details. Keep a copy of what was submitted and when.

Correcting the omitted receipts does not guarantee a higher score, better terms or approval. Other assessment factors and underwriting requirements may still apply.

7. Establish a Human Escalation Route

A generic support inbox may resolve access problems but not a disagreement about financial interpretation. Identify a person or responsible team that can review substantive issues.

Ask who handles:

  • Conflicting financial records or disputed classifications.
  • Recent changes not captured in historical data.
  • New locations, acquisitions or entity reorganizations.
  • Unresolved correction requests.
  • Questions about how the score affected the financing assessment.

Record the escalation channel and the provider’s stated response process. If a lender requires a particular scoring service, ask the lender how to submit supplemental financial context; you may not control its software selection.

When a material discrepancy remains unresolved, label the assessment as disputed rather than circulating it internally as verified.

8. Separate Software Review From the Borrowing Decision

A transparent scoring service can still accompany an unsuitable loan. Conversely, attractive advertised terms do not establish that the score uses accurate data.

Software and assessment review Financing decision review
Are the inputs accurate and appropriately scoped? Does the practice need the proposed amount for a defined purpose?
Can the result be explained and corrected? What are the interest charges, fees and total repayment obligations?
Is information handling acceptable? What collateral, guarantees, covenants or restrictions apply?
Is there relevant validation and human review? Can cash flow support payments while preserving payroll and operating reserves?

Have your CPA or finance adviser test repayment against a cash-flow forecast, including slower payer receipts and planned staffing costs. Do not use a favorable score as permission to borrow more than the practice can support.

Turn the Checklist Into an Owner Review

Keep a short decision record listing each issue, supporting evidence, responsible person and open question. Proceed only when you understand the score’s purpose, have checked material inputs and know how to obtain human review. Resolve material errors before relying on the assessment.

FAQs

Does a favorable AI credit score mean my medical practice is approved for financing?

No. Establish whether the result is a readiness estimate, preliminary screening or an underwriting input. Approval, final terms and funding depend on the lender’s process and requirements. Separately evaluate affordability and the proposed agreement.

What if the scoring service will not reveal its full model?

Ask for the information needed to review your assessment: intended use, input sources, major contributing factors, limitations, validation summary and correction process. Full model disclosure and a usable explanation are different requests. Escalate material unanswered questions to the adviser or lender before relying on the result.

Should I upload patient-level billing records to support the application?

Start with aggregate financial reports and ask what specific evidence is necessary. Patient-level records may introduce information that is unnecessary for the financing review. If protected health information is proposed for sharing, obtain appropriate privacy and legal review of the recipient, agreements and workflow first.

Who should review a discrepancy between the score and our books?

Have your bookkeeper or CPA reconcile the inputs first. Then send a focused correction packet to the service’s designated reviewer and notify the financing adviser or lender as appropriate. If the figures are accurate but their interpretation is disputed, request human review rather than changing the books to match the score.
