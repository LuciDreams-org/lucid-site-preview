Scenario Planning for Medical Practices: AI and Manual Methods

December 15, 2024 · 9 min read

Adding provider hours can create more appointment capacity without producing enough additional collections to cover payroll. Reducing hours can lower some costs while leaving rent and administrative expenses unchanged. Scenario planning helps a practice owner evaluate those trade-offs before changing the schedule.

For a staffed private medical practice, the useful comparison is not whether AI is inherently better than a spreadsheet. It is whether the planning workflow makes assumptions visible, calculates their financial effects correctly and helps management decide what to do.

A spreadsheet can support that process. AI-assisted work can help draft scenarios, organize assumptions and explain differences. Neither approach can determine whether patients will fill new appointments or whether payer receipts will arrive when the practice needs them.

Start With the Decision, Not the Tool

A scenario is a conditional financial model: if these operating assumptions hold, these are the expected financial consequences. It is not a promise or a prediction that one outcome will occur.

Consider a practice manager evaluating additional provider hours. The owner needs answers to several connected questions:

  • How much appointment capacity would the new schedule create?
  • How much of that capacity is likely to become completed visits?
  • Would those visits be new to the practice or shifted from existing sessions?
  • What collections could those visits generate, and when would the money arrive?
  • Which provider and support costs would increase immediately?
  • What result would justify keeping, adjusting or ending the schedule change?

Define the decision in writing before building the model. Specify the location, provider, proposed schedule, evaluation period and approval owner. This keeps a narrow scheduling question from becoming an unfocused practice-wide forecast.

Build a Common Financial Baseline

Manual and AI-assisted planning should start with the same reconciled financial information. Otherwise, differences between outputs may reflect inconsistent inputs rather than a meaningful comparison of methods.

Use recent operating results and enough history to identify relevant scheduling patterns, collection delays and seasonal changes. Avoid treating an unusually strong or weak month as normal without explanation.

Input What to establish Why it matters
Provider schedule Available hours and appointment capacity Defines the capacity being added or removed
Completed visits Actual utilization after cancellations and unfilled slots Separates scheduled capacity from completed work
Collections per completed visit Expected payer and patient collections for comparable services Avoids treating gross charges as collectible revenue
Receipt timing How collections arrive after services are performed Connects the operating model to cash availability
Provider payroll Incremental compensation and associated employer costs Captures the cost of the proposed schedule
Support coverage Additional administrative and clinical-support staffing costs Identifies coverage costs beyond provider pay
Supplies and other variable costs Costs that change with completed visits Supports contribution calculations
Existing commitments Rent, software and other costs that will not change Prevents false assumptions about savings or added costs

Keep payer receipts, patient receipts and collections attributable to older balances distinguishable where practical. A strong cash month caused by collecting old receivables does not necessarily show that current appointment capacity is more productive.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices help establish a dependable financial baseline; they do not validate a forecast's operating assumptions.

Spreadsheet and AI-Assisted Workflows Compared

The two methods can use the same underlying model. The difference is how the team prepares, tests and communicates it.

Planning task Spreadsheet-led workflow AI-assisted workflow Required human control
Define assumptions Manager enters labeled inputs AI helps draft an assumption checklist Manager confirms operational relevance
Build calculations Analyst writes formulas and scenario tables AI helps draft formulas or calculation logic Finance reviewer checks formulas and units
Compare cases Team changes inputs or uses sensitivity tables AI helps propose variations and summarize differences Owner approves plausible ranges
Explain results Manager prepares a written summary AI drafts a summary from verified outputs Reviewer checks every financial statement
Update the plan Team replaces actuals and revises assumptions AI helps organize variance explanations Staff confirm source data and causes

When a spreadsheet is enough

A spreadsheet is often a practical choice for one schedule change at one location. Inputs, formulas and outputs can remain visible in a compact model that the manager and owner can review together.

Its main risks are uncontrolled versions, overwritten formulas, inconsistent assumptions and accidental links to outdated information. Use a clearly labeled input area, separate calculations from presentation and retain a dated approved version.

Where AI assistance can help

AI assistance is useful when the team needs help structuring the analysis, drafting alternative cases or turning verified calculations into a readable decision memo. For a multi-location practice, it may also help organize questions about differences across locations.

But an AI-generated explanation can sound convincing while using the wrong collection basis, omitting support payroll or confusing monthly and weekly figures. Review the actual calculations rather than relying on the narrative. Include setup, review time and data-handling requirements when comparing workflow costs—not just software fees.

Worked Example: Should the Practice Add Provider Hours?

Illustrative example: Every number in this section is invented for planning purposes, not a medical-practice benchmark.

A practice manager is considering adding 16 provider hours per week at one location. The model uses a four-week planning period. The provider would receive fixed additional compensation for the scheduled hours, and support coverage would also be committed for that period.

The manager assumes all completed visits in the added sessions would be incremental. That assumption needs testing: moving existing patients from other sessions would reduce the true financial benefit.

Establish the base case

Assumption Illustrative value
Added provider hours per week 16
Weeks in the planning period 4
Appointment capacity per hour 2
Added appointment capacity 128 visits
Completed-visit utilization 75%
Incremental completed visits 96
Expected eventual collections per completed visit $120
Incremental provider payroll, including associated employer costs $6,400
Incremental support payroll, including associated employer costs $1,920
Variable supplies per completed visit $10

The calculation is straightforward:

  • Expected eventual collections: 96 visits × $120 = $11,520.
  • Incremental supplies: 96 visits × $10 = $960.
  • Total incremental costs: $6,400 + $1,920 + $960 = $9,280.
  • Incremental contribution: $11,520 − $9,280 = $2,240.

Here, contribution means expected collections less the modeled incremental costs. It is not total practice profit. Existing overhead remains outside this incremental calculation unless the schedule change causes that overhead to change.

Test the assumption that drives the decision

Instead of generating many slightly different forecasts, begin with the uncertainty most likely to change the answer: completed-visit utilization.

Illustrative sensitivity analysis using the same invented assumptions:

Measure Downside case Base case Higher-utilization case
Completed-visit utilization 50% 75% 87.5%
Incremental completed visits 64 96 112
Expected eventual collections $7,680 $11,520 $13,440
Provider and support payroll $8,320 $8,320 $8,320
Variable supplies $640 $960 $1,120
Incremental contribution −$1,280 $2,240 $4,000

Under these assumptions, each completed visit contributes $110 toward the committed payroll costs: $120 in expected collections less $10 in supplies. Covering $8,320 therefore requires approximately 76 incremental completed visits during the four-week period.

That threshold gives the manager a concrete operational question: is there credible demand for at least that many additional completed visits, without simply moving patients from existing sessions?

Model receipt timing separately

Expected collections are not necessarily cash available during the same period as payroll.

Illustrative cash-timing assumption: Suppose only 40% of the base case's expected collections arrives during the first four weeks, while all modeled incremental costs are paid during that period.

The added sessions would bring in $4,608 in cash against $9,280 in payments, creating a temporary incremental cash shortfall of $4,672. The base case could therefore show positive eventual contribution while still requiring cash to fund the launch.

Extend the cash schedule into later periods to show remaining receipts and any continuing payroll commitments. Do not count collections from earlier sessions twice when the next period's schedule is added.

Review Assumptions Before Approving the Change

A mathematically correct model can still support a poor decision if its assumptions are unrealistic. Assign each material input to someone who can validate it.

Practice manager: capacity and demand

Check whether demand supports the proposed days and times, whether rooms and staff coverage are available, and whether the change would create overtime or other coverage costs. Confirm that utilization means completed visits, not merely booked appointments.

Finance reviewer: collections and costs

Check that the collection assumption reflects comparable services and payer mix rather than gross charges. Confirm payroll treatment, receipt timing and whether costs are genuinely incremental.

For a multi-location practice, separate the affected location's result from the practice-wide result. A session that attracts patients away from another location may improve one location's P&L without improving the overall business.

For multiple entities, identify which entity receives collections and which pays expenses. Show any shared-cost assumptions explicitly rather than assuming cash is interchangeable across accounts.

Owner: decision and risk tolerance

The owner should approve the scenario ranges, available funding and conditions for continuing the change. If demand is uncertain, a limited trial may be more appropriate than a permanent staffing commitment, subject to applicable agreements and operational constraints.

Record what would trigger a review: completed visits below the modeled threshold, collections below expectations or support costs above the approved plan. Set the review timing with enough allowance for collection delays to become visible.

Keep Financial Analysis Separate From Patient Details

Most schedule-planning models can use aggregated visit counts, collections, payroll and location-level expenses. Patient names, diagnoses and identifiable account details usually add no value to this decision.

Before using an external AI service, confirm what information the workflow requires and whether that service is appropriate for it. Do not paste patient-identifiable information into a general-purpose AI tool or ordinary Slack conversations.

HHS explains that accounting services involving protected health information can create business-associate responsibilities. Its minimum necessary guidance also describes limits and exceptions that require context. Use aggregated financial inputs where practical and obtain appropriate guidance when a workflow may involve protected information.

Turn the Model Into a Management Routine

After approval, preserve the original assumptions and compare actual results against them. Track completed visits, incremental collections, payroll, supplies and cash timing separately. Explain whether a variance came from demand, collection amounts, payment delays or costs before changing the forecast.

Keep the final decision memo concise: the proposed change, key assumptions, downside result, cash requirement, responsible manager and next review point. That document is more useful than a large set of unexplained scenarios.
