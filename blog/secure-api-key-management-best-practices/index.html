API Key Management for Medical Practice Finance Connections

November 3, 2026 · 8 min read

A financial connection can keep working long after the person who established it has left your practice. An accounting feed may still use a former administrator’s authorization, or a reporting service may retain access to an entity the practice no longer operates. When ownership is unclear, a routine staffing change can become a financial reporting interruption—or leave unnecessary access in place.

For a staffed private medical practice, API key management is a responsibility and continuity process, not a software development project. The owner needs to know which connections exist, who approves their access, who maintains the credentials, and how access ends.

Qualified IT staff or the relevant vendor should handle technical implementation. Your practice’s job is to define the business purpose, authorize appropriate access, document responsibility, and confirm that financial workflows still work after changes.

Know What Authorizes Each Connection

An API key is a credential that a system uses to identify or authorize another application. Depending on the vendor, possession of the key may allow access without an employee signing in each time. Treat the actual key value as a secret.

Not every financial connection uses an API key. Ask the vendor which method applies rather than labeling every connection a “key.”

Authorization method What it means for the practice What to document
API key A vendor-issued credential allows an application to connect Who maintains it, its permitted access, and how to replace or revoke it
OAuth authorization A user approves an application’s access; the connection may then use access and refresh tokens Who approved access, what was approved, and where to disconnect the application
Named user account A person signs in through an individual account Account owner, role, and offboarding responsibility
Dedicated service account An account supports an ongoing system-to-system workflow Business owner, technical custodian, permissions, and recovery arrangements
Certificate or another vendor-managed method Authentication depends on a different technical credential Vendor contact, maintenance responsibility, expiration if applicable, and replacement procedure

A user’s multifactor authentication protects that sign-in process; it should not be assumed to protect every subsequent use of an API key or token. Have IT explain the actual controls for each connection.

Start With the Financial Workflow, Not the Credential

Inventory the connections that support bookkeeping, payroll and reporting. Include vendor-managed connections even when practice employees never see the underlying secret.

For each connection, ask:

  • What financial task does it support?
  • Which legal entity, location or account does it cover?
  • Does it read information, change records, or initiate transactions?
  • Who depends on its output, and when?
  • What would happen if it stopped working today?

A reporting connection used for a per-location P&L may need access to several locations. A payroll connection may carry sensitive provider and administrative employee information. A bank-related connection used to reconcile payer receipts may need transaction visibility but no ability to initiate payments.

These are different business needs and should not receive identical permissions by default. Record unsupported or uncertain details as questions for IT or the vendor—not assumptions.

Assign an Approver, a Custodian and a Backup

Avoid making one person’s memory the control system. Assign these responsibilities even if a smaller practice combines some roles:

  • Business owner: Usually the practice owner, administrator or finance lead. Confirms that the connection remains necessary and approves its business scope.
  • Technical custodian: Qualified internal IT staff, an IT provider or the responsible vendor. Handles credential configuration, storage, replacement and technical troubleshooting.
  • Backup contact: Can coordinate action when the primary contact is unavailable. This does not mean giving the backup unrestricted access to every secret.
  • Financial validator: Checks that expected records and reports remain complete after a change. This may be the bookkeeper, payroll lead or controller.

For payroll-related connections, identify who can authorize an interruption and who will verify the next payroll workflow. For reporting connections, identify who will confirm that every expected entity and location remains included.

Document who has authority to disable a connection during suspected exposure. Do not wait until an incident to discover that only a departed employee can contact the vendor.

Maintain a Credential-Responsibility Register—Without Secrets

The register is an inventory of responsibility, not a password spreadsheet. It should help an authorized person find the right owner and procedure without revealing credentials.

The following is an illustrative register template, not a description of any particular product’s capabilities:

Connection and purpose Entity or location scope Authorization method Business owner / technical custodian Approved permissions Last reviewed / next review
Accounting feed for reconciliation Named entity and bank accounts Confirm with vendor Practice administrator / designated IT contact Read transactions only, if supported Enter completion date / scheduled review date
Payroll-to-ledger connection Employing entity Confirm with vendor Payroll lead / vendor support contact Approved payroll posting functions only Enter completion date / scheduled review date
Financial reporting connection Specified locations and entities Confirm with vendor Finance lead / designated IT contact Read approved financial data Enter completion date / scheduled review date

For each row, also record:

  • A connection identifier or vendor label that is not a secret.
  • The approved credential-store reference, not the credential itself.
  • The backup contact and vendor support route.
  • Expiration or replacement requirements, if applicable.
  • Dependencies, such as month-end reporting or payroll processing.
  • Revocation instructions or the location of the approved procedure.
  • A change reference showing who approved the latest access change.

Keep the register in an access-controlled location. Although it contains no keys, it still describes your financial systems and access structure. Do not include passwords, tokens, recovery codes, patient identifiers or screenshots displaying secrets.

Approve the Minimum Access Needed

Least privilege means granting only the access required for the approved task. Ask IT or the vendor to translate the available permissions into business consequences.

For a connection that prepares financial reports, useful questions include:

  • Can it read balances without changing accounting entries?
  • Can it be limited to the relevant entity or accounts?
  • Does it need employee-level payroll details, or would summarized payroll expense work?
  • Does it need patient-level information to report collections, or can it use totals?
  • Can reporting access be kept separate from payment or administrative authority?

Some vendors offer limited permission choices. If a connection requires broader access than the workflow appears to need, document that limitation and obtain an explicit decision from the appropriate practice leader. Do not describe it as read-only unless that has been verified.

Where supported, separate unrelated connections so that replacing or revoking one credential does not unnecessarily interrupt another workflow. IT should determine the appropriate configuration; the practice should approve the scope.

Store Credentials Through an Approved Process

Ask qualified IT staff to designate an appropriate credential-management system and configure access for the people or services that need it. A register entry should point to that approved location rather than duplicate the secret.

Do not put credentials in ordinary email, Slack messages, spreadsheets, support screenshots or general-purpose AI tools. If a vendor needs a credential to establish a connection, use its approved secure setup or transfer process. Ask how a temporary handoff will be completed and whether replacement is needed afterward.

Keep patient information out of credential-management discussions. Financial analysis should use aggregate or otherwise appropriately limited information where it can meet the business need. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; it is not a substitute for evaluating a particular workflow.

If a service will handle protected health information, have the practice’s privacy and legal advisers assess the arrangement. HHS guidance on covered entities and business associates provides background. A credential inventory alone does not establish compliance.

Plan Rotation Without Losing Financial Continuity

Rotation means replacing a credential and retiring the old one. Expiration, vendor requirements, suspected exposure and changes in access responsibility can all trigger a review or replacement. There is no single replacement interval that fits every authorization method and vendor.

Have IT establish a documented schedule based on the vendor’s requirements and the connection’s risks. For OAuth connections, the relevant action may involve reviewing authorization or reconnecting the application rather than manually replacing an API key.

For a planned change:

  1. Identify dependencies. Determine which feeds, reports or payroll steps rely on the connection.
  2. Approve the change window. Avoid critical processing periods when feasible, without postponing urgent containment.
  3. Replace through the supported process. IT or the vendor should handle the technical steps and any permitted transition period.
  4. Validate financial results. Confirm freshness, completeness, entity scope and any expected postings—not merely a “connected” status.
  5. Retire the old access. Confirm that the superseded credential or authorization no longer works as intended by the vendor’s procedure.
  6. Update the register. Record completion, the responsible person and the next review.

A reporting connection can appear active while omitting a location. A payroll posting can fail or be duplicated during troubleshooting. The finance team should reconcile expected results before treating the change as complete.

Revoke Access During Offboarding and Workflow Changes

Include financial connections in employee, contractor and vendor offboarding. Also review them when closing a location, changing accounting providers, moving payroll platforms or separating an entity.

Disabling a departing employee’s login should not be assumed to revoke every application authorization or credential they established. Have IT check each affected connection.

Transfer business ownership, remove unnecessary access, review credentials the person could retrieve, and replace or revoke them as appropriate. Confirm that continuing connections have an authorized custodian and recovery path.

Revoking access is different from deleting financial records. Preserve required business records through the practice’s recordkeeping process, with applicable requirements confirmed by its advisers. Arrange an approved fallback workflow if the change may interrupt payroll, reconciliation or close.

Escalate Suspected Exposure Immediately

Treat a key pasted into ordinary chat, an unexpected permission change, unexplained connection activity or access associated with a departed user as a reason to investigate. These signals do not by themselves prove misuse.

The employee who notices the issue should contact the designated IT or security lead through the established escalation route. Do not forward the secret again to explain the problem.

The response should include:

  • Identifying the affected connection without copying the credential.
  • Having authorized IT staff or the vendor contain access and revoke or replace credentials as appropriate.
  • Preserving relevant evidence through the approved incident process.
  • Determining which systems, entities and information may be affected.
  • Checking for unexpected financial changes, missing imports or duplicate postings.
  • Involving privacy, legal, banking or payroll contacts when the circumstances warrant it.

The practice owner should understand operational consequences, but suspected exposure should not be left active simply to finish a report. Qualified responders should determine containment priorities and any applicable notification obligations.

Make Credential Review Part of Finance Operations

Review the register on a documented schedule and after significant staffing, vendor or entity changes. Confirm that each connection is still needed, permissions remain appropriate, contacts are current and replacement procedures are usable.
