AI Financial Planning for Medical Practices: Illustrative Case Studies

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A financial forecast becomes useful when it helps a practice owner make a specific decision: when to add an administrative employee, whether to accept equipment financing, or how to fund an entity without leaving another short of cash.

AI can assist with organizing assumptions, comparing scenarios and drafting a decision memo. It does not establish whether the underlying records are complete, a financing proposal is suitable or a transfer between entities is properly authorized. Those judgments require human review.

The following fictional, illustrative case studies show that division of work. They are not Lucid Financials customer stories, measured results or predictions. All amounts, timing assumptions and decision thresholds are invented for illustration—not recommended benchmarks.

Each case follows the same path: evidence, an AI-assisted task, a human correction, alternatives and a documented decision.

Establish the evidence before reviewing a case

For a staffed private medical practice, the starting point is reconciled financial records—not a confident-looking AI summary. The owner and finance reviewer should identify the source and reporting date for each material input.

Evidence What the reviewer needs to establish
Bank balances and reconciliations Which entity owns the cash, and which payments are outstanding?
Payer receipts and patient collections Are these actual deposits, outstanding balances or assumptions about future collections?
Provider and administrative payroll Which costs are recurring, and which changes are already committed?
Accounts payable and debt schedules When will cash leave the account, including irregular payments?
Location and entity reports Are shared costs allocated consistently, and are transfers distinguished from operating activity?

IRS Publication 583 explains the importance of complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those records provide a foundation for planning; they do not make a forecast certain.

Use aggregate financial inputs wherever possible. Patient names, account identifiers and clinical details generally are not needed to compare hiring dates or financing proposals. Do not place patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack conversations or a general-purpose AI tool. When a workflow involves protected health information, obtain appropriate guidance about the service arrangement and applicable safeguards; HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities.

Case 1: Revising an administrative hiring date

Fictional, illustrative scenario: A single-location practice wants to add an administrative employee. The manager proposes a start next month because existing staff are carrying a sustained workload. The owner wants to know whether that date is affordable without relying on uncertain payer receipts.

Inputs and AI-assisted task

The finance team assembles reconciled cash balances, recent weekly collections, aggregate receivables aging, scheduled provider and administrative payroll, accounts payable, and the proposed employee's compensation and onboarding costs.

The illustrative model uses a fully loaded recurring employment cost of $6,000 per month, with $2,000 of initial onboarding costs. These are planning assumptions, not compensation benchmarks.

The AI-assisted task is to organize the inputs into a weekly cash forecast and compare an immediate start with a later start. The reviewer supplies the collection assumptions rather than asking the model to infer collectability from outstanding balances.

Human correction

The first draft treats a large overdue payer balance as cash arriving during the first forecast month. The practice manager explains that its resolution date remains uncertain. The finance reviewer removes it from the base case and includes it only in a separate collection scenario.

The reviewer also adds employer-paid benefits that were missing from the hiring estimate. Both corrections are recorded in the assumption log before the alternatives are compared.

Alternatives considered

Illustrative comparison: The owner has selected a $100,000 minimum operating-cash threshold for this decision. It is practice-specific, not an industry standard.

Alternative Lowest projected cash after corrections Operational consideration
Start next month $94,000 Addresses workload sooner but falls below the selected threshold
Defer the start by one month $103,000 Preserves more cash but extends the staffing gap
Use temporary administrative coverage Requires a separate quote and forecast Could bridge the gap, but cost and availability remain unconfirmed

The manager documents how work will be prioritized during a delay. The analysis does not assume that existing staff can absorb additional work indefinitely or that a new hire will automatically increase collections.

Final decision memo

The fictional owner selects the later start date, subject to another cash review before confirming the offer's start date.

Memo field Recorded conclusion
Decision and rationale Defer the proposed start by one month because the corrected immediate-start forecast breaches the owner's cash threshold
Evidence and corrections Attach the dated cash forecast; identify the uncertain payer receipt and corrected employment cost
Alternatives rejected or pending Immediate start rejected on current assumptions; temporary coverage pending a quote
Responsible people Owner approves; manager coordinates staffing; finance reviewer updates cash assumptions
Revisit trigger Reassess if collections weaken, workload coverage becomes unsustainable or the hiring terms change

The outcome is a conditional hiring decision—not a claim that delaying a hire guarantees a better financial result.

Case 2: Assessing an equipment financing proposal

Fictional, illustrative scenario: A staffed practice receives an equipment proposal offering a cash purchase or financing. The owner has already established an operational reason to consider the equipment. The financial question is how to pay for it without overcommitting cash.

Inputs and AI-assisted task

The reviewer collects the written vendor quote, financing payment schedule, fees, installation estimate, service costs, current debt obligations and a forecast of existing practice cash flows.

The illustrative proposal includes:

  • A $90,000 cash purchase price.
  • $5,000 of installation costs, payable under either option.
  • Financing with $10,000 upfront, followed by 48 monthly payments of $1,900.
  • A $1,000 financing fee and an $8,000 final payment.

AI assists by extracting the proposed payment dates into a comparison table and flagging missing terms. A human checks every amount against the actual proposal and independently verifies the arithmetic.

Human correction

The initial comparison focuses on the monthly payment and omits the final payment. It also treats installation as included, although the written quote lists it separately.

After correction, the financing option involves $110,200 in nominal payments before installation: $10,000 upfront, $91,200 in monthly payments, the $1,000 fee and the $8,000 final payment. Including installation brings the illustrative total to $115,200, compared with $95,000 for the cash purchase and installation.

This is a nominal cash comparison, not an interest-rate calculation or a complete economic valuation. The reviewer must also assess payment timing, contract terms and liquidity.

Alternatives considered

Alternative Financial question Unresolved condition
Buy with cash Can the practice absorb the initial outflow while meeting existing obligations? Confirm the post-purchase cash forecast
Accept the financing proposal Is retaining cash worth the higher nominal outlay and continuing payments? Review final-payment terms, fees and other obligations
Obtain another proposal Can different terms improve the liquidity trade-off? Compare total payments and contract conditions, not monthly payments alone
Defer the purchase What costs or operational constraints continue during the delay? Confirm whether the existing equipment remains a workable option

The base forecast assumes no incremental collections from the equipment. Any expected additional receipts belong in a separate scenario with explicit assumptions and supporting operational evidence.

Final decision memo

The fictional owner declines the proposal as written and requests an alternative before committing.

Memo field Recorded conclusion
Decision and rationale Do not sign the current proposal; the corrected comparison changes the apparent cost advantage
Evidence and corrections Attach the vendor quote, verified payment schedule and installation estimate; identify the omitted final payment
Alternatives Retain cash purchase and deferral as options while requesting another financing proposal
Required review Finance reviewer tests liquidity; appropriate advisers review material contractual and tax questions
Revisit trigger Reconsider when complete alternative terms are available or the operational need changes

The memo separates financing affordability from tax treatment. It does not assume a deduction, credit or particular accounting treatment makes the purchase worthwhile.

Case 3: Coordinating a multi-entity capital contribution

Fictional, illustrative scenario: An owner operates two practice entities with separate bank accounts. Entity B needs additional operating cash while its collections develop. The owner proposes a capital contribution funded from personal resources and wants to coordinate it with Entity A's upcoming obligations.

Inputs and AI-assisted task

The finance reviewer gathers entity-level balance sheets, bank reconciliations, cash forecasts, existing owner-transaction records and shared-cost schedules. The owner also provides the proposed contribution amount and confirms the source of funds.

In this illustrative scenario, Entity B needs $60,000, and the owner has $60,000 of personal funds available. Entity A holds $180,000 in cash, but has $150,000 of forecast payroll, vendor and debt payments before the next major expected collection cycle.

AI assists by organizing separate cash schedules, listing missing approvals and drafting the decision memo. It is not asked to determine ownership rights or decide whether a transfer legally qualifies as capital.

Human correction

The first draft combines both entities' cash and describes Entity A's balance as available to support Entity B. The CPA rejects that assumption: a consolidated view does not make separate entity accounts interchangeable.

The reviewer also finds that the draft shared-cost schedule assigns the same administrative expense to both entities. Correcting the duplication changes Entity B's forecast need. The underlying payment and allocation method are documented before funding is approved.

Alternatives considered

Alternative What must be resolved before proceeding
Owner contributes personal funds to Entity B Confirm approvals, ownership implications, documentation and accounting treatment with the appropriate advisers
Entity A provides funding to Entity B Assess Entity A's liquidity and obtain advice on a properly structured transaction; do not assume it is a capital contribution
Fund Entity B in stages Identify payment needs, review dates and whether staged funding is operationally sufficient
Defer discretionary spending in Entity B Determine which commitments can actually be postponed and the consequences

Final decision memo

The fictional owner chooses a personal contribution to Entity B, conditional on the required documentation and adviser review. Entity A's account is not used as an informal funding pool.

Memo field Recorded conclusion
Decision and purpose Fund Entity B's documented operating need through an owner contribution, subject to approval and documentation
Source and destination Identify the contributing owner, receiving entity and respective accounts without including banking credentials in the memo
Human corrections Remove assumed access to Entity A's cash and correct the duplicated shared expense
Authorization and execution Record who approves, who initiates the transfer and who verifies receipt and bookkeeping entries
Follow-up Reconcile the contribution and refresh Entity B's cash forecast before considering further funding

The memo documents an intended transaction. It does not substitute for legal or tax advice about entity structure, ownership or contribution treatment.

Reusable case-review template

Use this template for a specific decision rather than asking AI for an open-ended financial recommendation. Keep the supporting forecast and source documents alongside the final memo.

Before signing off, ask: Could another reviewer trace the decision back to its evidence and understand what would change it? If not, the memo needs more work, even if the forecast looks polished.

From analysis to an accountable decision

These cases share a practical lesson: the important output is not an AI-generated recommendation. It is a reviewed decision with identified evidence, corrected assumptions, explicit alternatives and a responsible owner.

FAQs

Are these actual medical practice customer results?

No. All three cases are fictional teaching examples. The amounts, alternatives and decisions illustrate a review process; they are not customer outcomes, financial benchmarks or evidence of guaranteed benefits.

What should a human check in an AI-assisted financial plan?

Check source completeness, arithmetic, receipt timing, omitted obligations, entity ownership of cash and contractual terms. A reviewer should also challenge assumptions about additional collections and document why the selected option is preferable to the alternatives.

Does every practice decision need a detailed memo?

The documentation should fit the significance of the decision. A hiring commitment, equipment financing agreement or entity funding transaction deserves enough detail to show who approved it, which evidence was used and when it should be reconsidered. Routine spending may require a simpler approval record.
