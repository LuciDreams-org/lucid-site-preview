IPO vs. Acquisition: Legal Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

For an established private medical practice group, an acquisition and an initial public offering are not interchangeable exit options. An acquisition involves negotiating the transfer of specified assets, ownership interests or another agreed business structure. An IPO involves offering shares in a company to public investors and preparing that issuer for public-company obligations.

An IPO is not a routine alternative for an individual practice. A public-market discussion may instead concern a larger group or affiliated company. Before comparing routes, owners need to establish which entity could participate, what it owns, how it relates to the clinical practices and whether the proposed structure is feasible.

Healthcare transaction counsel, securities counsel and tax advisers must assess feasibility, structure and applicable requirements. This article provides a framework for those discussions—not a determination that a practice or affiliated company can pursue either route.

Start With the Entity, Not the Exit Label

A practice brand may cover several distinct legal businesses: clinical entities, a management company, a real-estate entity and other affiliates. Shared ownership or a common name does not make their assets, contracts and obligations interchangeable.

Prepare an entity diagram before discussing a proposed transaction. For each entity, identify:

  • Its legal name, formation jurisdiction and business role.
  • Its owners and the records supporting their interests.
  • The locations, employees, bank accounts and material assets associated with it.
  • Its relationships with other entities, including management, lease, loan and shared-service arrangements.
  • The entity that holds each significant payer, vendor or financing contract.

Then ask counsel: What exactly would be sold, reorganized or offered to investors? If the answer is an affiliated management company rather than the clinical practice, identify the agreements connecting them and the limits of those relationships. Do not assume affiliation permits a particular ownership or control arrangement.

This distinction also matters financially. Payer receipts in one entity's account, provider payroll in another and administrative costs shared across locations need documented explanations. Consolidated reports alone may conceal questions that legal diligence must resolve.

Compare the Legal Work, Not Promised Outcomes

Neither route guarantees liquidity, a particular closing date or continued owner control. Use the comparison below to frame adviser questions rather than predict results.

Legal question Acquisition discussion Potential IPO discussion
What participates? Define the assets, interests and entities included or excluded. Identify the proposed issuer and its relationship to clinical and affiliated entities.
Who must approve? Examine owner, lender, contractual and applicable regulatory approval questions. Examine approvals for any reorganization, share issuance and public offering.
Which records matter? Support ownership, contracts, liabilities and purchase-agreement disclosures. Support the issuer's ownership, business disclosures, financial reporting and governance review.
What happens to existing rights? Determine whether rights are exercised, waived, amended or carried forward. Determine how existing securities and investor rights would be treated.
What continues afterward? Address retained liabilities, employment, transition duties and negotiated seller obligations. Assess ongoing reporting, governance, disclosure and shareholder obligations.
Can owners sell immediately? Payment may include deferred, contingent or retained-equity components. Sale opportunities depend on the offering structure and applicable contractual and securities restrictions.

The first decision is whether a route warrants further investigation. It is not yet a choice between two fully available alternatives.

Acquisition: Questions Before Signing an LOI

A letter of intent can shape the negotiations before the definitive purchase agreement is drafted. Have counsel review it before signing, including which provisions would be binding and how confidentiality, exclusivity, expenses and termination would work.

Define the transaction perimeter

Ask whether the proposal concerns assets, equity interests, a merger or another structure. Then identify what stays with the seller. Relevant questions include:

  • Who receives pre-closing payer receipts and outstanding patient balances?
  • Who handles refunds, recoupments or disputed historical payments?
  • Which entity retains debt, leases and other liabilities?
  • Are real estate, equipment and affiliated services included?
  • What happens to intercompany balances and personal guarantees?

The answers require both contract review and accounting support. For example, the team needs to distinguish revenue earned before closing from cash received afterward. The purchase documents should address that distinction rather than leave staff to improvise.

Review consents and continuity

Do not assume a contract follows the business automatically. Counsel should review assignment, change-of-control, notice and termination provisions in material agreements.

For a staffed practice, the review should include payer agreements, premises leases, financing, provider and administrative employment agreements, management arrangements and significant technology or service contracts. Ask which approvals or other steps are needed before closing and which affect operations afterward.

Healthcare counsel should also identify applicable ownership, professional-entity, licensing, enrollment, transaction-notice and other regulatory questions. Their relevance depends on the entities, jurisdictions, services and transaction structure; a generic checklist is not a legal conclusion.

Identify obligations that survive closing

A sale does not necessarily end the owner's responsibilities. Ask counsel to explain proposed representations, indemnification, escrow or holdback arrangements, contingent consideration, restrictive covenants and ongoing employment or management duties.

If the seller receives equity in the buyer or an affiliate, review the new ownership documents as carefully as the sale agreement. Determine what voting, information, transfer and future-sale rights accompany that interest—and what obligations come with it.

Public-Market Route: Questions Before Treating an IPO as Feasible

An established group considering an IPO needs a separate feasibility review. A strong practice business does not, by itself, establish that a proposed issuer has an appropriate structure or is prepared for a public offering.

Identify the issuer and any necessary reorganization

Ask securities and healthcare counsel to evaluate the proposed issuer alongside the clinical entity structure. Would a reorganization be contemplated? Which contracts, ownership interests and financing arrangements would it affect? What approvals would need to be investigated?

If an affiliated company is the proposed issuer, document its actual assets, contractual rights and obligations. Avoid describing it as owning clinical operations or controlling professional decisions unless counsel confirms that the description is accurate and appropriate.

Scope disclosure, reporting and governance work

Public offerings involve securities registration and disclosure questions, followed by continuing obligations. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission is the federal securities regulator; securities counsel should identify the requirements applicable to the proposed issuer and offering.

The initial adviser discussion should cover:

  • The business, risk, ownership and related-party information needed for disclosure.
  • The financial statement, audit and reporting work that must be assessed.
  • Board, committee and governance changes that may be necessary.
  • Responsibility for reviewing public statements and ongoing disclosures.
  • Existing investor rights and potential restrictions on owner share sales.

Do not substitute an ordinary monthly bookkeeping package for an IPO-readiness assessment. The accounting and audit work must be scoped with appropriately qualified advisers.

Separate capital raising from an owner's exit

An IPO may raise money for the issuer without providing the owner with the desired opportunity to sell. Ask who would sell shares, who would receive proceeds and what restrictions could affect later sales.

Owners who remain executives or directors should also understand their continuing responsibilities. Going public is a change in the organization's legal and reporting environment, not simply a closing event.

Build a Counsel Briefing File

Create an indexed file before requesting a detailed route assessment. The practice manager can coordinate collection, but counsel should define the legal scope and disclosure process.

File section Records to assemble Questions for advisers
Entity diagram Formation documents, governing agreements, affiliate relationships and location list Does the diagram reflect the legal and operating structure? What is missing?
Ownership records Ownership ledger, issuance and transfer documents, amendments and approval records Are ownership interests supported and consistent across records?
Investor and owner rights Voting agreements, buy-sell terms, transfer restrictions, options and other equity-related instruments Who has approval, participation, information or other relevant rights?
Material contracts Payer, lease, lender, employment, management and significant vendor agreements, including amendments Which provisions could be triggered by the proposed transaction?
Consent questions A contract-by-contract list of potential notices, consents and approvals Who must act, what evidence is needed and what could prevent closing?
Disputes and obligations Claims, inquiries, guarantees, debt documents and unresolved contractual matters What needs investigation, disclosure, resolution or allocation?
Financial support Entity-level books, bank reconciliations, payroll summaries, intercompany balances and shared-cost schedules Do the records support the legal descriptions and proposed transaction boundaries?
Continuing obligations Proposed employment, transition, retained-equity and post-closing terms What remains binding on the owner and each entity afterward?

Record missing documents explicitly. An unsigned draft should not be labeled an executed agreement, and an unexplained ownership discrepancy should not be silently corrected in a spreadsheet.

For general recordkeeping preparation, IRS Publication 583 supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices help organize financial evidence; they do not establish transaction eligibility or resolve legal ownership questions.

Turn the File Into a Decision Workflow

1. Assign responsibility

Name an owner sponsor and a practice-management coordinator. Have healthcare transaction counsel lead healthcare structural questions, securities counsel assess public-market issues and tax advisers evaluate tax consequences and reporting questions. Engage accounting and audit specialists as the contemplated work requires.

2. Maintain a legal issue register

For each issue, record the affected entity, source document, open question, responsible adviser and next action. Separate missing records from unresolved interpretations. Finding a contract is different from determining whether it requires consent.

3. Reconcile documents with operations

Check whether contracts, payroll records, bank accounts and accounting reports describe the same entity relationships. If an administrative company pays staff supporting several locations, document the arrangement and shared-cost treatment for adviser review. Do not move funds or rewrite agreements simply to make the transaction file look cleaner.

4. Control information sharing

Use a counsel-directed diligence process with appropriate access restrictions. Start financial analysis with aggregated information where possible rather than patient-level records.

HHS guidance explains covered entities and business associates and the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions. Have advisers assess the intended disclosures and appropriate safeguards. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

5. Request a written feasibility assessment

Before authorizing a full process, ask the advisers to summarize feasible structures, unresolved conditions, required specialist work and major consent or continuing-obligation questions. This gives the owner a concrete basis to proceed, pause or narrow the discussion.

Keep Financial Preparation in Its Proper Role

Clean books help advisers trace entity activity and investigate discrepancies. They do not replace legal diligence, determine an ownership structure's permissibility or establish public-offering readiness.

FAQs

Can an individual medical practice choose an IPO instead of an acquisition?

An IPO is not a routine alternative for an individual practice. A public-market discussion may concern a larger group or affiliated company, but advisers must assess the proposed issuer, healthcare structure, securities requirements and tax consequences before treating that route as feasible.

Which records should a practice owner prepare first?

Start with the entity diagram, governing documents, ownership records, investor rights and material contracts. Add a consent-question register and entity-level financial support. Counsel should identify gaps and determine what additional records are needed for the contemplated structure.

Does an acquisition eliminate the seller's legal obligations?

Not necessarily. The negotiated documents may leave the seller with retained liabilities, indemnification duties, employment or transition commitments, guarantees or obligations attached to retained equity. Counsel should explain what continues after closing and how those obligations could be enforced.

Can the practice's finance team determine which route is legally available?

The finance team can organize records, reconcile entity activity and support diligence. Healthcare transaction counsel, securities counsel and tax advisers must assess feasibility, structure and applicable requirements. Financial readiness alone does not establish that either route is available.
