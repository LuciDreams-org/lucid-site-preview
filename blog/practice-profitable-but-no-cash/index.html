Why is my practice profitable but short on cash?

December 3, 2026 · 9 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 3, 2026

Your practice can show a profit and still run short because profit isn't cash. Profit counts what you've earned. Your bank balance counts what you've collected and haven't spent. You'll usually find the gap in seven places: unpaid insurance and patient balances, prepaid bills, supplies on the shelf, equipment, loan principal, owner draws and tax payments.

None of them is a bookkeeping error.

Key points

  • Medicare can't pay a clean electronic claim until the 14th day after it receives it, so a practice that bills insurance is always waiting on some visits.
  • When you pay loan principal or take an owner draw, you take cash out of the bank, but you won't see either one as an expense on your P&L.
  • Days in accounts receivable equals your receivables balance divided by your average charges per day. At $6,000 a day, each extra day means $6,000 you haven't collected yet.

Where does a practice's profit go if it isn't in the bank?

You'll find most of the gap between profit and cash in the seven places below. Your bookkeeper records each one on your balance sheet or cash flow statement, not on your P&L, so owners who read only the P&L miss them.

Where the money is Why it isn't on your P&L as cash out
Receivables
Billed visits not yet paid		 On accrual books, you count revenue the day you see the patient, not the day you're paid
Prepaid expenses
An insurance premium paid months ahead		 You spread the cost over the months it covers
Supplies on the shelf
Braces, tape, injectables, retail product		 You count the cost when you use or sell it
Equipment
A machine paid in cash		 You spread the cost over the years you'll use it
Loan principal
The part of each payment that pays down the loan		 You record only the interest as an expense
Owner draws
Money you move to your own account		 A draw pays you as owner; it isn't a practice cost
Tax payments
Quarterly estimated tax paid from the practice account		 In a PLLC taxed as a sole proprietorship or partnership, or in an S corp, the income tax is yours, not the practice's

Tax rules depend on your state and your numbers, so ask your CPA before you act on the tax line in this table.

What do the cash flow terms in this post mean?

Accounts receivable is the money patients and insurance companies owe your practice for visits you've already billed. Accounts receivable turnover is how many times a year you collect your average receivables balance. A prepaid expense is a bill you've paid for a period that hasn't happened yet, such as next quarter's insurance. An owner's draw is money an owner takes out of the practice that isn't salary. A 13-week cash forecast is a week-by-week plan of the cash you expect in and out over the next three months.

Not sure which method your books use? Read cash or accrual accounting for medical practices. The examples below use accrual books, because you only record receivables on that method.

How do I trace a profitable month that left little cash?

Start with your profit for the month, then take out each of the seven places, one line at a time, until you reach the change in your bank balance.

Example 1: a month with $40,000 of profit and $6,000 more in the bank. A physical therapy practice with three therapists keeps its books on the accrual method. In March, it shows $40,000 of profit, but its bank balance rises by only $6,000.

Line Amount What happened
Profit for March $40,000 From the March P&L
Receivables grew -$11,000 The practice billed $11,000 more than it collected
Insurance premium paid ahead -$4,000 The owner paid $6,000; March's share is $2,000
Supplies still on the shelf -$3,000 The practice bought supplies it will use in April
Loan principal -$6,000 The part of the payment that lowered the loan
Owner draw -$10,000 The owner moved $10,000 to a personal account
Cash the bank gained $6,000 $40,000 minus $34,000

On the cash method, you won't have a receivables line, but the loan, the draw and any equipment still leave a gap. To see what each line on the statement means, read how to read your practice's P&L.

How long do insurance companies take to pay a practice?

Medicare can't pay a clean electronic claim until the 14th day after receiving it, and must pay or deny it within 30 days (CMS, Medicare Claims Processing Manual, chapter 1, sections 80.2.1.1 and 80.2.1.2). Commercial insurers pay on your contract terms and your state's prompt pay rules.

A clean claim is a claim with no errors or missing details, so the payer can process it without asking you for more. When a payer sends a claim back for a fix, the wait starts over once you resend it. So when one payer slows down, you can run short for weeks with no change in profit.

Patients usually take longer to pay their deductible or coinsurance once you bill them. To see which part of an unpaid balance you'll never collect, read how a contractual adjustment differs from patient bad debt.

How do I figure days in accounts receivable?

Divide your receivables balance by your average charges per day. To get charges per day, add up your net charges for the last three months and divide by the number of days in them. Use the same method every month.

Net charges are what you billed after contractual adjustments, the amounts you write off under payer contracts. If you use gross charges instead, you'll get a number that looks better than it is.

Example 2: days in AR for a primary care practice. At March 31, a primary care practice has $210,000 in receivables. Its net charges for January through March were $540,000, over 90 days.

Step Figure
Net charges, January to March $540,000
Average charges per day ($540,000 ÷ 90) $6,000
Receivables at March 31 $210,000
Days in AR ($210,000 ÷ $6,000) 35 days
Days in AR if receivables grow to $252,000 42 days
Cash not yet collected because of the 7 extra days $42,000

You can get the same answer from accounts receivable turnover. If the practice bills $2,190,000 a year ($6,000 a day) and its receivables average $210,000, it turns its receivables about 10.4 times a year, and 365 ÷ 10.4 is about 35 days.

For the month-end steps that keep this number honest, read aging your receivables at month end.

How do I build a 13-week cash forecast?

List the cash you expect to collect and pay out each week for the next 13 weeks, and carry each week's ending balance into the next. Each week, replace your estimates with what actually came in and went out.

  1. Start with today's bank balance.
  2. For cash in, use recent weekly collections, lowered for slow payers.
  3. For cash out, put each payment in the week you'll make it.
  4. Find the lowest week and decide now what you'll do.

Example 3: a 13-week forecast for a physical therapy practice. The practice starts with $30,000. It pays $30,000 of payroll every other week, $8,000 of rent each month, a $6,500 monthly loan payment, $4,000 of bills each week, a $10,000 owner draw every four weeks and a $14,000 premium in week 7. One payer pays late in weeks 5 to 7.

Each cash out figure includes the weekly bills. The practice drops to $9,000 in week 7. The owner sees it coming in week 1, with time to skip the week 4 draw, ask to split the premium, or chase the late payer.

How do I close the gap between profit and cash?

You can close it by collecting sooner, timing large payments to match what you collect, and setting your owner draw from your forecast instead of the bank balance on the day. Start with whichever one fixes your lowest forecast week.

  • Send claims within a day or two of the visit, and fix denials the same week.
  • Collect copays and known deductibles at the front desk.
  • Pay large premiums in installments where the vendor allows it.
  • Keep a reserve that covers your lowest forecast week plus one payroll.

For physical therapy and primary care practices

Physical therapy practices see each patient many times over a plan of care, so when one payer pays slowly, you wait on dozens of small payments at once. See bookkeeping for physical therapists.

Primary care practices bill many payers and collect many small patient balances, which grow early in the year when deductibles reset. See primary care practice accounting.

Questions practice owners ask about cash flow

Why is my practice profitable but I have no money?

You measure profit and cash at different moments. You count profit when you earn it, but cash only arrives when insurance and patients pay, and it leaves for things your P&L doesn't show as expenses, like loan principal and owner draws. Look at your balance sheet: if receivables, prepaid bills or supplies on hand went up this month, that's where you'll find your cash.

What are days in accounts receivable, and what is a good number for a practice?

Days in accounts receivable is how many days of charges you're still waiting to collect. There's no single good number, because it depends on your payers and specialty, so watch your own trend. If it climbs for two or three months in a row, you or your biller are falling behind on claims or patient balances. If you compare against a published benchmark, use the formula its publisher uses.

How long do insurance companies take to pay a practice?

Medicare can't pay a clean electronic claim before the 14th day after receiving it. It must pay it within 30 days, and it owes interest if it pays later. Commercial insurers follow your contract and your state's prompt pay rules, if your state has them. Patients often take longer than either.

How much cash should a medical practice keep in reserve?

There isn't an official number. Build a 13-week forecast, find your lowest week, and keep enough to cover that week plus one full payroll. Some owners also keep enough for a large annual bill, such as an insurance premium, so a slow payer never puts payroll at risk.

Does loan principal show on my P&L?

No. You record only the interest on the loan as an expense. Paying principal lowers the balance you owe, so you'll find it on your balance sheet and your cash flow statement. For tax, you generally deduct the interest, not the principal.

How do I make a 13-week cash forecast?

Use a spreadsheet with one row per week. Start with today's bank balance, add expected collections, subtract payroll, rent, loan payments, draws and large bills, and carry the ending balance forward. Each week, replace the estimate with what happened and add a new week at the end, so you always see 13 weeks ahead.
