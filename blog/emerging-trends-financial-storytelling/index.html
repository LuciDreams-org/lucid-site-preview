New Approaches to Financial Storytelling for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A statement packet can show that profit declined without helping a practice owner decide what to do next. A polished explanation can be equally unhelpful if it confidently attributes the decline to the wrong cause.

For staffed private medical practices, the useful question is not whether financial reporting looks more sophisticated. It is whether a different narrative approach improves a specific discussion: Should the practice fill an administrative vacancy, investigate slowing payer receipts, change a location’s staffing plan, or postpone a discretionary purchase?

Exception-led narratives, interactive drill-downs and AI-drafted commentary are options to test—not proven improvements every practice needs. Each should connect reconciled results to supported explanations, unresolved questions and a decision. None replaces the underlying financial statements or the finance professional responsible for reviewing them.

Start With the Decision the Report Must Support

Before changing the reporting format, identify what the owner and manager need to decide. “Review quarterly performance” is too broad. “Determine whether the second location can support its planned administrative hire” gives the narrative a purpose.

Then establish the reporting foundation:

  • Period and accounting basis: State whether results are monthly or quarterly and cash-basis or accrual-basis.
  • Scope: Identify the entities and locations included, including any consolidation adjustments.
  • Comparison: Use a clearly identified budget, prior period or updated forecast.
  • Definitions: Distinguish recognized revenue, payer and patient receipts, operating profit and available cash.
  • Status: Mark preliminary results and unresolved reconciliation items.

These details prevent a compelling story from obscuring a mismatch. For example, higher cash receipts do not necessarily mean the current quarter’s services generated more revenue. Collections may relate to earlier periods.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those practices provide a foundation for analysis; a narrative cannot repair incomplete books.

Compare Two Narratives Using One Hypothetical Quarter

Illustrative example: The following figures and supporting observations describe a hypothetical two-location private medical practice. They are not benchmarks or a client case study. The practice uses accrual-basis management reporting, and its owner is deciding whether to proceed with an additional administrative hire at the second location.

Quarterly measure Budget Actual Difference
Recognized revenue $1,200,000 $1,160,000 $40,000 below budget
Provider and administrative payroll expense $620,000 $650,000 $30,000 above budget
Other operating expenses $350,000 $350,000 On budget
Operating profit $230,000 $160,000 $70,000 below budget
Payer and patient cash receipts, shown separately $1,200,000 $1,100,000 $100,000 below budget

Operating profit here means recognized revenue less the payroll and other operating expenses shown. It is not the practice’s cash balance or an amount automatically available for owner distributions.

The Conventional Narrative

Revenue finished $40,000 below budget, while payroll was $30,000 above budget. Other operating expenses were on plan, leaving operating profit $70,000 below budget. Cash receipts were also below expectations. Management should monitor collections and staffing costs before approving additional hiring.

This account is accurate, concise and easy to produce. For a stable quarter with no major decisions, it may be sufficient. But it leaves the owner with several unanswered questions: Which staffing costs changed? Is the collections shortfall temporary? Would delaying the hire make the underlying problem worse?

The Decision-Centered Account

In this same illustrative example, the finance team has reviewed payroll detail, location results and aggregate receivables reports. Its revised account reads:

Decision: Do not approve or reject the additional administrative hire solely from the consolidated payroll variance. First determine whether the proposed role addresses the second location’s documented workload and whether the cash plan supports it.

Results: Operating profit was $70,000 below budget: $40,000 from lower recognized revenue and $30,000 from higher payroll. Payroll detail attributes $20,000 of the variance to a provider starting earlier than budgeted and $10,000 to administrative overtime. These explain the expense variance, but do not establish whether either cost was economically justified.

Evidence and uncertainty: The second location accounts for most of the revenue shortfall. Its aggregate receivables report also shows more balances in older aging categories. That raises a collection concern, but does not prove why recognized revenue missed budget. Service volume, contractual adjustments and posting completeness require separate review. Receipts below budget do not establish that the revenue shortfall is permanent.

Counterevidence: Administrative overtime also occurred at the first location. The proposed second-location hire therefore cannot be assumed to resolve the full overtime variance.

Next actions: The practice manager will review overtime by location and role. The billing lead will provide an aggregate explanation of aging changes. The finance team will test hiring and delayed-hiring scenarios against the cash forecast. The owner will revisit the decision after those reviews and before making an offer.

The second account is not better because it is longer. It separates arithmetic from causation, identifies evidence that complicates the proposed explanation and assigns the work needed to make a decision.

Option 1: Lead With Exceptions Instead of Statement Order

A conventional report often follows the income statement from revenue through expenses. An exception-led report starts with the changes that could alter an owner’s action.

An exception might be an unexpected expense, a deteriorating receivables pattern, a location missing its plan or a material difference between profit and cash generation. It can also be favorable: stronger receipts may support a planned purchase, provided upcoming obligations are covered.

Choose exceptions using both financial significance and decision relevance. A small recurring expense can deserve attention if it signals a broader allocation problem. A large expected payment may need little discussion beyond confirming that it was planned.

Reporting approach Useful when Main risk Evaluation question
Statement-order commentary Owners need a consistent overview Important decisions get buried Can readers identify what requires action?
Exception-led narrative A few changes drive the quarter’s decisions The author omits inconvenient results Are material favorable and unfavorable exceptions included?
Interactive drill-down Owners need to test competing explanations Filters change the meaning of totals Can each view reconcile to the approved packet?
AI-drafted commentary Structured facts need a first draft Fluent wording invents causes or certainty Can every factual and causal statement be verified?

Keep the full statements available. Exception-led reporting should prioritize discussion, not conceal parts of the financial picture.

Option 2: Use Interactive Drill-Downs to Test Explanations

Interactive reporting is useful when the next question depends on the previous answer. In the hypothetical quarter, an owner might move from consolidated payroll to payroll by location, then separate provider compensation from administrative overtime.

The value is the ability to test a claim—not merely click through more detail.

For a multi-location practice, a useful review path is:

  1. Start with the consolidated variance.
  2. Identify the location or entity contributing to it.
  3. Separate direct expenses from allocated shared costs.
  4. Inspect the relevant supporting schedule.
  5. Return to the original decision with an updated explanation.

Shared costs deserve particular attention. If administrative expenses are allocated differently between periods, a location’s apparent improvement may reflect the allocation method rather than better operations. Label the method and disclose changes before comparing location margins.

Use the same reporting cutoff, accounting basis and account definitions across views. Preserve a dated snapshot of the approved report so a later live view does not silently contradict what owners discussed.

Interactive reporting may be unnecessary for a single-location practice with a small number of stable accounts. A well-organized spreadsheet and clear supporting schedules may answer the same questions with less maintenance.

Option 3: Use AI for Drafting, Not Unsupervised Conclusions

AI-drafted commentary can be tested as a writing aid. Give it approved summary figures, defined comparisons and reviewed management observations. Ask it to distinguish facts, possible explanations and open questions.

A useful drafting instruction is:

Summarize the supplied variances without inventing causes. Identify which explanations are documented, which remain hypotheses and which require follow-up. Do not infer current-period revenue performance from cash receipts alone.

Then have a responsible finance reviewer check the output against the source schedules. AI may describe an expense as “inefficient” when the records show only that it increased. It may also turn a manager’s tentative explanation into a definitive claim.

Keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary Slack conversations and general-purpose AI tools. Prefer aggregate financial inputs where they answer the question. HHS explains that accounting services involving protected health information can create business-associate responsibilities. It also describes the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions. Confirm the appropriate handling arrangements before sharing sensitive source material; a drafting workflow does not establish compliance.

Review Every Narrative Before Distribution

Use the same review rubric for human-written and AI-assisted commentary. Strong prose should not receive a lighter review than a spreadsheet.

Review area What the reviewer checks Reason to return the draft
Reconciled numbers Figures match approved statements and supporting schedules; variance arithmetic works The narrative uses an unreconciled bank balance or superseded report
Consistent definitions Period, basis, entity scope and metric definitions are explicit Receipts are described as recognized revenue
Supported causes Each causal claim has identified evidence A payroll increase is called overstaffing without workload analysis
Uncertainty Hypotheses and unresolved items are clearly labeled A possible collection delay becomes a promised recovery
Counterevidence Relevant facts that weaken the preferred explanation are included Overtime elsewhere is omitted from a location-specific hiring argument
Cross-version consistency Owner summaries, manager reports and interactive views use the same approved facts Different audiences receive conflicting explanations
Actionability Each material issue has a decision, responsible person and review point The only recommendation is “monitor closely”

Different audiences can receive different levels of detail, but not different underlying facts. Maintain a common approved fact sheet, and update all distributed versions when a material correction changes the conclusion.

Pilot the Approach Before Replacing the Packet

Use a completed quarter to compare the current narrative with one alternative. Keep the financial statements unchanged so the test evaluates communication rather than different numbers.

Ask owners and managers:

  • Did the revised account make the pending decision clearer?
  • Could readers distinguish confirmed causes from hypotheses?
  • Did drill-downs resolve a question or create new reconciliation work?
  • Did drafting and review require more or less effort overall?
  • Were follow-up actions assigned and completed?

Do not judge success only by shorter meetings or faster drafting. A longer discussion that exposes an unsupported assumption may be more useful than quick agreement.

Keep the parts that help. A practice might retain its static statement packet, add an exception-led opening page and use drill-downs only for disputed explanations. AI drafting can remain optional.

FAQs

Should a medical practice replace its financial statements with a narrative?

No. The narrative should explain and prioritize the statements, not replace them. Owners still need the underlying financial reports and relevant supporting schedules. The commentary should make clear which figures support each material conclusion.

How can owners tell whether an explanation is supported?

Ask what record supports the claim and whether another explanation fits the same facts. Payroll detail can explain the components of a payroll variance, but it does not alone prove that staffing is excessive. Look for evidence connecting the financial change to the proposed cause, and identify what remains unknown.

Does interactive reporting require a new software platform?

Not necessarily. Consistent spreadsheets and supporting schedules may provide enough drill-down capability. Evaluate whether the approach answers important questions, reconciles to approved reports and is practical to maintain before adding software.

Who should approve AI-drafted financial commentary?

A designated finance reviewer should verify the figures, definitions and financial reasoning. The practice manager should confirm operational explanations, and the owner should approve decisions within their authority. AI-generated wording should remain a draft until those checks are complete.
