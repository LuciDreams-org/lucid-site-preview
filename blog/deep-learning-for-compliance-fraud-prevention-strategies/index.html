Deep Learning for Fraud Review in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

An unusual vendor payment deserves investigation, but unusual does not mean fraudulent. A medical practice might pay a new equipment supplier, prepay a service contract, or consolidate purchases across locations. Each can look suspicious without the supporting documents.

Deep learning can help prioritize financial transactions for review by identifying patterns in amounts, timing, and relationships. It does not establish intent, authenticate an invoice, or replace payment approval. For a practice with modest transaction volume, straightforward controls may be easier to operate and more useful than a complex model.

The owner's decision is therefore not simply whether to buy AI screening. It is whether a model can identify useful exceptions beyond existing controls—and whether the practice has the records, reviewers, and authority structure to act on them.

This article focuses on vendor-payment review for staffed U.S. private medical practices. It excludes patient data and clinical claims adjudication.

Start with the payment risk, not the model

Define the specific problem before evaluating a detection method. “Find fraud” is too broad to test. “Identify vendor payments that warrant additional verification before release” provides a workable scope.

A practice manager might want to review payments following bank-detail changes, repeated invoices across locations, or purchase activity that differs from a vendor's history. Some of these checks require no machine learning.

Payment concern Useful first control Possible role for a model
Same invoice submitted twice Match vendor, invoice number, amount, and prior payment status Identify less exact similarities for review
Vendor requests a new bank account Independently verify the change through an established contact Prioritize unusual sequences of changes and payments
Several purchases fall below an approval limit Aggregate related purchases by vendor and period Identify patterns not captured by the aggregation rule
Shared costs appear in several entities Check allocation support and paying-entity records Flag departures from established allocation patterns
Payment amount differs from the invoice Reconcile the payment proposal to supporting documents Usually unnecessary for the basic mismatch

Keep these controls even if a model is introduced. A low anomaly score should never override a missing approval or an unverified change to payment instructions.

What deep learning can—and cannot—tell an owner

Deep learning uses multilayer neural networks to learn patterns from data. Its usefulness depends on the task, the available history, and the quality of evaluation—not simply the sophistication of the model.

Supervised models need reliable labels

A supervised model learns from examples assigned an outcome, such as confirmed fraud or verified legitimate activity. Those labels need evidence and consistent definitions.

An unpaid invoice is not necessarily fraudulent. Neither is an employee's disputed reimbursement. “Not investigated” should not be treated as “verified legitimate,” and a suspicious transaction should not become a confirmed-fraud label merely because a reviewer escalated it.

Confirmed fraud may be rare in a practice's own records. That limits what a practice-specific supervised model can learn. A vendor may use outside training data, but the owner should ask whether those transactions resemble the practice's payments and whether the model has been tested on relevant data. A large external dataset does not automatically make a model appropriate.

Anomaly detection does not require confirmed fraud examples to train

An anomaly model can learn patterns in ordinary activity and flag deviations. An autoencoder, for example, attempts to reconstruct transaction characteristics; larger reconstruction errors can contribute to an anomaly score.

The important limitation is that difference is not dishonesty. A new location, annual insurance payment, or equipment purchase may be highly unusual and entirely legitimate. Historical activity can also contain undetected problems that the model learns as normal.

Human-reviewed outcomes remain necessary to determine whether anomaly alerts are useful.

Sequence and relationship models need suitable records

Sequence models examine events in order, such as a vendor bank-detail change followed by a payment request. Graph models examine relationships among records, such as vendors, paying entities, and payment destinations.

These methods require dependable timestamps and identifiers. Shared payment details can warrant investigation, but they may also reflect legitimate arrangements. A relationship alone is not proof of wrongdoing.

Check data readiness before paying for complexity

For a vendor-payment pilot, use a narrowly scoped dataset: transaction identifiers, vendor identifiers, invoice references, dates, amounts, paying entities, locations, approval status, and relevant vendor-change events. Use restricted identifiers rather than exposing full account details unnecessarily.

Keep patient names, diagnoses, patient balances, and clinical records out of this workflow. They are not needed to evaluate vendor-payment patterns. Do not paste patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool.

Before testing, answer these questions:

  • Can each payment be traced to its invoice, approval, and bank record?
  • Are vendor names and identifiers consistent across locations and entities?
  • Can transfers, refunds, reversals, and shared-cost allocations be distinguished from purchases?
  • Are missing fields and duplicate imports identifiable?
  • Are investigation outcomes supported and consistently labeled?
  • Does the history represent normal operating cycles, including periodic expenses?

Do not silently fill missing approval fields with “approved.” Do not remove genuine duplicate-payment events while cleaning duplicate imports. Document the difference.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts, and reconciliation of bank records. Those fundamentals support transaction review regardless of whether a model is used.

Decide whether simpler controls are enough

There is no universal transaction count that makes deep learning worthwhile. The relevant questions are whether the data contain enough representative examples, whether a model improves review, and whether that improvement justifies its operating burden.

A single-location practice may have too few repeated transactions and confirmed outcomes to evaluate a complex model confidently. A multi-location group may have more history, but inconsistent vendor records and inter-entity activity can still undermine results.

Compare three approaches using the same review scope:

  1. Existing controls: invoice matching, approval checks, vendor-change verification, and bank reconciliation.
  2. Simple analytics: duplicate checks, vendor-level payment summaries, and comparisons with historical ranges.
  3. Model-assisted review: alerts added to the same process, with documented human decisions.

Use a shadow pilot in which the model produces alerts without changing payment authority. Review ordinary payments as well as flagged ones; examining only alerts cannot show what the system misses.

A model is not justified merely because it produces more alerts. It should identify useful exceptions beyond the baseline without creating a review queue the practice cannot manage.

A synthetic vendor-payment example

Illustrative example: A two-location practice normally pays a facilities vendor $2,400 monthly. A proposed $7,200 payment appears shortly after a change to the vendor's payment instructions. The invoice description says it covers quarterly service.

Assume a test model assigns an anomaly score of 0.92 on its own zero-to-one scale. That does not mean a 92% probability of fraud. Unless a score has been specifically calibrated and validated as a probability, it should be treated only as a ranking signal.

The reviewer has several explanations to investigate: a legitimate quarterly invoice, a duplicate payment request, an incorrect vendor-record change, or an attempted diversion of funds. The model cannot choose among them without evidence.

This distinction matters: the invoice may be valid while the destination remains unverified. The reviewer documents what the evidence establishes, what remains unknown, and who has authority to act.

Keep model review separate from payment authorization

Assign three responsibilities explicitly:

  • Screening: Produce and route an exception with its source records and model version.
  • Investigation: Check documents, verify facts independently, and record a reasoned disposition.
  • Authorization: Approve or withhold payment under the practice's established authority rules.

A model should not approve payments. Nor should clearing an alert automatically release a payment. Normal approval requirements still apply.

Where staffing makes full separation difficult, use an owner or another designated approver for sensitive exceptions, especially vendor banking changes. Avoid having the person who created a vendor or changed payment instructions serve as the only reviewer.

Use neutral dispositions such as “verified legitimate,” “accounting correction required,” “additional evidence needed,” or “escalated for investigation.” Record fraud as confirmed only when the available evidence supports that conclusion. Escalate unresolved concerns through the practice's established management and adviser process rather than making accusations from a score.

Measure false positives, missed issues, and drift

A useful evaluation needs more than overall accuracy. When fraud is rare, a model can appear accurate while finding nothing useful.

Measure What it tells the owner Important limitation
Alert volume Whether the queue fits reviewer capacity Fewer alerts do not prove better detection
Precision Share of reviewed alerts meeting a defined target outcome A useful accounting exception is not necessarily fraud
False positives Legitimate activity incorrectly flagged for the chosen target Review time and payment delays still have a cost
Recall Share of known target cases identified Unknown or undetected cases cannot be counted reliably
Review time and unresolved age Whether exceptions receive timely attention Fast closure is not evidence of a sound investigation
Results by entity, location, and vendor type Whether performance varies across the practice Small groups may have too few examples for firm conclusions

Test on later transactions that were not used for training. Keep information learned after a payment investigation out of prepayment model inputs; otherwise, the test can appear stronger than actual use.

Model drift means the relationship between the model and current activity has changed. Opening a location, changing purchasing arrangements, or adding a service line can alter legitimate payment patterns. Monitor input changes, alert volume, and reviewed outcomes. Investigate sudden shifts rather than automatically raising thresholds or retraining on unresolved cases.

Keep a short model record covering purpose, data scope, exclusions, versions, evaluation results, alert thresholds, reviewer responsibilities, and conditions for pausing use. Suspend model-assisted screening if records become unreliable or the queue overwhelms reviewers; continue the underlying payment controls.

Make the adoption decision

Deep learning is worth considering when the practice has reliable transaction history, a defined detection gap, enough review capacity, and evidence that a pilot improves on simpler methods. Otherwise, strengthen vendor verification, payment approvals, reconciliations, and exception documentation first.

FAQs

How much data does a medical practice need for deep-learning fraud review?

There is no reliable universal minimum. Supervised models need representative, well-supported outcome labels, while anomaly models need enough history to characterize ordinary activity. Both need separate evaluation data and human-reviewed results. If outcomes are scarce or records inconsistent, simpler controls may be the better starting point.

Does a high anomaly score prove fraud?

No. It indicates that a transaction differs from the model's learned patterns according to its scoring method. A legitimate annual payment can score highly. Reviewers must examine supporting records and independently verify sensitive changes before reaching a conclusion.

Should a model automatically block or approve vendor payments?

For this workflow, keep model output advisory and payment authorization separate. An established policy may require additional verification for certain exceptions, but a score alone should neither authorize payment nor establish wrongdoing. Document who investigates, who decides on escalation, and who can release funds.

What should an owner ask a screening vendor?

Ask what the model was trained on, how labels were established, how it performs on comparable transactions, and what its scores mean. Request results against a simple baseline, explanations reviewers can verify, data-access requirements, drift monitoring procedures, and a clear process for stopping use. Do not assume that AI branding establishes suitability or compliance.
