Cross-Border Payment Rules: Compliance Checklist

November 25, 2025 · 15 min read

Managing cross-border payments is complex, but compliance is non-negotiable. Startups must navigate global regulations like KYC, AML, and FATF, while adhering to U.S.-specific rules from OFAC and FinCEN. Failure to comply can result in fines, frozen funds, and reputational damage.

Here’s how to ensure compliance:

  • Verify identities (KYC): Collect and authenticate IDs, addresses, and ownership details.
  • Monitor transactions (AML): Flag irregular patterns and report suspicious activities.
  • Stay tax compliant: File necessary forms (e.g., 1099) and track withholding obligations.
  • Adopt automated tools: Use AI-driven platforms for real-time compliance checks and record-keeping.
  • Conduct audits: Regularly review processes to identify gaps and improve systems.

With global cross-border payments projected to hit $250 trillion by 2027, startups that prioritize compliance early can avoid risks and position themselves for growth.

Key Regulatory Requirements for Cross-Border Payments

When it comes to cross-border payments, compliance is not optional - it's the backbone of your operations. Here's what you need to know about global standards and U.S.-specific regulations.

Global Compliance Standards Startups Should Know

Know Your Customer (KYC) is the cornerstone of compliance in cross-border payments. Before processing any transactions, you need to verify the identities of your customers, vendors, and contractors. This involves collecting government-issued IDs, proof of address, and business registration documents. By doing so, you reduce the risk of fraud and ensure you're not unknowingly enabling illegal activities.

Anti-Money Laundering (AML) regulations require you to keep a close eye on transactions for anything suspicious. This means setting up systems to flag irregular amounts, unusual frequencies, or unexpected destinations. When something seems off, you must file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs). Unlike a one-time setup, AML compliance is an ongoing responsibility that extends throughout your business relationships.

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) plays a central role in shaping international compliance standards. Many countries follow FATF guidelines, including the Travel Rule, which requires you to collect and share detailed information about the parties involved in each transaction. This rule applies to both traditional payments and cryptocurrency transfers.

For businesses dealing with European partners, regulations like Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2) and the Sixth Anti-Money Laundering Directive (AMLD6) come into play. PSD2, for instance, mandates Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) for certain transactions, which can directly impact how customers interact with your checkout processes. Even U.S.-based startups must comply with these rules when working with European entities.

U.S.-Specific Regulations

On top of global standards, U.S. regulations bring their own set of requirements.

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) enforces sanctions that directly affect cross-border payments. You'll need to screen all parties against the Specially Designated Nationals (SDN) list. A single violation can result in fines up to $1 million or even criminal charges.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) oversees AML compliance for U.S. businesses. Under FinCEN's rules, you must file Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs) for payments exceeding $10,000 and keep detailed records of all international transactions. FinCEN also requires you to report beneficial ownership information, which means identifying the individuals who ultimately own or control the businesses you're working with.

Tax compliance adds another layer of complexity. For instance, the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) requires Form 1099 reporting for payments made to foreign contractors. Additionally, the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) mandates reporting of foreign financial assets. If you're paying a contractor in India, for example, you'll need to ensure they're not on the SDN list and report the payment to the IRS. One U.S. startup found itself adjusting its checkout flows to meet PSD2's Strong Customer Authentication requirements when working with European partners, highlighting the global reach of these regulations.

Documentation and Reporting Requirements

Proper documentation is your best defense against audits and investigations. Here's what you need to keep in order:

  • Business registration documents: Maintain certificates of incorporation, tax IDs, and proof of good standing for all international partners. Update these records whenever changes occur.
  • Identity verification records: Go beyond collecting documents. Use systems that verify the authenticity of IDs, proofs of address, and business ownership records. Cross-check these against global databases to ensure they're not flagged as fraudulent or stolen.
  • Payment records: Keep detailed logs of every transaction, including the purpose, parties involved, amounts, exchange rates, and fees. These should be stored securely for at least five years.
  • Tax compliance documentation: Track tax obligations across jurisdictions, calculate withholding requirements, and produce the necessary forms. Payments to foreign contractors, for example, may require different tax treatments depending on their location and the nature of their services.

Compliance Checklist for Cross-Border Payments

Creating a solid compliance system takes time and effort, but following these five steps can help your startup stay compliant while keeping operations efficient.

Step 1: Verify Identities (KYC) for All Parties

Start with a thorough Know Your Customer (KYC) process. Gather government-issued IDs, proof of address, and business registration documents for all involved parties. Use digital identity verification tools to cross-check these documents against global databases for authenticity.

Modern e-KYC platforms can speed up onboarding while improving accuracy. These tools compare submitted documents with official records, helping to detect fraud early. For business clients, go beyond surface-level information - dig into beneficial ownership details to identify who truly controls the company, not just the person signing the paperwork.

Your verification process should also consider risk factors like the nature of the business relationship, expected transaction volumes, and geographic risks. Keep detailed records of all verification activities and ensure they meet regulatory retention requirements.

Step 2: Monitor Transactions for Suspicious Activities

After verifying identities, keep an eye on transactions to spot anything unusual. Use a combination of rule-based and AI-driven systems to monitor for anomalies. Large, rapid, or high-risk transfers should trigger immediate alerts.

Under FinCEN regulations, any transaction over $10,000 requires a Currency Transaction Report (CTR). But don’t stop at dollar thresholds - watch for patterns like structuring (breaking a large sum into smaller amounts), round-number transactions, or activity that doesn’t align with a customer’s stated business purpose.

When suspicious activity is flagged, act quickly. Investigate, document your findings, and file Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs) if needed. Establish clear escalation procedures so your team knows what to do and who to contact. Over time, machine learning can improve detection accuracy by learning from past cases and reducing false alarms.

Step 3: Maintain Current Tax and Registration Documents

Keep your business registration certificates, tax IDs, and tax filings up to date, as well as those of your international partners.

For every cross-border transaction, maintain detailed records like invoices, contracts, foreign exchange rates, and the purpose of the payment. When paying foreign contractors, prepare the necessary tax forms, such as Form 1099 for U.S. tax reporting, and calculate withholding taxes based on applicable treaties. These records are crucial for supporting your KYC process and transaction monitoring.

Step 4: Implement AML and CTF Policies

Develop Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Counter-Terrorist Financing (CTF) policies tailored to the risks specific to your business and customer base.

Set clear procedures for monitoring and reporting suspicious activities. Your policies should outline when to file SARs, who is authorized to do so, and how to document these decisions. Regular employee training is essential, as human judgment still plays a critical role even with automated systems.

Update your policies regularly to address new threats and regulatory changes. For example, an e-commerce platform might face different risks than a B2B software company, so tailor your approach accordingly.

Step 5: Conduct Regular Audits and Reviews

Schedule internal audits annually and external audits every two years, or as required. These audits should evaluate your KYC processes, transaction monitoring systems, documentation accuracy, and overall adherence to policies.

Focus on high-risk areas, such as onboarding new customers, high-value transactions, and payments to regions with stricter regulations. Regularly assess your technology controls to ensure they’re functioning correctly and haven’t been affected by updates or changes.

Document all audit findings, take corrective actions, and track resolutions to demonstrate compliance. Use the results to refine your processes and training programs, turning audits into opportunities for improvement instead of just routine checks.

For added diligence, consider conducting quarterly mini-audits on specific areas like transaction monitoring or documentation completeness. This proactive approach helps catch small issues before they escalate and underscores your commitment to maintaining strong compliance standards.

Standardized systems play a crucial role in simplifying compliance for cross-border payments. Overview of Interoperability Standards

One of the standout standards for cross-border payments is ISO 20022, which is set to be a key messaging framework by 2025. This global standard facilitates structured data exchange between financial institutions, ensuring that transaction details align with regulatory reporting requirements. Building on this, SWIFT gpi enhances transaction transparency, supporting Anti-Money Laundering (AML) and Know Your Customer (KYC) compliance efforts.

API-based integrations are another game-changer. These allow businesses to directly connect with payment networks, banks, and fintech platforms, enabling real-time compliance monitoring. Systems like Brazil's Pix and India's UPI highlight how API-driven solutions can deliver detailed regulatory reporting across multiple jurisdictions.

Best Practices and Risk Management

Turning compliance into a strategic advantage requires effective risk management. Startups that adopt thorough best practices not only avoid regulatory pitfalls but also establish stronger operational systems, enabling sustainable growth across global markets.

Employee Training and Security Measures

Regular compliance training is the cornerstone of any successful cross-border payment strategy. Teams should participate in quarterly sessions covering AML, KYC, sanctions screening, and transaction monitoring. These regulatory frameworks vary widely across regions, including the United States, Europe (GDPR), and Brazil (LGPD).

Security protocols must extend beyond the basics. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is now a baseline requirement. Additionally, specialized training tailored to modern payment solutions is critical.

Training programs should also address requirements like data localization and multi-jurisdictional compliance, both of which directly impact cross-border transaction handling. For instance, some countries mandate that transaction data be stored within their borders, influencing decisions about payment processors and data storage solutions. Understanding Travel Rule compliance adds another layer of complexity to cross-border transfers. These training initiatives set the stage for robust partner due diligence, which is vital when navigating diverse regulatory landscapes.

Conduct Due Diligence for Partners

Know Your Business (KYB) due diligence needs to be an ongoing effort, not a one-time process. Startups should assess partners' compliance certifications (like ISO standards), AML systems, and regulatory standing in target markets. This is especially important when expanding into regions with varying regulatory requirements, such as Lagos, São Paulo, or Jakarta.

Documenting KYB findings and maintaining records for audits is essential. When choosing payment orchestrators or infrastructure providers, evaluate their ability to handle multi-jurisdictional compliance and review their track record with compliance automation. Ensuring that partners are properly licensed and embedded in the regions they operate reduces risk and supports multi-market scalability. It's also critical to verify that partners can handle high transaction speeds without sacrificing security. Consistent due diligence not only ensures compliance but also strengthens the overall risk management framework.

FAQs

What regulations do startups need to follow for cross-border payments in the U.S. and globally?

Startups managing cross-border payments need to navigate both U.S. and international regulations to keep operations running smoothly and avoid hefty penalties. In the U.S., this means adhering to Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) sanctions, meeting Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) requirements for anti-money laundering (AML) compliance, and ensuring proper IRS reporting for international transactions. On the global front, startups working with EU customers must comply with General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), while also addressing local licensing rules in the countries where payments are handled.

One way to make compliance less daunting is by leveraging tools or services that incorporate regulatory checks directly into your payment processes. Prioritizing compliance from the beginning not only helps sidestep these issues but also strengthens trust with international partners and customers.
