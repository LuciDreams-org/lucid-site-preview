How do I know which of my physical therapy clinics is actually making money?

December 10, 2026 · 10 min read

By Lucid · Last reviewed December 10, 2026

Give each clinic its own profit and loss every month. Code each clinic's own costs, like its rent and its therapists' pay, straight to that clinic. Split shared costs, like your office manager and software, by one written rule. Then close every clinic's books the same way each month, so you can compare them side by side.

How do I split costs between my physical therapy clinics?

Code direct costs, like each clinic's rent, therapists' pay and supplies, straight to that clinic. For costs the clinics share, like an office manager or practice software, pick one written rule, such as each clinic's share of visits, and use it every month. Then you can read a profit and loss for each clinic.

A direct cost is a cost that belongs to one clinic, such as its lease. A shared cost is a cost that serves more than one clinic. An allocation is a written rule for splitting one shared cost between clinics or therapists. A location tag is a label your accounting software puts on each transaction to show which clinic it belongs to.

Example 1: splitting shared costs between two clinics. A physical therapy practice has two clinics, north and south, with one office manager for both. The practice keeps one set of books in QuickBooks Online, with a location tag for each clinic. In November, the north clinic had 1,100 visits and the south clinic 900, so the bookkeeper splits two shared costs 55% and 45%.

Shared cost November total North clinic (55%) South clinic (45%)
Office manager's pay $5,200 $2,860 $2,340
Practice software $900 $495 $405
Total $6,100 $3,355 $2,745

If the bookkeeper coded all $6,100 to the north clinic, the north clinic's profit and loss would carry $2,745 of the south clinic's costs, and the south clinic would look $2,745 more profitable than it is.

Change the allocation only at the start of a year, so you can compare one month with the next.

What does a profit and loss for each clinic show?

It shows what each clinic earned after its own costs and its share of the shared ones. A practice can look healthy in one combined report while one clinic carries the other. Compare each clinic's profit, its profit as a share of revenue and its profit per visit, month by month.

Example 2: which clinic makes the money. The same practice's November numbers, with the shared costs from Example 1. The south clinic opened last year, pays higher rent and has the same therapist payroll as the north clinic, for 200 fewer visits.

Line North clinic South clinic Both clinics
Revenue from visits $121,000 $90,000 $211,000
Therapists' pay -$62,000 -$62,000 -$124,000
Rent -$9,000 -$14,000 -$23,000
Supplies and other direct costs -$4,000 -$3,500 -$7,500
Shared costs (Example 1) -$3,355 -$2,745 -$6,100
Profit $42,645 $7,755 $50,400
Profit as a share of revenue 35% 9% 24%

In one combined report, the owner sees $50,400 of profit, about 24% of revenue. Split by clinic, the north clinic earns about $38.77 for each visit and the south clinic about $8.62. Before the owner opens a third clinic, the owner knows the south clinic needs more visits per therapist or lower rent.

How do I close every clinic's books the same way each month?

Use one month-end close checklist for every clinic, with a named person and a business day for each step. Your office manager handles the first two steps on business day 1, your bookkeeper does the reconciling and coding by business day 6, and you review each clinic's report and sign off by business day 8.

A month-end close is the set of steps you take after a month ends to check, correct and lock that month's books, so the reports you read are right. You need one whether you keep the books in QuickBooks Online, Xero or another system.

Step Who Business day
1. Post the last day's payments and close the final card batch Office manager 1
2. Pull the practice software's month-end reports for each clinic: payments, adjustments, refunds, aging Office manager or biller 1
3. Reconcile every bank account to its statement Bookkeeper 3
4. Reconcile every credit card and loan account to its statement Bookkeeper 3
5. Match each clinic's deposits to its collections: card batches, insurance payments, patient checks Bookkeeper 3
6. Record card processing fees and bank fees Bookkeeper 3
7. Code every expense to a clinic and send the owner a list of unknown charges Bookkeeper 4
8. Record payroll from the payroll company's reports, by clinic Bookkeeper 4
9. Split loan payments into principal and interest; list new equipment Bookkeeper 5
10. Split shared costs across clinics and therapists Bookkeeper 5
11. Tie receivables in the books to the aging report; review refunds and credit balances Bookkeeper with the biller 6
12. Review each clinic's report against last month, sign off and lock the month Owner, then bookkeeper 8

Business day 1 is the first working day of the next month. You'll answer the step 7 questions by business day 5. If your bookkeeper finishes a step late, move the sign-off day back rather than skip a step. For step 11 in detail, see the receivables part of the close. If you're months behind, start with how to catch up bookkeeping for a medical practice.

These are the other terms you'll see in this post:

  • A bank reconciliation is the check that every transaction on a bank statement is in your books, and every bank transaction in your books is on the statement.
  • A card batch is the group of card payments your front desk settles at the end of a day, which your card processor deposits as one amount, minus fees.
  • A remittance advice is the payer's statement listing which claims one insurance payment covers. The electronic version is called an ERA.
  • An open-items list is the list of amounts nobody has explained yet, with the person checking each one and the day it's due.
  • Locking the month is setting a closing date in your accounting software, so nobody can change that month without a password.

How do I match each clinic's deposits to my practice software's collections report?

Match by payment type, not by day. Your card processor deposits each batch minus fees, insurance payers deposit when they pay, often days after you post, and patient checks reach the bank when someone takes them. Once you've matched each type, any gap left should be fees or timing you can name.

Medicare pays claims by electronic funds transfer, and payers send the electronic remittance advice in a national format called the 835 (CMS, Electronic Funds Transfer). Your bookkeeper matches each insurance deposit to its remittance advice by payer and amount, then lists what's left: deposits with no posting, and postings with no deposit.

Example 3: matching one week of deposits for one clinic. The north clinic posts $24,530 of payments in its practice software for Monday, November 2 to Friday, November 6, 2026. The bank shows $23,100 of deposits for the same week.

Payment type Practice software Bank deposits Difference and why
Card batches $8,420 $8,170 $250 of processing fees, taken out before the deposit
Insurance payments $14,960 $13,780 $1,180 from one payer, posted Friday and deposited the next Monday
Patient checks $1,150 $1,150 $0
Total $24,530 $23,100 $1,430, all of it named

Your bookkeeper records the $250 as a card fee expense and carries the $1,180 into the next week's match. Anything still unnamed goes on the open-items list for your office manager.

How do I know when my books are done each month?

Your books are done when your bookkeeper has finished all 12 steps for every clinic, you've signed off, and someone has locked the month in the accounting software. Pick a fixed sign-off day, such as business day 8, and ask for a short note listing anything still open.

On sign-off day, ask for a reconciliation for every account, the open-items list and each clinic's report. Then read the results with how to read a P&L for your medical practice.

Save each month's statements, reconciliations, reports and open-items list. The IRS says "You must keep your records as long as needed to prove the income or deductions on a tax return" (IRS, Recordkeeping).

What do you add to the close in December?

The December close is a normal close plus a short year-end list: payroll and contractor records for Forms W-2 and 1099, a count of any product on the shelf, an updated list of equipment, and a clean-up of old open items. Your CPA will use all of it to prepare the year's tax returns.

  • Payroll and Form W-2: check each employee's name, address and year-to-date pay. For year-end bonuses, see how bonuses are taxed for practice owners.
  • Contractors and Form 1099-NEC: collect a Form W-9 from each contractor you paid, and total what you paid each one. For who gets one, see who gets a 1099 from a medical practice in 2027.
  • Product count: count braces, retail product and supplies on the shelf at each clinic on December 31.
  • Equipment list: list each piece of equipment you bought during the year, with its date, cost and clinic, so your CPA can figure depreciation (IRS, Publication 946).
  • Old open items: clear or explain every uncleared check and old deposit before you lock December.

Tax rules depend on your state and your numbers, so ask your CPA before you act on this.

Questions physical therapy owners ask about clinic profit and the month-end close

Should each physical therapy clinic have its own set of books?

Only if each clinic is its own company. If all your clinics sit in one company, keep one set of books and tag every transaction to a clinic, so you can pull a profit and loss for each. If each clinic is a separate company with its own bank account, give each one its own books and combine them in one owner report.

How do I compare a new clinic with an older one?

Compare them per visit and by months since opening, not only in the same calendar month. A new clinic pays full rent and staff before its schedule fills, so watch its trend: its profit per visit should rise month by month. Decide before you open it how many months you'll give it to reach your older clinic's numbers.

What is a month-end close?

A month-end close is the work you do after each month ends to make that month's books complete and correct, then lock them. For a practice, that means reconciling accounts, matching deposits, coding expenses and splitting shared costs. Once you've closed a month, you can compare its profit and loss with the month before.

How long should a month-end close take for a practice?

Plan on a fixed sign-off day in the first two weeks of the next month; the checklist above signs off on business day 8. The hours depend on how many accounts, locations and transactions you have. If your close often runs past the 15th, ask your bookkeeper what's holding it up.

How do I match insurance deposits to the payments in my practice software?

Match each insurance deposit to that payer's remittance advice by amount, then check that the payments posted in your practice software add up to the same total. One deposit can cover dozens of claims. If you can't find the remittance for a deposit, ask the payer or your billing company for it before anyone posts the payment.

Who should do the close: the office manager, a bookkeeper or a CPA?

Usually all three, each on different steps. Your office manager closes out payments and pulls reports. A bookkeeper does the reconciling, coding and cost splitting. A CPA reviews the year and prepares the tax returns. Split the work so the person who records payments isn't the only one who checks them.
