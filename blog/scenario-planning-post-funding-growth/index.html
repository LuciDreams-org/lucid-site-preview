Post-Funding Growth Scenarios for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Financing has closed. The next decision is how quickly your practice should commit the money. Signing every equipment order and starting every new employee immediately can leave an otherwise viable expansion short of operating cash before payer receipts arrive.

A post-funding deployment plan connects spending to actual readiness: when a location can open, when providers can begin work, when collections are expected and when financing payments become due. It also identifies which decisions remain reversible.

For owners of staffed private medical practices, the goal is not simply to spend within the approved expansion budget. It is to reach the next operating milestone without compromising existing payroll, required payments or the cash needed to support delayed collections.

Establish what is actually available after closing

Start with settled funds and final closing documents, not the financing amount shown in an earlier proposal. Confirm net proceeds, fees already deducted, permitted uses, disbursement conditions and payment obligations against the executed agreements.

A bank balance alone does not show spending capacity. Separate the following categories before approving deployment:

Cash category What to verify Deployment treatment
Restricted proceeds Contractual limits on uses, accounts or disbursements Use only for documented permitted purposes
Unrestricted operating proceeds Funds received and available for operating uses Include in the operating deployment schedule
Existing practice cash Amount needed for current payroll, vendors and other obligations Do not silently treat it as expansion funding
Previously committed amounts Unpaid invoices, deposits, signed orders and approved payroll starts Include expected payments before approving additional commitments
Owner-designated reserve Cash the owner wants protected against delays Treat as an internal spending limit, not a contractual restriction

Keep legally restricted cash distinct from an internal reserve. An owner may reconsider a management reserve after reviewing the consequences; that does not make restricted proceeds available for another purpose.

For multi-entity practices, confirm which entity received the financing and which entity owes each expense. Do not assume cash held by one entity is freely available to another. Have the CPA and relevant advisers review proposed transfers and documentation where necessary.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those basics help establish a reliable starting balance; the financing documents determine the permitted uses of proceeds.

Translate the approved expansion into dated commitments

An approved budget is not yet a deployment schedule. Each major item needs both a commitment date and a cash-payment date.

An equipment deposit may leave the bank this week, while signing the order creates a much larger obligation payable at delivery. A provider may start next month, but recruiting expenses and onboarding payroll may begin earlier. Track the full commitment even when only a deposit appears in current cash spending.

Match hiring dates to operational readiness

For each provider and administrative role, record the proposed start date, expected payroll cash dates, employer costs, benefits and onboarding expenses. Use the practice’s actual payroll assumptions rather than a generic salary multiplier.

Then connect the start date to dependencies. These may include space availability, equipment installation, payer participation status where relevant, scheduling readiness and the staff needed to operate the location.

Do not assume every hire can be delayed without consequences. Signed employment terms, notice requirements and minimum safe staffing needs can limit flexibility. Identify which start dates are still negotiable before relying on a hiring delay as a cash-saving action.

Separate opening, service and collection dates

A location opening does not mean its receipts begin that day. The schedule should distinguish:

  • When the premises and equipment are ready.
  • When providers can begin seeing patients.
  • When services can be billed through the applicable workflow.
  • When payer receipts and patient-balance payments are expected to reach the bank.

Use the practice’s collection history where comparable, adjusted for the new location, provider mix and known operational differences. Charges and accounting revenue are not substitutes for forecast cash receipts.

Use a staged post-close deployment schedule

The following is an illustrative deployment schedule, not a recommended spending amount or universal timetable. It shows how an owner might stage a new-location expansion after financing closes.

Stage Proposed commitment Cash effect to model Owner-approved pause point
Closing reconciliation Confirm proceeds, restrictions and financing schedule Establish available operating cash and separate restricted funds Do not authorize discretionary commitments until balances and uses reconcile
Site preparation Release approved buildout payments and essential deposits Record each deposit, progress payment and remaining contractual balance Review unresolved opening dependencies before signing additional noncancelable orders
Initial staffing Start the minimum provider and administrative team needed for launch Add payroll, employer costs, benefits and onboarding payments on actual payment dates Confirm readiness before finalizing start dates; honor existing commitments
Opening Accept essential equipment and begin operations Include delivery balances, occupancy costs, payroll and financing payments Delay optional purchases if the projected cash low point falls below the owner’s reserve
Collection validation Compare actual receipts with expected timing Replace estimated payer and patient receipts with actual deposits Require owner review before releasing the next hiring or equipment stage
Further expansion Add capacity supported by demand and cash Include the full cost of the next commitment, not just its deposit Proceed only if the revised forecast preserves required payments and the approved reserve

Assign a responsible person to each gate. The practice manager can report readiness, the finance team can update cash effects, and the owner can approve or pause discretionary commitments.

Approval should record the decision, the current forecast and the condition for reconsideration. “Wait until collections improve” is too vague. “Reassess after actual receipts support the next payroll cycle and the revised forecast remains above the reserve” is actionable.

See how delayed collections change deployment without changing funding

Consider an illustrative example of a practice opening another location after receiving $600,000 in financing:

  • $200,000 is restricted to specified buildout costs.
  • $400,000 is available for the expansion’s operating deployment.
  • The owner sets a $150,000 minimum operating reserve for this expansion.
  • Existing locations’ operating cash is kept outside this example.

The restricted buildout account pays $120,000 in Month 1 and $80,000 in Month 2. Those payments do not appear in the operating table below, and those funds cannot cover an operating shortfall under the assumed restrictions.

The operating schedule includes the new location’s payroll, occupancy, operating purchases and assigned financing payments. Month 4 also includes a proposed $60,000 discretionary capacity addition that has not yet been committed.

All amounts in this table are illustrative.

Operating cash movement Month 1 Month 2 Month 3 Month 4
Base-case receipts $0 $40,000 $100,000 $130,000
Delayed-collection receipts $0 $10,000 $50,000 $110,000
Planned cash payments, both cases $100,000 $100,000 $110,000 $170,000
Base-case ending operating cash $300,000 $240,000 $230,000 $190,000
Delayed-collection ending operating cash $300,000 $210,000 $150,000 $90,000

Both cases start with the same $400,000 of operating cash. Funding has not changed. The collection schedule has.

In the base case, the Month 4 capacity addition leaves $190,000, above the owner’s reserve. In the delayed-collection case, the same spending plan leaves only $90,000.

If the owner pauses the uncommitted $60,000 addition, delayed-case Month 4 payments fall to $110,000 and ending cash remains at $150,000. That preserves the illustrative reserve but leaves no cushion above it. The owner should review the following weeks before releasing the paused purchase.

Monthly ending balances can also conceal a shortfall before a large receipt arrives. During deployment, review weekly balances around payroll, equipment payments and financing due dates.

Set distinct responses for readiness and collection changes

Not every adverse scenario calls for the same response. An opening delay changes the value of starting staff or accepting equipment. A collection delay may occur after those commitments are already unavoidable.

Post-close development Financial consequence Deployment response
Opening is delayed Rent, existing commitments and financing obligations may continue before services begin Re-sequence negotiable start dates and deliveries; calculate costs that continue regardless
Collections arrive later than planned Payroll and vendors may be paid before corresponding cash receipts Pause uncommitted capacity additions and revise expected receipt dates
Equipment delivery slips A deposit may already be paid while staffing or occupancy costs continue Review contract terms and readiness before adding dependent spending
Receipts arrive faster than planned Cash rises, but the improvement may be temporary Confirm sustained collections and remaining obligations before accelerating deployment
Financing payments differ from the model Required outflows change independently of opening progress Correct the forecast using the actual agreement and current payment information

Include principal, interest and other required financing payments on their cash dates. Do not assume an opening delay postpones them. For variable-rate obligations, use the agreement’s terms and lender information rather than an unsupported interest-rate prediction.

A pause should target spending that is genuinely optional or not yet committed. It is not permission to withhold payroll, miss contractual payments or operate without necessary staff and equipment.

Run a short deployment review until cash stabilizes

During active deployment, hold a weekly review focused on decisions due before the next meeting. Refresh the forecast immediately when a significant start date, opening date, receipt expectation or payment obligation changes.

The review packet should show:

  1. Reconciled cash by account and entity, with restricted balances separated.
  2. Committed but unpaid spending, including deposit balances and signed contracts.
  3. Expected receipts, split between payer collections and patient payments where useful.
  4. Upcoming payroll and financing payments, on their expected bank dates.
  5. The lowest projected cash balance, not just the period-end balance.
  6. Decisions requiring owner approval, with the cash effect of proceeding or pausing.

After each monthly close, compare the new location’s P&L with its cash deployment. Use a consistent approach to shared administrative costs so a location does not appear stronger simply because central expenses were omitted. Keep profitability analysis separate from cash: equipment purchases, debt principal and collection timing affect cash differently from reported operating profit.

Investigate receipt misses rather than automatically shifting them into the next period. A timing delay, an unresolved billing issue and a balance unlikely to be collected require different forecast treatment. Use aggregated financial reports wherever possible; patient-identifiable detail generally is not needed for an owner’s deployment decision.

Keep the next commitment conditional on current facts

The practical output of post-funding scenario planning is a controlled release of commitments—not another presentation of the expansion budget.

Before each major approval, confirm that the use is permitted, the operational dependency is ready, the full obligation is recorded and the updated cash forecast supports it. When collections move, deployment should move with them, even if the original funding amount remains intact.

FAQs

Can a practice use all financing proceeds immediately after closing?

Not necessarily. Review restrictions, disbursement conditions, existing commitments and required payments first. Even unrestricted proceeds may need to support payroll and operating costs while the expansion waits for collections. Available cash and prudent spending capacity are different amounts.

Should hiring wait until the new location collects revenue?

Not always. Essential staff may need to start before opening or before receipts arrive. Budget that lead time explicitly. Stage additional roles around readiness, demand and cash capacity, and check employment commitments before assuming a start date can change.

What if the expansion stays within budget but collections are late?

A project can remain within its total budget and still face a cash shortage. Move receipts to realistic collection dates, preserve required payments and test the resulting weekly cash low point. Pause uncommitted spending when necessary rather than treating expected revenue as available cash.

When should an owner release a paused spending stage?

Release it when the reason for the pause has been resolved and the updated forecast supports the full commitment. Confirm operational readiness, actual collection performance, remaining contractual payments and the owner-approved reserve. A higher bank balance on one day is not enough by itself.
