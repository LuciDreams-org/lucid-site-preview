Financial Data Accuracy Checks for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Before a private practice owner uses a monthly financial report to approve hiring, compare locations or consider an owner distribution, someone needs to confirm that the underlying data is complete and correctly classified. A polished profit and loss statement can still omit a payroll run, count a bank transfer as revenue or place one location’s expenses in another location’s results.

Financial data accuracy checks are the review between posting transactions and releasing reports. They answer a practical question: Are these numbers dependable enough for the decision we are about to make?

For a staffed medical practice, that review should cover reporting cutoffs, reconciled cash, complete payroll, collections versus charges, duplicate bills, entity coding and shared-cost allocations. It should end with documented exceptions and a clear sign-off—not simply an exported spreadsheet.

Set the Reporting Rules Before Checking the Numbers

Start each close with a defined reporting period, accounting basis and scope. Without those rules, two accurate source reports can appear to conflict because they measure different things.

Confirm:

  • Period: Which month or other reporting window is being closed?
  • Accounting basis: Are the financial statements prepared on a cash, accrual or other CPA-approved basis?
  • Scope: Which legal entities, bank accounts and practice locations belong in the report?
  • Source dates: Were payroll, collections and accounting reports extracted for comparable periods?
  • Status: Is the report preliminary, final or final with disclosed exceptions?

Keep management schedules clearly labeled when they use a different basis from the financial statements. For example, a production report based on service dates should not be presented as collected revenue.

The IRS guide to business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. Those fundamentals support the review, but they do not replace practice-specific checks on payroll, collections and reporting dimensions.

A Pre-Report Accuracy Checklist

Use this checklist before distributing the monthly reporting package. Assign each check to a named person; the roles below are examples rather than a required staffing model.

Check Evidence to review Release condition Typical owner
Reporting cutoff Source report dates and close checklist Period and accounting treatment are consistent Bookkeeper or accountant
Cash and credit cards Reconciliations and outstanding-item lists Differences are explained and supported Bookkeeper, reviewed by accountant

1. Check Cutoffs and Missing Activity

A cutoff review asks whether transactions appear in the appropriate period under the practice’s accounting basis. It is not just a check of invoice dates.

Review activity around month-end for payer settlements, patient-payment batches, payroll, vendor bills, refunds and recurring expenses. Look for source reports that stop before the end of the period or transactions posted after the initial export.

Practical checks include:

  • Confirm that every bank and credit card account has activity through the reporting period’s end.
  • Check whether the final payroll run and related entries have been posted.
  • Review bills received after month-end for expenses that may belong to the period under the applicable accounting method.
  • Confirm that recurring items such as rent and software costs are present, without assuming that every recurring expense should be identical each month.
  • Review late adjustments and identify which reports need to be rerun.

Ask the CPA how to handle timing differences rather than moving transactions simply to make monthly results look smoother. If a material source is still missing, label the report preliminary and identify the affected line items.

2. Verify Reconciled Cash, Then Inspect the Exceptions

A reconciled cash balance is a prerequisite for reliable reporting, but the accuracy review goes beyond confirming that a reconciliation was completed.

Check that the reconciliation uses the correct statement ending balance and covers the full reporting period. Then inspect unresolved items: old outstanding checks, deposits in transit, uncategorized transactions and manual adjustments made to force a match.

Pay particular attention to transactions that can inflate income or expenses when incorrectly coded:

  • Transfers between practice-owned accounts
  • Loan proceeds and principal repayments
  • Owner contributions and distributions
  • Credit card payments already represented by recorded card expenses
  • Refunds or reversed deposits

A balance can reconcile while its transactions remain misclassified. For example, a transfer from the operating account to a payroll account should not create a second payroll expense. Review classification as well as the ending balance.

3. Confirm Complete Provider and Administrative Payroll

Payroll completeness means more than finding the bank withdrawal. The withdrawal may combine net pay, taxes and fees, while the financial reports need appropriate expense and liability classifications.

Compare payroll registers with the ledger for every payroll run in scope, including off-cycle runs, bonuses, corrections and voids. Review provider compensation, administrative wages, employer payroll taxes, benefits and payroll service fees separately where the reporting structure requires them.

For an accrual-basis report, ask the accountant whether wages earned but not yet paid require an accrual. For any basis, verify that payroll-related liabilities and subsequent payments are handled consistently.

Illustrative example: A practice’s payroll register shows $84,000 in gross wages, but the ledger shows only $62,000 categorized as wages because the entry captured net pay alone. The review should trace the complete payroll entry, including withholdings, employer costs and liabilities, rather than adding an unexplained adjustment to wages.

If staff work across locations, check their location assignments before using payroll totals to compare site profitability. Contractor payments should also be reviewed for completeness, with classification questions referred to the appropriate adviser rather than resolved through bookkeeping assumptions.

4. Distinguish Charges, Collections and Bank Deposits

Medical-practice reporting often combines measures that should remain distinct:

Measure What it represents Accuracy concern
Charges Amounts billed for services Not equivalent to cash received or necessarily recognized revenue
Collections Payer and patient receipts recorded in the relevant source system Dates, reversals and definitions may differ from the ledger
Bank deposits Funds posted to a bank account May include transfers, financing or other non-revenue items
Patient balances Amounts recorded as due from patients Not the same as cash available to spend

Compare collections summaries with settlement information and bank deposits using consistent dates. Investigate timing differences, payment-processing fees, refunds, recoupments and unapplied receipts where relevant.

Illustrative example: A payment processor reports $18,000 in patient receipts, withholds $400 in fees and deposits $17,600. Under an accounting treatment that records gross receipts and processing fees separately, posting only the net deposit as revenue would omit both part of the receipts and the fee expense.

Do not force collections to equal charges. Instead, document what each report measures and explain the bridge between receipt records, settlements and the ledger. A collection amount recorded against an outstanding receivable also needs different treatment from a new revenue entry; otherwise, revenue can be counted twice.

5. Find Duplicate Bills and Missing Expenses

Duplicate expenses can arise when a vendor invoice is entered manually and its payment is later recorded as another expense. A vendor statement can also be mistaken for a new bill even though the underlying invoices are already recorded.

Search for repeated combinations of vendor, invoice number, amount and date. Review similar vendor names, recurring bills with unexpected increases and invoices marked paid more than once. A possible match is a prompt to investigate, not permission to delete an entry.

Use the original invoice and payment record to determine whether each entry is a bill, payment, credit or duplicate. Also check the opposite problem: paid expenses that remain uncategorized, bills received but not entered and vendor credits that have not been applied appropriately.

For purchases such as equipment, ask the accountant to confirm the treatment rather than automatically placing the entire payment in a routine operating-expense category.

6. Check Legal Entities and Practice Locations Separately

An entity error and a location error are not the same. A legal entity identifies the business to which a transaction belongs; a location is a management reporting dimension within the practice’s structure.

Review bank-account ownership, invoice addressees, payroll records and contracts when resolving entity questions. Do not move an expense between entities just because one has more available cash or because the change improves its reported profit.

For multi-entity owners, confirm that intercompany amounts are supported and agree between the relevant books. If a combined management report is prepared, have the accountant address internal transactions so they do not distort the combined results.

For location reporting:

  • Find transactions with blank or invalid location codes.
  • Check providers and staff who changed locations or work across sites.
  • Confirm that directly attributable rent, supplies and other expenses reach the correct location.
  • Separate unallocated shared costs from genuinely missing coding.

Resolve material coding gaps before ranking locations by profitability.

7. Review Shared-Cost Allocations

Shared administration, central billing support and common software costs can change how each location’s performance appears. An allocation should have a documented purpose and a reasonable driver—not simply produce the desired result.

Shared cost Possible allocation approach Review question
Administrative staff Documented time or another supported workload measure Does the driver reflect where the work benefits the practice?
Shared software Users, licenses or documented usage Does the allocation match the cost structure?
Common occupancy costs Supported space usage Are dedicated and shared areas treated consistently?
Central management costs A documented management-reporting method Can the owner distinguish site performance before and after allocation?

These are possible approaches, not universal rules. Select the method with the finance team, apply it consistently and explain changes. Reconcile the allocation schedule to the original expense total so costs are neither lost nor counted twice.

Where useful, show location results before and after shared costs. That helps owners distinguish a site's direct operating performance from the effects of the allocation method.

8. Resolve Exceptions and Obtain Sign-Off

The final review should identify errors that individual transaction checks can miss. Compare the completed reports with prior periods and operational context. Investigate unexpected changes rather than assuming every variance is wrong.

Examples include payroll falling while staffing is unchanged, a location showing no rent, or revenue rising because loan proceeds were misclassified.

Maintain a short exception log:

Exception Potential reporting effect Required next step
Missing payroll entry Expenses may be understated Obtain register and post reviewed entry
Unexplained deposit Revenue or liabilities may be misstated Identify source and correct classification
Unallocated shared expense Location margins may be distorted Complete and review allocation
Unresolved settlement difference Receipts may be incomplete or misclassified Trace settlement and document resolution

The preparer should confirm completion, and a separate reviewer should inspect material corrections where staffing permits. If full separation is impractical, arrange a compensating review by the owner or outside accountant.

Sign-off should record the reporting period, reviewer, release date, unresolved items and decisions those items could affect. Do not present a report as final merely because its totals look plausible. If an unresolved error could change a hiring, distribution or financing decision, defer that decision or clearly explain the limitation.

Keep the Review Focused on Financial Data

Most of these checks can use summarized collections, payroll totals, vendor records and account-level schedules. Avoid including patient-identifiable information when it is unnecessary for the financial task.

The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains a standard with exceptions; its application depends on the circumstances. Coordinate information-sharing procedures with the practice’s privacy lead and advisers. Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

Make Accuracy Review Part of Every Reporting Cycle

A useful reporting package needs more than clean formatting. It needs consistent cutoffs, supported balances, complete payroll, explained receipt differences and reviewed entity and location coding.
