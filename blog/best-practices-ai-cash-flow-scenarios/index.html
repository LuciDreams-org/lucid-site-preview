Validating AI Cash Flow Scenarios for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An AI-generated cash scenario can balance mathematically and still be unsafe to use. It may start with an unreconciled bank balance, treat billed charges as collections, or leave out a scheduled loan payment. For a staffed medical practice, those errors can turn an apparently affordable owner distribution into a payroll funding problem.

Before approving a distribution, equipment payment, hiring commitment or other use of cash, require a review of the scenario's mechanics. The question is not whether the output looks reasonable. It is whether the practice can trace the opening cash, expected receipts and scheduled payments to supporting records—and explain the assumptions that connect them.

This workflow focuses on validating an existing scenario. It does not prescribe a universal cash reserve or replace the owner's approval process.

1. Define the decision and freeze the review version

Start with a short decision statement: what commitment is being considered, which entity would pay it, when payment would occur and what period the scenario covers.

A distribution from one practice entity should not be justified by a combined balance that includes another entity's cash. Similarly, a location-level profit report does not establish how much cash is available in the entity's bank account.

Save a dated review version containing:

  • The proposed commitment and payment date.
  • Included entities, locations and bank accounts.
  • The cutoff date for actual transactions.
  • Source-file dates and the scenario's assumptions.
  • The preparer's name and the designated reviewer.

Separate actual activity through the cutoff from projected activity afterward. Otherwise, a receipt that has already cleared may appear both in beginning cash and in forecast collections.

If an assumption or source changes during review, update the version and rerun the affected checks. Approval should attach to a specific scenario, not an evolving file.

2. Reconcile beginning cash before reviewing the forecast

The first number deserves as much scrutiny as the final balance. Establish beginning cash by account and entity, reconciling bank activity to the accounting records at the same cutoff.

The IRS business-recordkeeping guidance in Publication 583 supports keeping complete business records, maintaining separate business accounts and reconciling bank records. Those records provide a foundation for this review; they do not establish that a particular forecast is correct.

Opening-cash check Supporting records What to resolve
Bank and book balances agree after reconciliation Bank statements or activity and general ledger Unrecorded fees, deposits, payments or stale reconciling items
Outstanding payments receive consistent treatment Reconciliation and payment register Whether a payment reduces opening available cash or appears as a future outflow—not both
Deposits in transit are not counted twice Deposit records and subsequent bank activity Whether an expected deposit is already included in opening cash
Account ownership is clear Account list and entity ledger Whether the paying entity actually holds the cash
Reserved or restricted amounts are identified Reserve schedule and applicable account documentation Whether money included in bank cash is unavailable for the proposed use

Distinguish total bank cash from cash available for the decision. A tax reserve held in an operating account still contributes to the bank balance, but it should not silently become available for a distribution.

3. Test collection timing, not just revenue totals

A medical practice's charges, recognized revenue and bank receipts are different measures. An AI scenario should not convert a production target or monthly P&L revenue line directly into spendable cash without a supported timing assumption.

Review expected payer receipts and patient collections separately where their timing differs. Use aggregate information that supports financial analysis without unnecessary patient identifiers.

Ask the reviewer to check:

  • Starting receivables: Are expected collections tied to existing balances, future services or both? Is the same balance included twice?
  • Expected collectibility: Does the scenario distinguish gross charges from amounts reasonably expected to be collected?
  • Receipt timing: Are assumptions consistent with the practice's recent deposit patterns and known payer delays?
  • Patient balances: Are payment-plan installments treated as scheduled receipts rather than immediate collection of the entire balance?
  • Adjustments: Are known refunds, recoupments and collection interruptions reflected where relevant?

Historical averages are a reasonableness check, not proof that a particular deposit will arrive on a particular day. If a commitment depends on an uncertain receipt, identify that dependency explicitly. The owner may decide to wait until the deposit clears rather than approve payment against an assumption.

Keep assumptions coherent. A scenario should not delay payer receipts while leaving the same delayed money available elsewhere in the cash calculation.

4. Verify payment dates and search for omitted liabilities

A monthly expense estimate is not a payment calendar. A practice can finish a month with positive projected cash and still lack funds on a payroll debit date.

Trace material outflows to schedules, invoices and other source records. Review the earliest cash movement associated with each obligation, including payroll funding requirements, rather than relying only on the expense month.

Cash item Review evidence Common scenario error
Provider and administrative payroll Payroll register, funding notice and pay calendar Using an average expense instead of actual funding dates
Payroll-related taxes and benefits Payment schedules and supporting records Assuming these are included when the model contains only net wages
Debt payments Current lender schedule and notices Including interest expense but omitting principal or a scheduled larger payment
Rent and shared costs Leases, invoices and allocation schedules Counting an allocated expense without identifying which entity pays it
Equipment purchases Approved agreement and payment terms Treating depreciation expense as the cash payment
Tax payments and reserves CPA-reviewed payment and reserve schedule Omitting a known payment or treating reserved cash as freely available
Owner distributions Proposed authorization and payment date Testing cash before, rather than after, the distribution

Search beyond the P&L. Review balance-sheet liabilities, unpaid bills, approved purchases, lender notices and previously authorized payments. An obligation can require cash without appearing as a current-period expense.

For tax amounts and timing, use the practice's current CPA-reviewed schedule. Do not let an AI-generated estimate silently replace it.

5. Check the arithmetic and the difference between profit and cash

For each time interval, independently recompute:

Beginning cash + cash receipts − cash payments = ending cash.

The next interval must begin with the preceding interval's ending balance. Then test whether the model correctly handles items that affect profit and cash differently:

  • Collecting an existing receivable brings in cash without necessarily creating new current-period revenue.
  • Paying loan principal uses cash without being an operating expense.
  • Depreciation reduces accounting profit but is not itself a cash payment.
  • Equipment purchases and owner distributions use cash but are not ordinary operating expenses.
  • Loan proceeds increase cash but are not operating revenue.

Check subtotals, signs, formulas and date assignments outside the AI's narrative explanation. A confident explanation does not establish that its calculations are correct.

For multiple entities, match both sides of intercompany transfers and eliminate them from consolidated receipts and payments. A transfer can improve one entity's balance without adding cash to the practice group. Do not assume the transfer is available or authorized merely because the model includes it.

Illustrative example: a healthy-looking scenario misses a debt payment

The following is a synthetic, illustrative example—not a client result or a recommended reserve level. All amounts and circumstances are invented.

A practice owner is considering a $35,000 distribution. The AI-generated four-week scenario starts with reconciled cash of $120,000 and projects $100,000 in collections. It includes $130,000 in payroll, rent, benefits and other payments.

The model reports $55,000 remaining after the distribution. During review, the practice manager finds a $60,000 scheduled principal payment due within the same period. It appears on the lender's schedule but was absent from the expense-based inputs.

Four-week cash calculation Original scenario Corrected scenario
Beginning cash $120,000 $120,000
Expected collections $100,000 $100,000
Payroll and other included payments ($130,000) ($130,000)
Scheduled principal payment Omitted ($60,000)
Cash before proposed distribution $90,000 $30,000
Proposed distribution ($35,000) ($35,000)
Ending cash $55,000 ($5,000)

The original arithmetic was correct for its inputs. The inputs were incomplete.

The reviewer should reject the original version for approval purposes, add the liability and inspect dated cash movements. Even the corrected period-end balance does not reveal whether cash runs short earlier, when payroll and debt payments occur before collections.

Removing the proposed distribution would leave a positive period-end balance in this illustration, but that alone would not establish that another distribution is appropriate. The owner still needs to review the lowest dated balance, uncertain receipts, designated reserves and other commitments.

6. Apply a scenario acceptance checklist

Use an explicit acceptance checklist rather than an informal “looks good” response. A material unresolved item should block reliance on the scenario until corrected or bounded by a documented condition.

Acceptance test Evidence required
Scope and cutoff are clear Named entity, accounts, decision date and actual-versus-projected boundary
Beginning cash reconciles Reconciliation with explained outstanding items
Receipts are supportable Collection assumptions tied to records and recent receipt patterns
Payment timing is complete Payroll, debt, bills, taxes and approved commitments checked against schedules
Profit and cash are separated Principal, capital spending, noncash expenses and distributions treated correctly
Reserves receive consistent treatment No double deduction and no silent release of designated cash
Calculations roll forward correctly Independent arithmetic check and matching opening/closing balances
Timing risk is visible Lowest projected dated balance and receipt dependencies identified
Proposed commitment is included Correct amount, paying entity and payment date
Review is documented Version, reviewer, findings, conditions and decision recorded

Do not invent a single minimum balance for every practice. Test against the practice's documented liquidity requirements and known obligations, with uncertainty made visible to the owner.

7. Document approval and refresh it when facts change

The preparer should assemble the source packet and resolve exceptions. A designated reviewer—such as the practice's finance lead, CPA or CFO adviser—should check the mechanics. The authorized owner or manager then decides whether to approve the commitment.

A useful approval note records the scenario version, source cutoff, proposed payment, material assumptions, reviewer findings and any conditions. A conditional approval might require confirmation that an expected receipt has cleared before releasing funds.

Refresh the review when material facts change: payroll is revised, a payer deposit is delayed, a new liability arrives or the proposed payment date moves. Keep the approved version so later actual-versus-projected differences can be investigated.

Use financial summaries with unnecessary patient information removed. The HHS minimum necessary guidance provides context for limiting protected health information where that standard applies. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool.

FAQs

Can an owner approve a distribution because projected cash stays positive?

Not on that fact alone. Confirm that beginning cash reconciles, all material liabilities and reserves are addressed, and the proposed distribution appears on its actual payment date. Review the lowest dated balance, not just the period-end total, and document approval against the practice's own liquidity requirements.

What if the scenario agrees with the P&L?

Agreement with the P&L is not enough. A cash scenario must also account for collection timing, loan principal, equipment purchases, distributions and other movements that differ from accounting profit. Reconcile the forecast to bank records, balance-sheet items and payment schedules as well.

Who should approve an AI-generated cash scenario?

Assign a preparer, a reviewer responsible for checking the mechanics and an authorized decision-maker. The reviewer should document the version checked, supporting records, unresolved exceptions and any approval conditions. AI-generated commentary is not a substitute for that review.

When should the practice recheck an approved scenario?

Recheck it when a material receipt, payment, opening balance or commitment changes. If approval depends on a deposit clearing or a liability being confirmed, verify that condition before releasing funds. Preserve the earlier version and record why the decision changed.
