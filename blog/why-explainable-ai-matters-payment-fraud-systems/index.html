Explainable AI for Medical Practice Payment Fraud Reviews

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

An AI-generated payment-fraud alert should start a review, not settle it. Before staff hold, verify, or release a payment, they need to know which records triggered the alert, what looks unusual, what information is missing, and who has authority to decide.

For a staffed private medical practice, this is a practical accounts-payable issue. A changed bank account for a supply vendor may be legitimate—or may redirect practice funds. An urgent equipment deposit may be authorized—or may bypass normal approval. A vague “high risk” label does not tell the practice manager which situation they face.

Explainability means making the alert understandable and testable against source records. It does not mean proving fraud. The owner's job is to establish what evidence staff need and preserve independent payment approval while the alert is reviewed.

This article focuses on interpreting alerts and documenting human decisions. It is not a software ranking, a guide to building a fraud-detection model, or a cross-border payment-control checklist.

What an actionable payment alert must contain

A useful explanation connects a specific concern to a specific record. “Vendor bank account changed” is more actionable than “unusual payment behavior,” but it still needs supporting detail: which vendor record changed, when, and how the proposed destination differs from the previously verified destination.

Set a minimum evidence standard before staff rely on AI-generated alerts:

Required element What the reviewer should see Why it matters
Payment identity Vendor, invoice reference, amount, paying entity, location, and scheduled payment date Prevents reviewing the wrong transaction or entity
Source evidence References to the invoice, vendor record, request message, approvals, and relevant payment history Lets staff check the explanation against actual records
Understandable reason The observed difference and the comparison used Explains what needs investigation
Missing context Unavailable records, incomplete history, or conflicting information Shows where the alert may be unreliable
Verification needed A concrete question and an independent way to answer it Turns an alert into a review task
Decision record Reviewer, approver, action, rationale, and resolution Separates the model's suggestion from the human decision

A risk score can supplement this information, but it cannot replace it. Do not interpret an unexplained score as the probability that fraud occurred. Ask what the score represents, which records were evaluated, and whether the explanation reflects the actual alert logic rather than a plausible narrative generated afterward.

If the tool cannot expose supporting records, staff should document that limitation. A confident explanation without verifiable evidence is not a sound basis for releasing funds—or accusing a vendor.

Synthetic example: a vendor changes its bank account

Illustrative example: All names, record references, amounts, and circumstances below are synthetic administrative records. They contain no patient information.

A two-location practice receives a request to change the payment destination for Harbor Office Supply. An invoice for $4,800 is awaiting payment from the practice entity that operates the north location.

Administrative record Information available to the reviewer
Vendor record V-042 Previously verified account ending 1842; proposed account ending 9076
Change request CR-118 Email requesting new banking instructions before the next payment
Invoice INV-630 $4,800 for office supplies; amount agrees with the approved invoice
Prior payment register Previous payments went to the account ending 1842
Approval record Invoice approved; bank-account change not yet independently verified

An actionable alert would say:

The proposed payment destination differs from the previously verified vendor account. The account-change request is supported only by the incoming email, and no independent verification is recorded. Verify the change before releasing payment to the new destination.

This explanation identifies an observable discrepancy. It does not establish that the email is fraudulent. The vendor may have changed banks legitimately.

What staff should do next

Keep the proposed bank change and affected payment pending under the practice's exception procedure. Contact the vendor through a previously established phone number or another independently verified channel—not a number supplied only in the change request. Confirm the change with an authorized vendor contact and record how that person's identity and authority were established.

The employee updating the vendor record should not also provide the sole approval for releasing the payment. A separate authorized approver should review the verification evidence and the payment details.

If the change is confirmed, document the evidence, obtain the required approval, and use the verified destination. If verification fails or the vendor denies requesting the change, do not release funds to the disputed account; escalate through the practice's established process. An unanswered call means the issue remains unresolved, not that fraud is confirmed.

Synthetic example: an unusual urgent payment request

Illustrative example: All records and amounts in this scenario are synthetic.

An administrator receives a message requesting an urgent $12,600 deposit for replacement equipment. The request names a familiar supplier but asks for payment outside the normal accounts-payable process. The AI alert cites an unusually large amount and urgent wording.

Those reasons are incomplete. A legitimate equipment deposit may be larger than recurring supply invoices, and urgency alone does not establish misconduct.

Alert observation Context the reviewer needs Useful verification step
Amount exceeds routine vendor payments Is this an equipment purchase rather than a recurring expense? Compare with the approved quote or purchase authorization
Message requests immediate payment Is there a documented operational deadline? Confirm with the responsible manager through an established channel
Normal approval route is missing Was an authorized exception approved elsewhere? Locate the approval or obtain it under the exception policy
Supplier name is familiar Is the payment destination also verified? Compare payee details with the verified vendor record

Suppose the practice manager finds an approved equipment quote and independently confirms the request with the authorized owner and supplier contact. The payment may be legitimate, but it still needs normal payment authorization. The alert did not grant approval, and the explanation did not eliminate the need to verify the destination.

If the quote cannot be found, record that gap and keep the request pending. Do not turn “missing documentation” into “confirmed fraud.” The operational question is whether sufficient evidence exists to authorize this payment now.

Use an alert-review form that separates facts from conclusions

The review record should preserve the original alert and show what the human reviewer added. This makes it possible to distinguish a model error, a documentation gap, and a genuinely unauthorized request later.

Use the following form within the practice's approved recordkeeping process. Reference supporting documents rather than copying unnecessary sensitive information into the form.

Field Information to capture
Review reference and timing Internal case reference; alert time; review time; next follow-up date
Payment details Vendor, invoice reference, amount, due date, paying entity, and location or shared-cost designation
Current payment status Not submitted, pending review, scheduled, released, rejected, or bank confirmation pending
Original alert Exact alert text, reason codes, and score if provided; tool or rule version if available
Source evidence Document references, record dates, relevant fields, and where authorized staff can retrieve them
Understandable reasons What changed or differed, the comparison used, and why it requires review
Known limitations Missing invoices, incomplete history, stale vendor data, or unverified assumptions
Independent verification Established contact channel, person contacted, authority checked, time, and confirmation obtained
Reviewer assessment Facts confirmed, facts contradicted, and questions still open
Reviewer decision Hold, seek additional verification, recommend release, or reject the request; include rationale
Separate approval Authorized approver, decision, time, and any approved exception
Resolution Final payment status, verified destination reference, payment or bank reference if applicable, and unresolved follow-up
Learning note Confirmed legitimate exception, data-quality issue, suspected unauthorized request, or outcome still unknown

Avoid storing complete bank credentials in a general review log when a masked account reference and a controlled document reference will suffice. Do not include patient names, clinical notes, or patient-level billing records in these vendor-payment examples or reviews.

Preserve separate duties when the alert reaches a person

AI does not replace the practice's approval structure. Neither the employee who receives an alert nor the person who edits vendor details should gain additional payment authority simply because a tool recommends release.

Define responsibilities before the next exception occurs:

  • Payment preparer: Collects the invoice, coding, entity, and approval records.
  • Reviewer: Checks the alert against source evidence and performs independent verification.
  • Authorized approver: Decides whether the evidence supports release under practice policy.
  • Payment releaser: Executes the approved payment and checks the destination against the authorized record.
  • Reconciliation reviewer: Confirms what actually cleared and follows up on unresolved differences.

A smaller practice may combine some roles, but it should preserve an independent approval check for sensitive vendor changes and payment exceptions. Document backup coverage so urgency or staff absence does not silently remove that check.

For multi-entity owners, verification must include the paying entity. A valid invoice paid from the wrong entity's bank account is still a financial-control problem, even when no fraud occurred.

Handle false positives and missing context without weakening controls

A false positive is a legitimate activity that was flagged as suspicious. Common sources of misleading alerts include a newly opened location with little payment history, a one-time equipment purchase, or a verified vendor bank change that has not reached every record.

An explanation helps staff investigate these situations; it does not guarantee fewer false positives. An alert can also rely on accurate data but use an inappropriate comparison—for example, comparing an equipment deposit with monthly office-supply purchases.

Review recurring exceptions for patterns:

  • Are alerts driven by incomplete or outdated vendor records?
  • Are transactions being compared within the correct entity and expense category?
  • Are confirmed legitimate exceptions repeatedly flagged for the same reason?
  • Are staff overriding alerts without evidence because the queue is burdensome?

Correct source data and consider narrowly defined rule changes through an authorized review process. Do not remove independent verification of bank changes merely because earlier changes proved legitimate. Likewise, an absence of alerts does not establish that a payment is safe.

Connect the review to cash planning and bookkeeping

A payment hold affects expected cash timing, but it is not necessarily a bank transaction. Keep the payment queue, cash forecast, accounts payable, and bank reconciliation consistent with what actually happened.

For example, if an approved invoice remains unpaid during verification, do not describe it as a cleared bank payment. If a submitted payment has uncertain status, obtain bank confirmation before assuming cancellation or creating a replacement that could duplicate it.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete business records and bank reconciliation. Those principles support retaining the invoice, approval, verification evidence, and final payment status together; they do not establish that an AI explanation proves fraud.

Keep reviews limited to necessary administrative information. If a proposed workflow would involve protected health information, assess it before using a new service. HHS guidance on business associates explains why accounting services involving PHI can raise additional responsibilities. Do not put patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool.

Set the standard before releasing funds

The practical test for an alert is straightforward: can an authorized reviewer trace the concern to evidence, verify the disputed facts independently, and explain the payment decision?

Require that record before resolving the exception. Keep “flagged,” “unverified,” and “confirmed unauthorized” distinct. A useful explanation supports human judgment; it never substitutes for evidence or separate approval.

FAQs

Does an understandable AI explanation prove payment fraud?

No. It describes why a system flagged a payment. The underlying data may be incomplete, the comparison may be unsuitable, or the unusual activity may be legitimate. Staff must verify the facts and record their decision separately from the alert.

Should every alert automatically stop a payment?

Not necessarily. Establish hold and escalation rules in advance based on the concern and available evidence. An unverified destination change warrants different handling from a routine amount variation. Any automatic hold should have a named reviewer, a follow-up deadline, and a documented route to resolution.

What if the tool provides only a risk score?

Request the source records, understandable reasons, comparison basis, and known limitations. Until those are available, treat the score as an unsupported signal rather than a complete review. Staff can still verify the payment using the practice's established controls.

Who can release a payment after an alert is cleared?

The person authorized under the practice's payment policy, with the required independent approval. Clearing an alert resolves a concern; it does not grant payment authority or replace invoice approval, destination verification, or reconciliation.
