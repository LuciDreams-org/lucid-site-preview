Equity Dilution and Staff Retention in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Offering ownership can help connect a key employee’s financial interests to a medical practice’s long-term success. But an additional ownership award also changes what existing owners receive, may affect governance, and creates questions about what happens when someone leaves.

The decision is not simply whether a valued clinician or administrator deserves a reward. It is whether actual ownership is an appropriate, legally available retention tool—and whether its ongoing economic cost makes more sense than cash compensation or a contractual incentive.

Before promising a percentage, build a before-and-after ownership schedule, test distributions against available cash, and review departure obligations. Healthcare counsel must first confirm whether the proposed recipient and arrangement are eligible for the specific practice entity and jurisdiction. This article explains the financial comparison, not ownership eligibility rules or detailed plan design.

What equity dilution means for a medical practice

Dilution occurs when an entity issues additional ownership interests and existing owners hold a smaller percentage of the resulting total. Their number of units may stay the same while their share of the practice decreases.

That can affect several different rights:

  • Current economic participation: the share of distributions an owner may receive under the governing documents.
  • Future value: participation in appreciation or proceeds from a permitted sale, subject to the applicable terms.
  • Governance: voting, approval or information rights, which need not match economic percentages.
  • Future dilution exposure: the effect of later awards on everyone already holding interests.

An existing owner transferring some of their own interests is different from the practice issuing new interests. A transfer may reduce only that owner’s position; a new issuance can dilute all existing owners. Model the actual proposed transaction rather than using “give someone equity” as shorthand.

For a multi-entity practice, identify the entity involved. An interest in one operating entity is not necessarily participation in every location, a management entity or separately owned real estate.

Compare ownership with cash and contractual alternatives

Start with the retention problem. Is a physician seeking a credible ownership path? Is a practice manager asking for more predictable compensation? Is a long-serving employee seeking recognition for helping expand operations? Those needs may require different responses.

Arrangement Effect on existing ownership Financial trade-off Main question before proceeding
Salary increase No direct dilution Raises recurring payroll costs Can the practice support it through slower payer receipts and seasonal changes?
Cash retention bonus No direct dilution Creates a payment obligation under agreed terms Is the payment affordable, and are conditions clearly documented?
Contractual profit-linked incentive No direct ownership dilution if structured solely as compensation Shares an agreed financial result through compensation How are profit, shared costs and payment timing defined?
Phantom equity or another value-linked contractual award Does not itself issue actual ownership interests Can create a future cash obligation tied to a defined measure How are value, payment triggers and departure treatment determined?
Actual ownership issuance Dilutes existing holders unless another structure is used Changes economic participation and may create additional obligations Is the recipient eligible, and do the ownership, governance and tax consequences fit?

These are comparison categories, not interchangeable legal forms. Contractual alternatives still require legal and tax review. Avoid assuming that calling an arrangement a “bonus” or “phantom equity” resolves its treatment.

Ownership also should not substitute for a realistic cash-pay discussion. A valuable but illiquid interest may not address an employee’s immediate compensation needs.

Illustrative example: an additional award changes everyone’s share

Illustrative example only: Assume an eligible medical practice has one class of interests with distributions proportional to ownership. Owner A holds 800 units, and Owner B, an existing staff owner, holds 200. The practice proposes issuing 250 new units to another eligible staff member, Owner C.

For simplicity, this example assumes the award is fully effective immediately, involves no purchase proceeds and has no special distribution rights. These are modeling assumptions—not recommended terms or a valuation conclusion.

Before-and-after ownership schedule

Holder Units before Ownership before Units after Ownership after
Owner A 800 80% 800 64%
Owner B, existing staff owner 200 20% 200 16%
Owner C, additional staff owner 0 0% 250 20%
Total 1,000 100% 1,250 100%

Owner A loses 16 percentage points, and Owner B loses four percentage points. Each existing owner’s percentage is reduced by 20% relative to their previous position. Owner B needs to understand this change just as clearly as the founding owner does.

The denominator matters: issuing 250 units against 1,000 existing units creates a 20% post-award interest, not a 25% interest.

Distribution effects at unchanged available cash

Continuing the illustrative example: Assume the owners approve a $400,000 distribution pool after considering payroll, operating obligations, reserves and other cash needs.

Holder Distribution before award Distribution after award Change
Owner A $320,000 $256,000 −$64,000
Owner B $80,000 $64,000 −$16,000
Owner C $0 $80,000 +$80,000
Total $400,000 $400,000 $0

The award reallocates the pool; it does not create additional cash. It also does not guarantee that the pool will be available each year.

Under these same illustrative assumptions, a $500,000 distribution pool would restore Owner A’s distribution to $320,000 and Owner B’s to $80,000, while providing $100,000 to Owner C. That is an arithmetic comparison, not a forecast that the award will generate the additional cash.

A smaller ownership percentage does not necessarily mean lower dollar value if the practice grows. Conversely, a higher practice valuation does not ensure cash distributions or a buyer for an owner’s interest.

Test the award against practice cash flow

Do not use collections or accounting profit alone as the distribution budget. Payer receipts can arrive unevenly, patient balances can remain outstanding, and provider and administrative payroll still need funding.

Prepare a comparison using consistent assumptions:

  1. Establish the baseline. Reconcile the books and distinguish owner compensation, employee compensation, distributions and nonrecurring expenses.
  2. Identify the affected entity. Use its financial results and obligations, not an unsupported consolidated total.
  3. Review shared costs. For multiple locations or entities, document how central administration, rent and other shared expenses are allocated.
  4. Forecast available cash. Include payroll, operating expenses, debt payments, planned equipment spending and working-capital needs.
  5. Compare alternatives. Model the proposed ownership award beside a cash or contractual arrangement over the same period.
  6. Stress-test the decision. Examine slower collections, a provider vacancy and a departure-related payment obligation.

The IRS guide to starting and operating a business supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide a sound foundation for this analysis; they do not determine an award’s legal or tax treatment.

Model departure before making the offer

An ownership award can outlast employment. Ending someone’s job does not, by itself, tell the practice what happens to their interest. The governing agreements must address that separately.

The following departure scenarios continue the illustrative example above. They describe possible contractual outcomes to review, not default rules.

Scenario Ownership effect Financial issue to model
Owner C leaves but retains all 250 units Ownership remains 64% / 16% / 20% A former employee may retain economic rights while the practice pays a replacement employee
The practice repurchases and retires all 250 units With no other changes, the remaining percentages return to 80% / 20% The repurchase creates a cash requirement under the agreed valuation and payment terms
Owner A purchases all of Owner C’s units Ownership becomes 84% / 16% The purchasing owner’s obligation differs from a practice-funded repurchase
Only part of the award remains outstanding Percentages depend on the actual units remaining Update the schedule and separately identify any payment obligation

Ask counsel to address resignation, termination, retirement, death, disability and loss of eligibility as applicable. Ask the valuation adviser how the relevant interest would be valued under the agreement. Ask the CPA to model the resulting cash and tax questions.

Do not treat a possible future repurchase as free reversal of dilution. Restoring percentages may require a substantial payment. Detailed vesting mechanics and financing arrangements need separate design work.

Records and questions for your advisers

Prepare a coordinated adviser packet before communicating a firm offer. Include current governing documents, ownership records, prior awards, employment agreements, financial statements, debt agreements, distribution history and any existing valuation work.

Adviser Records to provide Questions to resolve
Healthcare counsel Entity structure, ownership schedule, proposed recipient’s role and credentials, governing and employment agreements Is this recipient eligible for this interest? What restrictions, approvals and healthcare-specific considerations apply? Which voting and information rights are appropriate?
CPA Reconciled books, payroll records, owner compensation, distributions, entity tax information and proposed award terms How would the arrangement affect compensation reporting, tax allocations, withholding or other obligations? Could tax obligations arise without matching cash?
Valuation adviser Entity- and location-level financials, shared-cost allocations, debt, compensation information and award rights What exactly is being valued? Which assumptions and valuation date apply? How should departure-related pricing be evaluated?

Where practical, use aggregated financial information rather than patient-level data. Do not include patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. The ownership analysis generally needs financial summaries, not patient records.

Staff communication checklist

Give existing owners and the proposed recipient a consistent explanation, with individual compensation details handled appropriately. Avoid language suggesting guaranteed value or tax outcomes.

Before an offer is accepted, confirm that the written materials explain:

  • [ ] Whether the arrangement is actual ownership or a contractual payment right.
  • [ ] Which entity, locations and economic activities are included—and excluded.
  • [ ] Units and percentages before and after the award, including dilution to existing staff owners.
  • [ ] How compensation, distributions and any future sale proceeds differ.
  • [ ] Whether voting rights differ from economic participation.
  • [ ] That distributions and future value are not guaranteed.
  • [ ] Whether later awards could cause further dilution.
  • [ ] What happens when employment ends, including any purchase or payment process.
  • [ ] Which terms remain subject to professional review and formal approval.
  • [ ] Where the recipient can obtain independent legal and tax advice.

Use the same assumptions in every presentation. A recruiting conversation based on optimistic distributions should not conflict with the agreement or the owners’ cash forecast.

Make the decision on economics, not the headline percentage

A sound retention arrangement should solve a defined staffing need without obscuring its cost to existing owners. Confirm eligibility first, compare ownership with cash and contractual alternatives, and review both steady-state distributions and departure obligations before committing.

FAQs

Does giving a staff member equity dilute every existing owner?

A new issuance generally reduces existing owners’ percentages when their unit counts remain unchanged. A transfer from one owner can affect that owner instead. The actual economic effect also depends on the rights attached to each interest. Review the proposed transaction and governing documents rather than relying on the word “equity.”

Can a nonclinical practice manager receive ownership?

Do not assume eligibility based on job title or tenure. Healthcare counsel must review the jurisdiction, entity, proposed recipient and arrangement. If actual ownership is not appropriate or available, ask counsel and the CPA to evaluate contractual compensation alternatives.

Is phantom equity a way to avoid all the costs of dilution?

It can provide a contractual payment right without issuing actual ownership interests, but that does not make it cost-free. It may create a future cash obligation, and its legal, accounting and tax treatment needs review. Compare expected payments and departure outcomes with the ownership alternative.

How much ownership should a practice award for retention?

There is no universal percentage established by this analysis. Start with the retention objective, the recipient’s role, eligibility, valuation and the economic cost to current owners. Model the award’s effects before discussing a percentage, and do not treat the illustrative award in this article as a benchmark.
