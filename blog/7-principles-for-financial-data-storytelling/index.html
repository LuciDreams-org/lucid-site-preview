7 Principles of Financial Storytelling for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Revenue increased, but available cash declined. Should the practice owner approve a planned equipment purchase, reduce distributions or investigate collections first?

A useful financial story helps owners and managers make that decision. It explains what changed, what the records establish, what remains uncertain and who will act next. It does not make weak results look better or substitute a persuasive explanation for reconciled books.

For a staffed private medical practice, the story usually draws on the income statement, balance sheet, bank reconciliations and supporting schedules. Payer receipts, patient balances, provider and administrative payroll, equipment payments and owner distributions may all matter—but only when they help explain the decision.

The following seven principles turn those records into a practical management discussion.

A running example: revenue rose while cash fell

Illustrative example—not a real practice or a benchmark: A single-location practice is reviewing February results before approving a discretionary equipment purchase. Its financial statements use accrual accounting, and its operating bank account has been reconciled.

Measure January February Meaning for the discussion
Recognized net revenue $400,000 $440,000 Revenue increased, but this is not the same as collections
Net income $40,000 $50,000 The practice remained profitable on its accounting basis
Net accounts receivable at month-end $250,000 $300,000 More recognized revenue remained uncollected
Available operating cash at month-end $200,000 $160,000 The operating cash balance declined despite higher profit

For this example, “available operating cash” means reconciled, unrestricted operating cash. It does not mean the entire balance can safely be spent: upcoming payroll, vendor payments and other commitments still need to be considered.

The owner needs more than “revenue is up.” The management question is whether the practice can fund the purchase without jeopardizing its near-term obligations.

1. Start with the decision

Write the decision before writing the financial summary. Otherwise, a monthly report can become a tour of every account without resolving anything.

In the example, the decision is:

Should we approve the discretionary equipment purchase now, or defer it until we validate collections timing and the near-term cash forecast?

That question determines the supporting information: reconciled cash, expected payer and patient receipts, payroll commitments, scheduled debt payments and the proposed purchase timing.

It also determines the audience. The owner needs to approve or defer spending. The practice manager needs to understand operational follow-up. The finance lead needs to reconcile the explanation and test the forecast. They do not all need every underlying transaction in the meeting packet.

A strong opening includes a recommendation, not just a result: “Defer approval until the cash review is complete.” The recommendation should remain conditional on the evidence—not on how confidently someone presents it.

2. Define the measures

Financial stories break down when people use the same word for different things. “Revenue” might mean gross charges, net recognized revenue or cash collected. Those measures answer different questions.

Define the few measures that matter before interpreting them.

Measure Definition to use in the report Common misunderstanding to prevent
Net revenue Revenue recognized under the practice’s accounting policy, after applicable adjustments Treating gross charges as expected collections
Collections Payer and patient payments received during the period, with treatment of refunds stated Assuming all receipts relate to that month’s services
Net income Recognized revenue less recognized expenses on the stated accounting basis Treating profit as the change in the bank balance
Net accounts receivable Receivables after recorded allowances and adjustments Assuming every outstanding balance will be collected in full
Available operating cash Reconciled, unrestricted operating cash within a stated account scope Treating it as cash left over after future commitments

In the example, February’s $440,000 is recognized net revenue, not bank deposits. That distinction makes the rise in receivables relevant.

Also state which locations, entities and accounts are included. A multi-location report should use consistent shared-cost allocation methods. A multi-entity report should identify intercompany balances and transfers rather than treating a transfer between included accounts as new revenue.

3. Use comparable periods

A comparison is useful only when its scope and accounting treatment are consistent.

Before saying February outperformed January, check whether both periods include complete payroll accruals, consistent revenue adjustments and the same locations. Note differences in business days, deposit timing or payroll payment schedules that could affect interpretation.

For the running example, compare:

  • January and February actual results using the same accounting policies.
  • February actual results against the February budget, if one exists.
  • Recent collection and receivable trends, rather than relying on one month alone.

Revenue increased by $40,000 in the illustrative example. That fact does not establish that the improvement is recurring. A prior-period adjustment could affect recognized revenue, while payment timing could affect cash independently.

For practices with several locations, show location results separately when the spending decision depends on location performance. Keep the consolidated view, but do not let it conceal a location’s cash needs or a change in shared-cost allocation.

4. Separate facts from hypotheses

A financial narrative should distinguish what the records show from what management suspects.

In the example, higher receivables are documented. “Payers are paying more slowly” is not yet established. The increase could reflect timing, revenue growth, unresolved balances, changes in adjustments or a combination of factors.

Statement Status Evidence or follow-up needed
February revenue exceeded January revenue Recorded fact, subject to close review Closed income statements and adjustment support
Operating cash fell by $40,000 Reconciled fact Bank reconciliations and general ledger
Net receivables increased by $50,000 Recorded fact Reconciled receivables rollforward
Slower payer payments explain the receivable increase Hypothesis Aggregate payer receipt trends and aging analysis
A discretionary purchase would reduce liquidity further Conditional conclusion Purchase terms and a dated cash forecast

Use language such as “the records show,” “may reflect” and “we need to verify.” These phrases preserve the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

For management reporting, use aggregate payer and patient-balance information where possible. Keep patient-identifiable details out of general-purpose AI tools and ordinary Slack discussions; use an appropriately reviewed workflow when sensitive information is necessary.

5. Connect profit and cash

This is the central explanation in the example. Profit and cash differ because revenue and expenses do not always occur when money moves. Equipment purchases, loan principal payments and owner distributions also affect cash differently from operating expenses.

Build a bridge that reconciles beginning cash to ending cash.

Illustrative example continued: The following simplified bridge assumes no other cash-flow movements. Each adjustment is supported by the relevant ledger or schedule.

February cash bridge Cash effect
Beginning operating cash $200,000
Net income +$50,000
Add back depreciation included in net income +$5,000
Increase in net accounts receivable −$50,000
Decrease in accounts payable −$10,000
Equipment purchased during February −$20,000
Loan principal paid −$5,000
Owner distributions −$10,000
Ending operating cash $160,000

The explanation is now specific: profit added to cash generation, but the receivable increase and other uses of cash more than offset it.

Do not describe every cash outflow as an expense. Loan principal and owner distributions reduce cash but are not operating expenses. The equipment purchase appears as a cash outflow here, while depreciation is added back because it reduced net income without using current-period cash.

This bridge explains the balance movement; it does not prove that receivables are collectible or that spending was inappropriate. Those are separate questions.

Reconciliation comes first. IRS Publication 583 discusses maintaining business records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. A polished narrative cannot repair missing transactions or unexplained differences.

6. Disclose uncertainty

The historical cash bridge explains what happened. The spending decision also depends on what may happen next.

List the material unknowns, including expected receipt dates, unresolved receivable adjustments, unrecorded invoices and purchase commitments. Identify which information is confirmed, estimated or awaiting review.

For the example, prepare a short-term cash forecast with:

  • Beginning reconciled cash.
  • Expected payer and patient receipts, supported by recent patterns and known timing information.
  • Provider and administrative payroll payments.
  • Scheduled vendor, debt and other obligations.
  • The proposed equipment payment shown separately.

Compare a base case with a slower-collections case. Make the receipt assumptions visible rather than presenting a forecast balance as certain. If older balances drive the forecast, explain how their expected collectibility was assessed.

Ask whether the proposed purchase leaves adequate cash under the slower-collections case. Management should establish a cash floor based on the practice’s obligations and risk tolerance, not an unsupported universal benchmark.

Uncertainty does not prevent a decision. It helps determine whether the decision should be conditional, staged or deferred.

7. End with an accountable next action

“Monitor cash” is not an action plan. Assign a deliverable, an owner and a review point.

Next action Accountable role Completion or review point
Validate the receivables rollforward and cash bridge Finance lead or CPA Before the spending decision
Review aggregate aging and payer receipt changes Practice manager with billing lead At the next management cash review
Update the short-term cash forecast, including payroll and committed payments Finance lead Before approval, then on an agreed schedule
Approve or defer the discretionary purchase against the cash floor Practice owner After reviewing the forecast and unresolved items

The finance lead explains the records; the practice manager coordinates operational follow-up; the owner makes the spending decision. Clear ownership prevents a financial discussion from ending with everyone assuming someone else will act.

Before and after: the management narrative

Illustrative example continued.

Before: a persuasive but incomplete summary

Revenue rose from $400,000 to $440,000, and profit improved. Cash is temporarily lower because collections are slow. Performance is strong, so we should proceed with the purchase and monitor cash.

This version confuses a hypothesis with a fact, leaves cash unexplained and recommends spending without testing upcoming obligations.

After: a decision-ready explanation

We recommend deferring the discretionary equipment purchase until the cash forecast is reviewed. February recognized revenue increased to $440,000 and net income reached $50,000, but reconciled operating cash declined from $200,000 to $160,000.

The cash bridge shows a $50,000 increase in net receivables, a $10,000 reduction in payables, equipment spending, loan principal payments and owner distributions. Together, these uses exceeded profit plus the depreciation add-back. The periods use the same accounting basis and location scope.

The receivable increase is documented; slower payer payments remain a hypothesis. The finance lead will validate the receivables rollforward and update the cash forecast, while the practice manager reviews aggregate aging and receipt patterns. The owner will reconsider the purchase after reviewing the slower-collections case against the practice’s cash floor.

Narrative reasoning is not presentation design

Colors, chart types and layout can make a report easier to read. They cannot establish why cash declined. A simple cash bridge may be more useful than an elaborate dashboard when the decision is whether to spend.

Similarly, an AI-generated executive summary is a draft, not evidence. A reviewer must check its figures, definitions, period comparisons, causal language and recommendations against the supporting records. Do not let fluent wording turn an untested explanation into a reported fact.

Review checklist before sharing

  • [ ] The opening names a specific management decision.
  • [ ] Measures have clear definitions and an accounting basis.
  • [ ] Periods, locations and entities are comparable.
  • [ ] Bank balances and supporting schedules reconcile.
  • [ ] Facts are separated from hypotheses and forecasts.
  • [ ] Profit connects to the cash movement without unexplained differences.
  • [ ] Material assumptions and uncertainties are disclosed.
  • [ ] Patient information is minimized and handled through an appropriate workflow.
  • [ ] The recommendation identifies an owner, deliverable and review point.

Financial storytelling should make the next decision clearer—not merely make the numbers sound convincing.
