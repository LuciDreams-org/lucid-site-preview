How Medical Practice Cap Tables Change With New Capital

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A medical practice cap table is an ownership schedule: it records who holds shares or units in a particular legal entity and how those holdings change through transactions. It helps owners answer a practical question before accepting capital or admitting a partner: Who will own what after this transaction closes?

For a staffed private practice, that question is separate from how much cash is available for provider and administrative payroll, how profitable each location is, or how owners are compensated for their work. Those financial details matter, but they do not establish ownership by themselves.

This guide explains how to maintain an ownership schedule through successive capital contributions, partner admissions and proposed outside investment. Permissible owners, instruments and structures depend on the practice and jurisdiction. Have healthcare counsel confirm what is permitted before treating any proposed structure as available.

Start With the Correct Entity and Ownership Baseline

A cap table belongs to a legal entity—not simply to a practice brand, office location or group of businesses sharing expenses.

If your practice operates through multiple entities, maintain a separate ownership schedule for each. A consolidated financial report does not replace entity-level ownership records. Likewise, allocating shared administrative costs among locations does not change who owns the underlying entities.

Before modeling new capital, establish a dated baseline containing:

  • The entity's legal name and the schedule's effective date.
  • Each owner's legal name.
  • The class and number of issued shares or units held.
  • Total issued shares or units and each owner's percentage on that basis.
  • References to governing agreements, ownership ledgers and transaction documents.
  • Any outstanding rights that could affect future ownership, tracked separately with their terms.

Use the terminology in the entity's documents. This article uses “units” for illustration; that does not prescribe an entity type or ownership structure.

For a single class with equal units, the basic calculation is:

Ownership percentage = owner's issued units ÷ total issued units × 100

If different classes have different rights, a single percentage column is not enough. Label exactly what each percentage measures rather than combining unlike interests into an unexplained total.

Identify What the Transaction Actually Changes

Money moving between an owner and a practice does not, by itself, tell you how ownership changes. Read the approved transaction documents before updating the schedule.

Transaction Effect on the ownership schedule Records to connect
Owner contributes capital without receiving additional units Unit counts may remain unchanged, as specified in the documents Contribution agreement, approvals, bank receipt and accounting entry
Practice issues new units to an existing owner That owner's units and total issued units increase; other owners may be diluted Issuance documents, consideration and updated ownership ledger
New partner buys newly issued units New partner enters the schedule; total issued units increase Admission documents, approvals and payment to the entity
New partner buys units from an existing owner Units transfer between owners; total issued units generally remains unchanged Transfer agreement, required consents and evidence of payment to the seller
Practice redeems an owner's units Holdings and possibly the outstanding-unit total change according to the documents Redemption agreement, payment terms and treatment of redeemed units
Proposed outside capital No completed ownership change until the required transaction steps occur Separate proposal model, eligibility review and eventual closing records

This distinction also matters for cash planning. A partner's purchase from an existing owner is not the same as a contribution of working capital to the practice. Do not include proceeds paid to a selling owner as cash available to fund the practice's operations.

Illustrative Example: Ownership Across Successive Transactions

All names, amounts, unit counts and percentages in this example are invented for illustration. The example assumes one class of equal units, no outstanding conversion rights and properly approved transactions. It does not establish a valuation, a permissible ownership arrangement or appropriate transaction terms.

The starting practice has two owners: Owner A holds 600 units and Owner B holds 400, for a total of 1,000 units.

The following transactions are modeled in sequence:

Stage Illustrative transaction Consideration and recipient Change in issued units
Starting position Existing ownership baseline Historical consideration is outside this example 1,000 units outstanding
Owner contribution Owner A receives 100 newly issued units Owner A pays $100,000 to the practice Add 100 units
Partner admission Partner C receives 250 newly issued units Partner C pays $250,000 to the practice Add 250 units
Proposed outside capital Proposed Owner D would receive 150 newly issued units, if permitted and approved Proposed payment of $150,000 to the practice Add 150 units only if completed

The repeated price per unit is a simplifying assumption, not a recommendation to use the same price for later transactions. Actual consideration, rights and valuation support require separate review.

Owner B never sells a unit, but B's percentage declines because the practice issues additional units to others. That is percentage dilution.

Owner A's percentage initially increases because A receives all the units in the first issuance. A is then diluted by the later issuances, despite continuing to hold 700 units.

A decline in ownership percentage does not, by itself, establish a decline in the economic value of an owner's interest. The example shows ownership arithmetic—not whether any transaction is financially attractive.

Keep the proposed D column in a clearly labeled scenario file. It should not replace the current ownership ledger before closing. If counsel determines that the proposed owner or structure is impermissible, the model is not authorization to proceed.

Ownership Percentage Is Not the Same as Owner Rights

A cap table summarizes holdings. The governing documents establish the rights attached to those holdings.

Item to review Why the percentage alone is insufficient
Voting rights Unit ownership may not directly determine voting power, approval thresholds or reserved decisions
Distribution rights Cash distributions may depend on class rights, agreement provisions and applicable restrictions
Compensation for work Provider compensation and management pay are separate from ownership, even when the recipient is also an owner
Redemption and transfer rights Departure, retirement or other events may trigger specific purchase procedures, restrictions or payment terms
Sale or liquidation proceeds Different rights and obligations can affect how proceeds are allocated

In the illustrative example, A falls below half of issued units after the proposed final transaction. That is a reason to review governance—not proof that A automatically loses control. Counsel must interpret the applicable voting and approval provisions.

Maintain a short rights summary alongside the cap table, referencing the controlling documents. Do not let a spreadsheet note substitute for the agreement itself.

A Transaction-Record Checklist for Practice Owners

Assign one person to coordinate the ownership file, with legal and accounting review appropriate to each transaction.

Before signing

  • Confirm the exact entity involved and reconcile its current ownership ledger.
  • Ask healthcare counsel to confirm owner eligibility, permitted instruments, structure and required approvals.
  • Separate newly issued interests from transfers of existing interests.
  • Identify what consideration is being provided, who receives it and when it is due.
  • Prepare a dated before-and-after model showing unit changes, percentages and assumptions.
  • Obtain appropriate valuation advice rather than deriving a transaction price from the desired ownership percentage alone.
  • Flag proposed changes to voting, distribution, transfer or redemption rights.

At closing

  • Collect executed agreements, approvals, consents and admission or transfer documents.
  • Match the number and class of units to the final documents.
  • Record the effective date and any unfulfilled conditions.
  • Match payments to bank evidence, distinguishing funds received by the entity from payments to selling owners.
  • Document noncash or deferred consideration without treating it as cash already received.
  • Have the CPA determine the appropriate accounting treatment and any applicable tax reporting or filing requirements.

After closing

  • Update the ownership ledger and cap table using the completed terms, not an earlier proposal.
  • Recalculate percentages and reconcile opening units, issuances, transfers and redemptions to closing units.
  • Save the prior version and a transaction summary explaining the change.
  • Reconcile related accounting entries and bank activity.
  • Update the rights summary and remove abandoned proposals from the current-record folder.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices help connect transaction documents to the books, but bank records alone do not establish ownership rights.

Connect the Cap Table to the Books Without Confusing Them

The ownership schedule, accounting equity balances and an owner's tax basis are related records, but they are not interchangeable.

A cash contribution should be traceable to the relevant agreement and accounting entry. It does not automatically establish a new ownership percentage. Likewise, a percentage in the cap table is not a substitute for an owner's capital-account or tax-basis records. Ask the CPA which schedules apply to the entity and transaction.

For a multi-location practice, continue tracking location profitability separately. A location's payer receipts, payroll costs or share of administrative expenses do not independently determine ownership. For multiple entities, identify which entity received the funds and which entity issued or transferred interests.

Questions to Bring to Your Advisers

Healthcare counsel

  • Can this proposed owner hold this interest in this entity and jurisdiction?
  • Are the proposed instrument and structure permitted?
  • Which consents, approvals and document amendments are required?
  • How do the transaction terms affect voting, distributions, transfers and redemption?
  • Which closing conditions must be satisfied before updating the legal ownership record?

CPA

  • How should each component of consideration be recorded?
  • Which amounts belong to the practice, a selling owner or another entity?
  • What capital-account, basis, allocation or reporting questions require separate analysis?
  • What tax consequences and filing requirements should be evaluated before signing?
  • How will the transaction reconcile across the ownership schedule, general ledger and bank records?

Valuation adviser

  • What interest and rights are being valued, and as of what date?
  • What financial records and adjustments are needed?
  • Does the analysis distinguish the entity's value from the value of the specific interest?
  • What assumptions or transaction changes would require the analysis to be revisited?

FAQs

Does every owner contribution change ownership percentages?

No. A contribution may leave unit counts unchanged, or it may accompany an issuance that changes ownership. Review the executed documents rather than inferring ownership from the payment amount. The CPA should separately determine the accounting treatment.

Does buying out part of another owner's interest dilute everyone else?

A direct transfer of existing units generally changes the buyer's and seller's holdings without increasing total issued units. Other owners' unit-based percentages therefore generally remain unchanged. An issuance by the practice increases the denominator and can dilute owners who do not receive additional units.

Should a practice show fully diluted ownership?

If outstanding rights could result in additional ownership interests, a separately labeled scenario may be useful. Have advisers interpret the actual terms, conditions and conversion assumptions. Keep the current issued ownership schedule distinct from hypothetical future ownership; do not assume startup financing instruments are appropriate or permitted for the practice.

When should the ownership schedule be updated?

Update it when an ownership transaction becomes effective under its documents, and reconcile it during periodic financial reviews. Keep proposed transactions in separate models until completed. If documents, payment records and the ownership ledger disagree, resolve the discrepancy with counsel and the CPA rather than forcing the spreadsheet to balance.

Keep a Clear Record of Every Ownership Change

Before accepting capital or admitting a partner, establish the current ownership, identify exactly what the transaction changes and model the resulting holdings. After closing, connect the final documents to the ownership ledger, accounting records and payment evidence.

The goal is not merely a percentage that adds to 100%. It is a reliable, entity-specific record showing who owns what, how that changed and which documents establish the associated rights.
