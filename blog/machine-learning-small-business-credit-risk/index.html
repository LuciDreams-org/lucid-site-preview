Machine Learning and Medical Practice Credit Risk

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A profitable medical practice can still have an uneven repayment picture. Payer receipts may arrive after payroll is due, patient balances may remain uncollected, and a strong consolidated profit and loss statement may conceal a location that needs ongoing support.

When a lender uses machine learning to help assess credit risk, those financial patterns may become inputs to its review. The owner's task is not to reverse-engineer a proprietary score. It is to provide accurate records, explain material changes and understand whether the practice can support the proposed payments under realistic conditions.

This guide explains model-assisted credit assessment from the perspective of a staffed US private medical practice. It includes a borrower-readiness packet, a repayment sensitivity example and questions to ask when information is stale or disputed. It does not predict approval or recommend a credit product.

What machine learning changes—and what it does not

Machine learning models identify patterns in historical data and apply those patterns to new information. In credit assessment, a lender may use a model to help estimate repayment risk, flag inconsistencies or identify applications needing further review.

Depending on the lender and its process, inputs could include financial statements, bank activity, existing obligations and credit-history information. Do not assume that every lender uses machine learning, accesses the same data or makes decisions without human review.

A model might detect relationships among receipt volatility, cash balances and recurring payments that are difficult to summarize with one ratio. But a pattern is not an explanation of your practice's circumstances. A deposit decline might reflect a temporary payer-processing delay, a provider departure or an actual reduction in business. Those situations have different implications.

Machine learning also does not make unreliable records reliable. Duplicate deposits, missing accounts and misclassified transfers can distort the inputs regardless of the assessment method.

Ask the lender what information it needs, which periods it evaluates and whether a reviewer can consider supporting explanations. You do not need its proprietary formula to make the submission more accurate.

The financial evidence behind repayment capacity

A lender's assessment and your internal borrowing decision should both start with the economic substance of the practice. Four areas deserve particular attention.

Area What the owner should understand Evidence to prepare
Cash-flow stability Whether recurring receipts cover recurring operating commitments through uneven collection periods Monthly cash-flow history, reconciled bank balances and explanations of unusual receipts
Repayment capacity What cash remains available after necessary operating needs and other relevant commitments A documented bridge from reported earnings to cash available for debt service
Existing obligations Which payments already compete for cash, including obligations outside the main operating account Debt schedule, payment statements, lease commitments and relevant related-party arrangements
Data quality Whether the reports describe the correct borrower, period and accounting basis Reconciliations, entity/account list, reporting dates and a correction log

Separate receipts from charges and receivables

Charges, recorded revenue and cash collections are not interchangeable. A large accounts receivable balance does not establish when cash will become available for provider and administrative payroll.

Explain the relationship among revenue, payer receipts and patient collections using consistent periods. If you provide receivables aging, distinguish payer and patient balances at an aggregate level and explain material collection uncertainty. Do not treat every outstanding balance as near-term cash.

A temporary collection catch-up can also make one month look unusually strong. Identify whether it reflects recurring activity or cash arriving from earlier periods.

Distinguish recurring operations from financing and owner activity

Loan proceeds, owner contributions and transfers between practice accounts are not operating receipts. Owner distributions are not the same as payroll expense. These distinctions matter when a lender or an automated process interprets account activity.

Prepare a bridge that identifies unusual transactions rather than simply removing anything that makes performance look weaker. A claimed one-time expense needs a documented reason for being nonrecurring. If a similar cost is likely to return, retain it in the planning analysis.

Make entity and location boundaries visible

For multi-location practices, location-level P&Ls can help explain which sites generate cash and which require support. Document how shared administrative payroll, rent and other overhead are allocated.

For multi-entity owners, identify the proposed borrower and the entities holding relevant cash, receivables and obligations. A consolidated report can provide context, but cash in another entity should not automatically be treated as available for the borrower's payments. Discuss the actual arrangements with the lender and appropriate advisers.

Build a borrower-readiness packet

First obtain the lender's document checklist and requested reporting periods. Then assemble a consistent packet rather than sending unrelated exports as requests arrive.

Packet component What to include Quality check before submission
Borrowing summary Intended use of funds, proposed borrower, requested timing and operating rationale The request matches the entity and cash-flow analysis
Financial statements Requested historical and current P&Ls and balance sheets; cash-flow statements where available Reports use consistent periods and clearly state cash or accrual basis
Cash evidence Requested bank statements, reconciliations and a near-term cash forecast All relevant accounts are included; transfers are not counted as new receipts
Collections context Aggregate payer and patient collections, receivables aging and explanations of material delays Charges, revenue, receivables and cash are clearly distinguished
Payroll and operating commitments Provider/admin payroll summaries, occupancy costs and other significant recurring commitments Costs reflect current staffing and planned changes
Debt and obligation schedule Balances, scheduled principal and interest, maturities and other relevant payment commitments Payments tie to statements; balloon payments and related-party debt are visible
Location and entity schedules Per-location results, entity-level accounts, intercompany activity and shared-cost allocation methods Consolidated totals reconcile without double-counting
Explanatory materials Material changes, corrections, forecast assumptions and requested tax returns Explanations reconcile to the supporting records

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals also make a borrowing packet easier to verify; they do not establish a lender's approval criteria.

Assign one person to maintain the submission list and coordinate with the practice's CPA or finance team. Record each report's period, preparation date and version. If a correction changes a previously submitted number, provide the replacement and a short explanation rather than leaving competing versions unresolved.

Test repayment capacity before discussing a score

Debt-service coverage is a useful planning lens, but definitions vary. Ask the lender how it defines both the cash-flow numerator and the debt-service denominator, including treatment of owner compensation, distributions, leases and proposed borrowing.

For an internal analysis, explicitly define:

Planning coverage ratio = annual cash available for debt service ÷ annual scheduled debt service.

This is a planning measure, not a prediction of a lender's score, covenant calculation or approval standard. Do not substitute accounting profit or EBITDA for cash available without explaining the adjustments. Consider working-capital needs and necessary expenditures not already reflected in the starting figure.

The correction reduces apparent repayment capacity without changing actual operations. The other rows reveal how much cushion remains under changed assumptions. None establishes what a lender will accept.

Also review timing. An annual surplus may hide a shortfall when payroll, rent and debt payments fall before payer receipts arrive. Pair the annual analysis with a near-term weekly cash forecast that includes expected receipts, payroll, other operating payments and scheduled debt payments. Treat uncertain collections as uncertain rather than forcing the forecast to balance.

Ask about explainability, stale inputs and disputed information

Model-assisted assessment can be difficult to interpret from the outside. Focus on information you can verify and questions the lender can answer:

  • Which reporting dates and source records were used? Older statements may not reflect current staffing, recent debt repayment or changed collections.
  • Which factors materially affected the assessment? Ask for an understandable explanation, not necessarily the model's code or formula.
  • Were transfers, financing proceeds or unusual receipts classified correctly? Provide a reconciliation where bank activity could be misread.
  • Can updated information receive human review? Ask how to submit corrections and whether the assessment can be refreshed.
  • What is the process for disputing inaccurate information? Identify the specific item, its source and the evidence supporting a correction.

Maintain a dated issue log. Distinguish a factual error, such as a paid obligation still shown as outstanding, from a disagreement about a forecast assumption. Submit supporting evidence through the lender's designated process and confirm whether it was incorporated.

An explanation does not by itself prove that a model is accurate or fair. Conversely, a disappointing assessment does not establish that the data is wrong. Keep the discussion tied to verifiable records and clearly identified assumptions.

Protect patient information in financial analysis

Borrower preparation should focus on business finances, not patient-level credit scoring. Use aggregate collections and receivables summaries when they meet the purpose. Do not upload patient-identifiable records to a general-purpose AI tool or ordinary Slack conversation to explain cash flow.

If a request appears to require patient information, pause to confirm its purpose, the minimum information needed and the appropriate handling process with responsible advisers. HHS explains business-associate responsibilities and the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions. These are not certifications of any lender, accounting firm or software product.

Keep credit risk separate from the financing decision

Three related questions need separate answers:

  • Credit-risk assessment: How does the lender evaluate the borrower's repayment risk?
  • Financing timing: When would funds be needed, and when would payments begin relative to the practice's cash cycle?
  • Debt-equity planning: How should owners fund the investment given repayment commitments, liquidity and ownership considerations?

A favorable credit assessment does not establish that borrowing is the right decision. Owners still need to test affordability, operating flexibility and downside cash needs. Likewise, preparing for a borrowing discussion is not the same as choosing a funding structure.

FAQs

Can machine learning compensate for incomplete practice books?

Do not rely on it to do so. A lender may use additional information, but missing accounts, duplicated receipts or misclassified transactions can still distort the assessment. Reconcile the records and disclose unresolved gaps rather than assuming a model will infer the correct answer.

What if the lender uses financial information that is out of date?

Identify the reporting period used, provide updated reconciled statements and explain material changes. Ask how updated documents enter the review process and whether the assessment can be refreshed. Keep a record of what you submitted and when.

What debt-service coverage ratio does a medical practice need?

There is no universal threshold presented here. Requirements and calculation methods vary. Ask the lender for its definition and evaluate your own cash cushion under realistic stress scenarios, including collection delays and existing obligations.

Should I send patient-level balances to explain receivables?

Start with aggregate payer and patient receivables summaries. If more detail is requested, clarify the purpose and appropriate handling process before sharing identifiable information. The objective is to explain business repayment capacity, not assess individual patients' creditworthiness.
