Medical Practice Investor Reporting Metrics Checklist

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A recurring investor report should let a medical practice’s existing equity partners or permitted investors answer three questions: What did the practice earn, where did the cash go, and what obligations remain? A collection total or profit-and-loss statement alone cannot answer all three.

The goal is a complete, consistent reporting package—not a ranked list of universal performance benchmarks. For a staffed private practice, that package should connect payer and patient receipts, recognized revenue, receivables, provider and administrative staffing costs, debt, capital spending and owner distributions.

Use the checklist below to assign responsibility, reconcile supporting records and explain changes before releasing the report. Have legal and accounting advisers determine applicable ownership restrictions, recipients’ information rights and disclosure requirements. This checklist is a management workflow, not a determination that a particular investor arrangement or disclosure is permitted.

Establish the reporting rules first

Put a short reporting-policy page at the front of the package. It should identify:

  • Reporting entities: The legal entities included and any excluded related entities.
  • Locations: Which offices are included, with opening or closure dates when relevant.
  • Accounting basis: Cash, accrual or another clearly described management-reporting basis.
  • Reporting period: The month or quarter covered, the period-end date and the close status.
  • Comparisons: Prior period, same period in the prior year and year-to-date results, where available.
  • Preparation and approval: Who prepared the report, who reviewed it and when it was released.
  • Limitations: Missing records, estimates, unresolved differences and changes in methodology.

Do not call management reports audited or reviewed financial statements unless the corresponding professional engagement supports that description. Internal review by an owner or controller is a different process.

Agree on a recurring delivery schedule that follows the close process and applicable reporting obligations. An early report with unfinished reconciliations should be labeled preliminary rather than presented as final.

Define each measure and its supporting records

The following table is a practical reporting specification. Keep the definitions stable and document any changes.

Keep collections separate from recognized revenue

Collections describe cash movement. Recognized revenue describes accounting performance under the selected basis. They may differ because of receipt timing, unpaid receivables, refunds, recoupments, adjustments and deposits not yet posted to the billing records.

For accrual reporting, build a bridge between net revenue, receivable movements, adjustments and collections. For cash-basis reporting, explain any differences between the collections report and ledger revenue, such as deposits in transit or unposted receipts. If receivables are not on the cash-basis balance sheet, present their aging as a supplementary operating schedule rather than implying it reconciles to a ledger asset.

Illustrative example: A practice reports $480,000 in collections and $510,000 in accrual-basis net revenue for a month. The $30,000 difference does not, by itself, prove that receivables increased by $30,000. The preparer must also examine adjustments, refunds, credit balances and posting timing before explaining the movement.

Review the measures as a connected financial picture

Receivable aging: explain changes, not just totals

Show payer and patient balances separately when that distinction helps explain collection timing. State whether aging begins at the service date, claim submission date or another defined date. Changing the aging clock can make balances look younger without improving collections.

Use consistent buckets, such as current, 31–60, 61–90 and over 90 days, and disclose the convention. Identify credit balances rather than allowing them to obscure older debit balances. If a separate collection allowance is used, show how it affects the reported net receivable balance.

A useful note names the driver, the follow-up owner and the next action. “Older payer balances increased following a posting backlog; the billing manager is reconciling remittances” is more useful than “receivables need attention.” Do not describe unresolved balances as collectible without supporting review.

Staffing costs: distinguish expense from payroll cash

Separate provider compensation, administrative payroll, employer taxes, benefits and contract labor. Explain how owner-provider compensation is classified so partners do not confuse payment for services with equity distributions.

Payroll expense and cash paid can differ because of payroll cutoffs, accrued compensation and benefit billing timing. Reconcile those differences under the stated accounting basis.

If staffing cost is shown as a percentage of revenue, specify both components. A ratio using collections is not directly comparable with one using accrual net revenue. Keep allocation methods for employees working across locations consistent, and explain vacancies, coverage arrangements or bonus timing that affect comparisons.

Cash flow, debt, capital spending and distributions: show the bridge

Profit is not the same as cash available for distribution. Present a bridge from beginning cash to ending cash, separating operating cash movement, capital purchases, borrowing, principal repayments, owner contributions and distributions.

Apply the accountant’s classification policy consistently, including treatment of interest and unusual cash items. Reconcile the bridge to the reported bank balances. Identify restrictions on cash and upcoming obligations that limit availability.

Illustrative example: A practice begins the month with $180,000 in cash, generates $70,000 from operations, pays $25,000 for equipment, repays $15,000 of loan principal and distributes $40,000 to owners. Ending cash is $170,000. The positive operating cash flow does not mean cash increased or that another distribution is prudent.

For equipment financed directly by a lender, distinguish the asset addition and borrowing from cash paid by the practice. Include committed but unpaid equipment purchases so readers can see obligations not yet reflected in bank activity.

Distribution decisions should consider upcoming payroll, payables, debt terms, planned spending and adviser guidance—not merely the current bank balance.

Make entity and location boundaries explicit

A location is an operating unit; a legal entity is a separate reporting boundary. They are not interchangeable. One entity may operate several offices, while related entities may hold equipment, property or shared-service costs.

Boundary issue Reporting treatment
Several offices within one entity Reconcile location P&Ls to the entity P&L, including any unallocated corporate costs
Separate entities with separate accounts Reconcile each entity’s bank accounts, debt and equity activity before preparing a combined view
Intercompany charges or transfers Identify both sides and document eliminations in a combined management report so internal activity is not double counted
Shared payroll, rent or administration Use a documented allocation method appropriate to the cost and explain changes
New or closed offices Separate comparable-location results from changes caused by the reporting footprint
Different ownership interests Have advisers determine which entity-level information each recipient is entitled to receive

Do not describe a combined management view as formal consolidated financial statements without the accountant’s assessment. Preserve entity-level schedules even when owners also want a total-practice view.

For location comparisons, disclose whether central costs are allocated or left in a corporate column. An office’s apparent margin can change simply because allocation methods changed.

Assemble and release the package

Use a repeatable close-to-release sequence:

  1. Collect source records. Obtain bank and lender statements, billing summaries, payroll reports, invoices and owner-transaction support.
  2. Complete reconciliations. Match cash, receivables where applicable, payroll, debt, fixed assets and equity activity to the relevant schedules.
  3. Review comparisons. Compare results with prior periods and approved budgets, using the same entity and location boundaries.
  4. Write exception notes. Explain material changes, estimates and unresolved items. Identify the person responsible for follow-up.
  5. Approve and distribute. Confirm the recipient list and approved delivery method, then preserve the released version. Label later corrections and explain their effects.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports maintaining complete business records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. Those fundamentals also make recurring partner reporting easier to substantiate.

Use aggregate financial information wherever possible. Investor packages generally do not need patient names or claim-level clinical details. Have the appropriate advisers assess privacy obligations and approved handling methods when patient information is involved. The HHS minimum necessary guidance provides relevant context, including exceptions; it is not a substitute for a practice-specific assessment. Do not send patient-identifiable data through ordinary Slack or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

How often should a medical practice report to equity partners?

Follow the applicable agreements and adviser guidance. A monthly management package can support operating oversight, while a quarterly package may suit some ownership arrangements. Whatever cadence is selected, define the close date, review steps and delivery schedule. Do not imply a preliminary report is final merely to meet an internal deadline.

Can a cash-basis practice include receivable aging?

Yes. Label it as a supplementary operating schedule if receivables are not recognized on the cash-basis balance sheet. Define the aging method, explain adjustments and reconcile the schedule to its billing source. Do not present it as though it ties to a ledger receivable account that does not exist.

What should happen when a number is not reconciled by the reporting deadline?

Assess its significance with the reviewer. Depending on applicable requirements, delay release or issue a clearly labeled preliminary package that identifies the affected measure, known limitation, responsible preparer and expected resolution. Preserve the original release and explain subsequent corrections rather than silently replacing figures.

Does positive operating cash flow mean owners can take distributions?

Not necessarily. Debt payments, capital commitments, payroll, payables, cash restrictions and governing agreements may limit available cash. Present these obligations alongside the cash-flow result and obtain the appropriate approvals and adviser input before deciding on a distribution.
