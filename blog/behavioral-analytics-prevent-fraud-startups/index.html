Behavioral Analytics for Fraud Prevention in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A vendor’s bank details change, an approval is overridden, and a payment is released shortly afterward. Each event may have a legitimate explanation. Together, they deserve an independent review.

For a staffed private medical practice, behavioral analytics means reviewing patterns of financial-system activity across time, users, and workflows. The purpose is to identify where a control may have been bypassed—not to infer an employee’s intent from unusual working hours or a single unfamiliar action.

Owners can begin with available activity histories, approval records, and bank documentation. Specialized software is not a prerequisite for a useful review, although the quality and availability of records will determine what can be evaluated.

The practical goal is straightforward: decide which sequences require attention, assign someone independent to examine them, and document a proportionate resolution.

What Behavioral Analytics Adds to Practice Financial Controls

A transaction review asks whether an invoice is supported and correctly recorded. A behavioral review also asks how it moved through the practice’s financial workflow:

  • Who created or changed the vendor record?
  • Who approved the invoice and released the payment?
  • Were normal approvals bypassed, and did that happen repeatedly?
  • Did the activity fall within each person’s assigned responsibilities?
  • Did the sequence differ from the practice’s documented process?

The focus is the relationship between events, not simply an unusual payment amount. Similar reviews can apply to payroll preparation, patient-balance adjustments, and transfers between practice entities.

Behavioral review complements basic controls such as separate responsibilities, independent bank reconciliation, and documented approvals. It does not replace them. The IRS’s business recordkeeping guidance supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and reconciliation of bank records; those records also provide a foundation for investigating workflow exceptions.

Activity records have limits. An account name identifies the account used, not necessarily the person responsible for every action. Shared credentials, compromised access, batch processing, and incomplete histories can all complicate interpretation.

Define the Expected Workflow Before Looking for Exceptions

A pattern is only unusual relative to a credible baseline. Start by documenting how work should move through the practice, including approved coverage arrangements.

Workflow Expected responsibilities Context needed for review
Vendor maintenance and payment One person maintains vendor details; another verifies sensitive changes and authorizes release Vendor-change procedure, payment schedule, backup approvers
Provider and administrative payroll Authorized staff prepare changes; an independent reviewer checks them before release Payroll calendar, approved compensation changes, coverage assignments
Patient-balance adjustments and refunds Staff act within assigned authority; designated reviewers approve exceptions Adjustment categories, approval responsibilities, batch-processing schedules
Multi-location purchasing Location staff request expenses; designated approvers authorize them for the appropriate entity Location assignments, shared purchasing roles, entity-level bank authority
Period-end accounting Bookkeeping staff prepare entries; a separate reviewer evaluates material or unusual adjustments Close calendar, catch-up work, approved shared-cost allocation methods

Do not treat a longstanding access arrangement as an appropriate control merely because it is common. A practice manager may have accumulated vendor, payroll, and bank permissions over time. Reviewing that concentration of authority is useful even when no suspicious activity exists.

For smaller practices, complete separation may be difficult. An owner’s documented review of vendor changes and payment releases can provide an additional check when staffing limits the available roles.

A Synthetic Sequence-of-Events Example

Illustrative example only—not a customer case study: A practice with two locations normally releases vendor payments after owner approval. The practice manager maintains vendor records, while the bookkeeper prepares payment batches.

Sequence Recorded event Question for the reviewer
Monday morning A vendor’s payment destination is changed using the manager’s account Was the change independently verified through an established vendor contact?
Monday afternoon A payment batch containing that vendor is prepared Does the batch reference the updated destination, and is the underlying expense supported?
Monday evening The usual approval step is overridden Who authorized the exception, and was that person permitted to do so?
Shortly afterward Payment is released using an account assigned to a different role Was temporary access approved, or was access broader than intended?
Following review Similar overrides appear in earlier payment cycles Is this a documented operating change, a recurring workaround, or an unexplained control failure?

None of these events establishes fraud. The owner might have authorized temporary coverage while traveling, and the vendor might have legitimately changed banks. Alternatively, the sequence could reveal unauthorized activity or a compromised account.

The reviewer should preserve the available records, verify the vendor change through independently established contact information, and compare the approval history with the practice’s authorization policy. If payment has not been released, an authorized reviewer can decide whether to pause that payment pending verification. If it has been released and cannot be verified, the owner should promptly contact the bank about available options without assuming recovery is possible.

Even a legitimate payment can expose a control problem: the practice may have no reliable way to distinguish an approved exception from an unauthorized override.

A Review Matrix for Financial-System Activity

Use a matrix to connect each signal to evidence and a responsible reviewer. Independent means the reviewer did not initiate, approve, or execute the activity under review. If the owner is involved, another authorized owner or an appropriately scoped external professional may be needed.

Signal across events Evidence to examine Independent reviewer Resolution step
Approvals repeatedly occur outside the usual workflow, followed quickly by release Activity timestamps, time-zone settings, approval history, coverage schedule, bank release record Owner or finance lead uninvolved in the batch Validate the authorization; document legitimate coverage or correct the approval process
The same control is repeatedly overridden Override history, stated reasons, policy, related payment batches Uninvolved owner or controller Determine whether the control is impractical, misunderstood, or bypassed; revise it or restrict override authority
Vendor-master changes are followed by payments to the changed destination Change history, verification record, invoice, release details Authorized reviewer who did not change the vendor or release payment Independently confirm the destination; resolve the payment and correct unauthorized changes
A user performs actions outside an assigned role across several workflows Access list, role assignments, activity records, temporary-access approvals Owner with the relevant system administrator Verify the business reason, remove unnecessary access, and review affected transactions
Payroll changes are entered and approved through overlapping access Payroll change history, authorized pay changes, preparation and approval records Owner or payroll reviewer not involved in the changes Confirm authorized changes and restore independent review before future releases
Adjustment authority expands, followed by repeated patient-balance adjustments or refunds Permission history, adjustment categories, approval records, aggregate refund reports Finance lead outside the adjustment process Validate authority and supporting records; correct errors and narrow access where appropriate

Where a system does not retain a particular history, record that limitation. Missing evidence is a control gap, not automatic evidence of wrongdoing. Ask the system administrator or vendor what records are actually available rather than assuming a full audit history exists.

Build a Repeatable Review Process

1. Start with a narrow financial risk

Choose a workflow where event order matters, such as vendor changes followed by payment release. Define what requires review and who can resolve it before collecting additional data.

Avoid launching a broad employee-monitoring program when a targeted review of financial records would answer the question.

2. Assemble the minimum useful event record

Where available, collect the account identifier, event time, action, affected record, entity or location, approval status, and linked transaction reference. Keep original exports separate from working analysis and note when they were obtained.

Check whether systems use different time zones or whether a timestamp reflects entry, posting, or processing. A scheduled batch can otherwise look like unexplained overnight activity.

For multi-entity practices, retain entity identifiers. A central administrator may legitimately work across locations, but authority for one entity should not be assumed to extend to every entity’s bank account.

3. Review linked events rather than isolated alerts

Connect the vendor change, approval, override, and release using record references. Compare the sequence with the applicable policy and approved exceptions.

If the records cannot be linked reliably, describe the uncertainty. Do not turn a loose timing coincidence into a finding.

4. Record the outcome and corrective action

Use a restricted review register containing the signal, evidence references, reviewer, explanation, disposition, and follow-up owner. Useful outcomes include:

  • Verified authorized activity.
  • Legitimate activity with missing documentation.
  • Processing or configuration error.
  • Inappropriate access or control design.
  • Unresolved activity requiring escalation.

Close the loop by confirming that corrective action actually occurred. Changing a policy without changing permissions or review responsibilities may leave the same weakness in place.

Handle False Positives and Workflow Changes Fairly

After-hours work alone is a weak signal. Practice administrators may complete approvals after patient-facing duties end. Payroll coverage, a new location, catch-up bookkeeping, or a system migration may also change activity patterns legitimately.

Before escalating, check:

  • Whether temporary responsibilities were approved.
  • Whether scheduled jobs or imports generated the events.
  • Whether credentials were shared or access was compromised.
  • Whether the documented workflow matches current operations.
  • Whether the same pattern affects many users because of a configuration change.

A useful review separates the factual question—what happened—from the control question—was it properly authorized and documented?

Do not automatically block essential payroll or vendor payments solely because a pattern is unusual. Route the exception to an authorized person who can verify it and decide on a proportionate response. Suspected misconduct should be handled through appropriate management, HR, legal, or investigative channels; activity patterns alone do not justify employment conclusions.

Keep Employee and Patient Data Proportionate to the Review

Financial activity histories can reveal information about staff schedules and responsibilities. Limit collection to the defined control purpose, restrict access, explain the review process through appropriate workplace policies, and establish a documented retention approach with qualified advisers.

Avoid collecting keystrokes, personal browsing, message content, or other intrusive information when financial-event records are sufficient. Keep review findings factual and confidential rather than circulating employee risk labels.

Patient information usually is not needed to review vendor changes or approval behavior. For patient-balance workflows, use aggregate reports or nonidentifying references wherever feasible. The HHS minimum necessary guidance describes a standard with exceptions; the practice should confirm its application to the specific workflow.

Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. If outside accounting work involves protected health information, assess the arrangement and applicable responsibilities using HHS guidance on covered entities and business associates.

Turn Findings Into Better Financial Oversight

Success is not the number of alerts produced. Track whether exceptions receive independent review, whether explanations are supported, and whether recurring control gaps are corrected.

FAQs

Does unusual financial-system activity prove employee fraud?

No. It identifies a question for review. Approved coverage, batch processing, configuration errors, compromised credentials, or undocumented workflow changes can produce similar patterns. Evaluate evidence and authorization before drawing conclusions.

Does a private practice need machine learning to begin?

No. Available activity histories, approval records, and a structured review register can support a focused process. First determine which events your systems record and whether they can be linked reliably. More sophisticated tools do not eliminate the need for independent judgment.

How often should the practice review these patterns?

Match the timing to the risk. Sensitive vendor changes should be verified before payment release, while recurring override patterns can be examined through a scheduled control review. Assign backup reviewers so planned absences do not routinely defeat approvals.

What if the practice manager handles most financial tasks?

Add an independent owner review at key points, such as vendor-bank changes, payroll changes, and payment release. Where staffing prevents full separation, document the limitation and the compensating review. Do not make the manager responsible for independently clearing exceptions involving their own activity.
