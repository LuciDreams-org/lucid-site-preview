Medical Practice Cost of Capital: Lessons From Telecom

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A private medical practice considering expensive equipment or a new facility faces a question familiar to capital-intensive businesses: will uncertain future cash generation justify committing capital today?

Telecom offers a useful—but limited—comparison. Network infrastructure and practice facilities can both require substantial spending before collections arrive. Financing payments and other fixed commitments may continue even when demand disappoints. However, telecom borrowing rates, public-company valuations, and regulatory return calculations are not medical-practice benchmarks.

For a staffed US private practice, the useful lesson is to match capital-cost assumptions to the investment's risks, useful life, financing terms, and exposure to uncertain collections. This article provides a framework for discussing those assumptions with a CPA, lender, CFO, or qualified valuation adviser—not a recommended financing rate or valuation conclusion.

What Cost of Capital Means for a Medical Practice

Cost of capital is the return required by those providing financing, reflecting the risks they bear. It is not simply the interest rate on an equipment loan.

  • Debt cost reflects borrowing terms, including interest, fees, and relevant financing conditions.
  • Equity cost reflects the return owners require for committing money to a risky, illiquid business rather than another opportunity. Retained practice earnings are not free capital.
  • Weighted average cost of capital, or WACC, combines debt and equity costs using appropriate financing weights and a consistent tax treatment.

The conventional expression is:

WACC = debt weight × debt cost × applicable tax adjustment + equity weight × equity cost

A qualified adviser should determine whether this framework fits the practice, what financing weights are supportable, and how taxes should be treated. Do not assume a standard corporate tax adjustment applies to an owner-operated practice. An accounting equity balance is also not automatically an appropriate valuation weight.

Three concepts should remain separate:

Concept Question it answers What it cannot establish alone
Loan interest rate What interest does the lender charge under the agreement? The required return on all capital committed
Cost of capital What return compensates capital providers for relevant risk? Whether the practice can meet next month's payroll
Project hurdle rate What return standard will the owners use for this investment? That every risk has been measured correctly

A project may appear attractive on a discounted basis yet create an unacceptable cash shortage during its ramp-up. Capital-cost analysis and liquidity planning are complementary tests.

What Transfers From Telecom—and What Does Not

The strongest comparison is the timing mismatch: money goes out before the investment proves its earning capacity.

Capital-intensive issue Telecom comparison Private-practice application
Upfront commitment Network construction precedes customer receipts Equipment installation or a build-out precedes incremental payer and patient collections
Fixed-cost exposure Infrastructure costs persist despite weak demand Loan payments, occupancy costs, and committed staffing can continue despite lower volume
Asset life Infrastructure may remain usable while technology changes Equipment can be physically usable but less economically attractive before it wears out
Financing maturity Borrowing may mature before the asset stops operating A practice may face a balloon payment while still relying on the asset
Uncertain cash generation Adoption and competitive conditions affect receipts Provider availability, payer mix, scheduling capacity, and collection timing affect receipts

The comparison stops there. A publicly traded telecom operator may have diversified operations, traded securities, and financing access unlike those of a closely held practice. A practice may depend heavily on a few providers, one location, particular payer contracts, or an owner's continued involvement.

Likewise, a telecom regulatory return calculation serves a different purpose from evaluating a US practice expansion. Importing its rate would create precision without establishing relevance.

Two Hypothetical Paired Cases

Every amount and time period in the following cases is an illustrative assumption, not a market quote, industry benchmark, or actual case study. The purpose is to identify which assumptions require scrutiny—not to recommend a rate.

Case 1: An Existing Network Upgrade and Equipment at an Established Practice

Imagine a telecom operator upgrading an existing network and a practice adding equipment at an established location. Both can examine operating history, but neither should assume that past demand guarantees future cash generation.

Illustrative assumption Telecom upgrade Medical practice equipment
Initial commitment $900,000 $240,000, including installation
Expected economic life 8 years 7 years
Financing structure 5-year borrowing with a balance due at maturity 5-year fully amortizing equipment loan
Fixed-cost exposure Financing and maintenance commitments Financing, service contract, and committed staff coverage
Main uncertainty Customer use and competitive pricing Utilization, net collections per service, and provider availability

For the practice, an established patient base may support a more grounded forecast than a new location would. But scheduled services are not cash receipts. The model needs assumptions for collections, timing, downtime, and the operating costs needed to use the equipment.

The financing distinction matters too. The illustrative practice loan is repaid before the equipment's assumed economic life ends. That avoids a scheduled balloon balance, but concentrates repayments into a shorter period. It does not automatically make the project affordable.

Capital-cost decision: ask the adviser whether the equipment project has risks comparable to the existing practice. Do not lower the required return merely because the lender accepts the equipment as collateral. Collateral protection for a lender does not eliminate the owners' operating risk.

Case 2: A New Network Area and a New Practice Location

Now compare entering a new service area with opening another practice location. Each requires spending before the new operation demonstrates sustainable receipts.

Illustrative assumption Telecom expansion Medical practice location
Initial commitment $3 million $750,000 for build-out, equipment, and opening liquidity
Expected economic life 12 years for the modeled infrastructure 10 years for the modeled improvements, subject to occupancy rights
Financing maturity 7 years, with refinancing needed for a remaining balance 5 years, with a balloon balance
Fixed-cost exposure Network operations and financing commitments Facility lease, provider/admin payroll, financing, and shared overhead
Main uncertainty Adoption in a new service area Provider recruitment, patient demand, payer receipts, and time to stable operations

For the practice, the modeled life of improvements must be considered alongside the facility lease and renewal provisions. A long physical life does not ensure the practice can use the asset for that entire period.

The balloon payment also creates a separate risk: the location could be operating adequately when refinancing becomes expensive or unavailable. A long-lived asset does not justify assuming future borrowing will be available on today's terms.

Capital-cost decision: do not automatically apply the established location's assumptions to the expansion. Ask whether the new location warrants different cash-flow scenarios, a different required return, or both. Document the distinction rather than adding an unexplained risk premium.

Build a Cost-of-Capital Input Worksheet

Separate documented terms from estimates and management judgments. A precise spreadsheet result is not reliable when its inputs are unsupported.

Input group What to record Supporting material or reviewer
Quoted debt terms Amount, fixed or variable rate, reset terms, fees, amortization, maturity, and balloon balance Current written lender proposal
Debt restrictions Collateral, guarantees, covenants, prepayment provisions, and other borrowing constraints Lender documents; legal and financial review
Equity assumptions Proposed required-return range, rationale, and valuation approach Qualified valuation or finance adviser
Financing weights Proposed debt/equity mix and why it fits the analysis Adviser-supported financing and valuation assumptions
Tax treatment Whether and how taxes enter the model CPA review specific to the entities and owners
Economic asset life Useful service period, replacement exposure, and residual-value assumptions Vendor information, contracts, and management assessment
Project-specific risks Provider dependence, payer concentration, utilization, opening delays, and collection uncertainty Practice operating history and explicit scenarios
Entity and location scope Borrower, asset owner, cash-generating location, and shared-cost treatment Entity-level books, agreements, and adviser review
Model consistency Cash-flow definition, discount-rate basis, and treatment of financing CFO or valuation adviser review

Keep Debt Evidence Separate From Equity Judgment

A written loan quote is evidence of proposed borrowing terms, not evidence of the owners' required return. Record its date and conditions; an indicative proposal is not an executed financing agreement.

Equity assumptions require a different process. A private practice generally cannot observe its own traded share price or stock-market risk measure. Qualified advisers may use relevant valuation evidence and methods, but the owner should ask why the selected comparisons fit the practice's size, concentration, ownership, and operating risks.

Do not substitute a telecom company's equity-return estimate because it is publicly available.

Describe Risks Before Assigning Rate Adjustments

Write down the mechanism through which each risk affects cash:

  • A provider vacancy may delay utilization and collections.
  • An opening delay may extend rent and payroll spending before receipts begin.
  • A change in payer mix may reduce realized collections.
  • Equipment downtime may interrupt receipts while financing costs continue.
  • Refinancing uncertainty may create a large cash requirement at maturity.

Many risks belong explicitly in cash-flow scenarios. If an adviser also adjusts the discount rate, ask how the model avoids counting the same risk twice. Not every operational concern translates sensibly into an added percentage point.

Review the Model Without Mixing Financing and Operating Returns

Before accepting a capital-cost conclusion, work through these checks with the finance team.

1. Define the Cash Flows Being Discounted

A WACC-based enterprise analysis generally uses operating cash flows before debt financing payments, with taxes treated consistently. Cash flows remaining for owners after debt service call for a different framework and an appropriate equity return.

Do not deduct principal and interest from a cash-flow stream and then automatically discount it using WACC. Have the adviser confirm that the cash flows and discount rate match.

2. Compare Economic Life With Financing and Facility Terms

Place expected asset life, loan maturity, interest-rate resets, lease expiration, and replacement spending on the same timeline. Flag any point where the model assumes continued use, renewal, sale proceeds, or refinancing without support.

3. Test Uncertainty Separately From the Central Estimate

Use a base case and specific downside cases rather than hiding uncertainty in a single rate. Show what happens if collections start later, utilization stays lower, or refinancing terms worsen. Review sensitivity to adviser-supported discount-rate assumptions separately.

A project that works only under one optimistic combination needs revision, not a more convenient cost of capital.

4. Protect Practice Operating Liquidity

Prepare a cash schedule showing payer receipts, patient-balance collections, provider/admin payroll, occupancy costs, and financing payments. Keep this liquidity test distinct from the valuation analysis.

For multi-location practices, examine each location's operating contribution and shared-cost allocations. For multi-entity owners, identify which entity borrows, owns the asset, and generates the cash expected to service the obligation. Do not assume cash can move freely among entities; obtain appropriate advice.

Turn the Analysis Into an Owner Decision

The decision memo should identify the proposed commitment, documented financing terms, adviser-supported capital-cost range, principal uncertainties, and conditions that must be satisfied before signing. Conditions might include a revised maturity structure, confirmed provider staffing, or a smaller initial commitment.

Reliable inputs start with clean financial records. The IRS guidance on starting and maintaining business records supports keeping complete records, separate business accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those practices help establish what the business has actually collected and spent; they do not determine its cost of capital.

FAQs

Can a medical practice use a telecom company's WACC?

Not as a direct benchmark. Telecom capital structures, scale, financing access, and operating risks differ from those of a closely held medical practice. Use telecom to understand long-lived commitments and uncertain cash generation, then ask qualified advisers to develop practice-relevant assumptions.

Is the equipment loan rate the practice's cost of capital?

No. It represents one component of financing cost. Owners also commit capital and bear risks that the lender's rate does not fully describe. Fees, maturity, guarantees, and other terms also matter when evaluating the borrowing proposal.

Should every practice location use the same discount rate?

Not necessarily, but different rates should have a defensible basis. An established location and an unproven expansion may have different risks. Some differences are better represented in cash-flow scenarios than in discount-rate adjustments. Review both with a qualified adviser and avoid double-counting risk.

Does a longer loan term make a long-lived asset a better investment?

Not by itself. A longer term may spread payments over more periods, but interest costs, resets, restrictions, and refinancing exposure still matter. Compare the financing schedule with economic asset life and facility rights, then assess both required return and operating liquidity.
