Budget Allocation for Multi-Location Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A multi-location medical practice should not divide its next budget equally—or simply give the most money to the office with the highest collections. Each location may have different provider coverage, staffing requirements, room capacity, lease obligations, and opportunities to use additional resources productively.

The owner's decision is more specific: Which commitments must remain funded, and where will the next available budget dollar solve a demonstrated operating constraint?

Answering that question requires two separate views. A location-level profit and loss statement shows how each office performs after direct expenses and shared overhead. An incremental spending analysis shows what would actually change if you approved a hire, additional provider sessions, equipment, or expanded hours. Confusing those views can lead to cutting a useful location or funding an expansion that does not address its real bottleneck.

This guide explains how to build the next location budget, document shared-cost allocations, and compare competing requests. It is a resource-allocation process—not a department budget template or a cash forecast.

1. Establish a Comparable Financial Baseline

Before discussing new spending, prepare a consistent financial view for every location. Use the same reporting period, account definitions, and accounting basis across the practice. Identify unusual expenses separately so they do not silently become next year's recurring budget.

Start with:

  • Revenue or collections attributed to the location where services were delivered, using a consistent method.
  • Provider and administrative payroll, including employer payroll costs and benefits.
  • Rent, utilities, supplies, equipment costs, and other location-specific expenses.
  • Shared services that support multiple offices.
  • Approved commitments that have not yet appeared fully in historical results.

Payer receipts and patient payments may arrive after the related services. A strong collections month can reflect older balances rather than current operating capacity. When comparing offices, explain these timing effects and avoid treating charges, earned revenue, and cash receipts as interchangeable measures.

Reconcile the underlying books before using them to justify allocations. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts, and reconciliation of bank records. Those practices help establish a reliable starting point; they do not determine which office deserves additional funding.

For multi-entity practices, keep legal-entity accounts and location reporting distinct. A location allocation is a management view, not permission to move expenses or cash between entities without appropriate accounting review.

2. Separate Direct Expenses From Shared Overhead

Assign an expense directly when reliable records identify the location that incurred it. Allocate only the portion that genuinely serves multiple locations.

Expense Preferred treatment Supporting record
Rent for one office Direct expense of that location Lease and invoice
Staff working at one location Direct payroll expense Payroll roster and work assignment
Staff rotating between offices Assign using documented work time Schedules or time records
Supplies delivered to a specific office Direct expense where identifiable Purchase and delivery records
Central scheduling team Shared cost allocated using a relevant workload measure Scheduling workload report
Practice-wide finance support Shared overhead using a documented driver Service scope and workload records
Equipment used at one location Direct location assignment, with accounting treatment reviewed separately Asset register and purchase approval

Do not allocate an expense merely because it appears on a central credit card or is paid from a headquarters account. Payment method does not establish which location used the resource.

Likewise, do not charge every shared employee equally across offices if schedules show materially different use. Direct assignment often produces a more useful result than an elaborate allocation formula.

3. Choose and Document Shared-Cost Drivers

A shared-cost driver should reasonably reflect resource use, be practical to maintain, and remain understandable to location managers. No single driver is appropriate for every overhead category.

Shared cost pool Possible allocation driver What to check
Central scheduling Scheduling transactions or documented staff time Whether some locations require more work per transaction
Shared operational management Time by location or a documented workload estimate Whether opening or turnaround work creates temporary demands
Shared administrative workspace Assigned space or supported headcount Whether the arrangement actually follows that measure
Organization-wide systems Active users or assigned licenses Whether charges contain fixed and variable components
Central billing support Documented workload or a consistent activity measure Whether complexity differs across locations

Revenue can be a practical driver for some general overhead, but it should not become the default for every cost. Higher reimbursement does not necessarily create more administrative work. Conversely, a lower-collection office may consume substantial shared support.

Use this calculation for each pool:

Location allocation = shared cost pool × location driver units ÷ total driver units

Illustrative example: A monthly central scheduling budget is $18,000. Location A represents 50% of the selected workload measure, Location B represents 30%, and Location C represents 20%. The allocations are $9,000, $5,400, and $3,600. These amounts assign the existing budget; they do not prove that closing Location C would eliminate its $3,600 allocation.

For every allocation rule, record:

  • Which accounts belong in the pool.
  • The driver, source report, and measurement period.
  • Why the driver reasonably reflects usage.
  • Who approves the rule and any exceptions.
  • When the rule will be reviewed.

Keep the method stable enough for meaningful comparisons. If circumstances justify changing it, show managers how much of the reported variance comes from the method rather than operating performance.

4. Read Location Profitability in Layers

A fully allocated location P&L is useful, but it should not be the only view used for expansion or reduction decisions.

Show at least these layers:

  1. Revenue or collections on the stated reporting basis.
  2. Direct location expenses.
  3. Contribution before shared overhead: the amount remaining to support central costs and practice earnings.
  4. Allocated shared overhead.
  5. Location result after allocation.

Then distinguish costs that would change under a proposed decision from those that would remain. A location may report a loss after overhead allocation while still contributing toward central expenses that cannot immediately be reduced.

The reverse also matters: an office with a strong historical margin may need substantial additional staffing or equipment before it can handle more activity. Its current margin is not proof that expansion will be equally profitable.

5. Identify the Constraint Before Approving Incremental Spending

Ask each location manager to describe the operating problem before submitting a spending solution. “We need another employee” is a request, not an analysis.

For a staffing proposal, examine provider schedules, administrative workload, coverage gaps, overtime, and room availability together. Additional support staff will not create usable capacity if provider sessions or suitable rooms are unavailable. More provider time may not translate into completed visits if check-in, scheduling, or other support is already constrained.

Observed constraint Evidence to review Potential budget response
Provider sessions available but support coverage is insufficient Schedules, coverage gaps, overtime, completed activity Additional support hours or a targeted hire
Rooms constrain usable sessions Room schedules and overlapping assignments Schedule redesign before committing to more space
Central administrative workload is delaying routine work Backlogs, workload by location, staffing coverage Reallocate shared staff or add targeted capacity
Demand is insufficient for existing capacity Appointment availability, utilization, cancellation patterns Avoid adding fixed staffing without a stronger demand case
Equipment availability limits planned operations Utilization records and scheduling conflicts Compare purchase, rental, or shared use where appropriate

Separate the cost of maintaining current operations from the cost of expanding capacity. Required replacement spending, existing lease commitments, and necessary staffing coverage should not compete on exactly the same terms as discretionary growth proposals.

6. Compare the Full Incremental Cost of Each Request

For each proposal, estimate the costs and operational changes that would occur because of approval. Do not use an allocated overhead charge as a substitute for this analysis.

A staffing request should account for more than base pay. Include applicable employer payroll costs, benefits, recruitment, onboarding, training coverage, and any equipment or workspace needed. Identify when spending begins and when the added capacity could realistically become usable.

Illustrative example: competing location requests

Request Incremental budget Constraint addressed Evidence required before approval
Location A: added support-staff coverage $24,000 for the budget period Existing provider sessions lack adequate support coverage Coverage plan and evidence that rooms and demand are available
Location B: extended office hours $32,000 for the budget period Demand cannot be served within current hours Provider commitment, staffing coverage, and credible demand evidence
Location C: additional equipment $20,000 upfront, plus ongoing costs Existing equipment availability limits scheduled operations Utilization data, alternatives, and full ownership costs

The figures are hypothetical and are not pricing or return benchmarks. Location A is not automatically the best choice because it costs less. Location C's upfront purchase is not directly comparable with recurring payroll until the analysis includes the relevant budget period and ongoing obligations.

Compare proposals using a common worksheet:

  • Expected added usable capacity and the evidence supporting it.
  • Expected incremental revenue or collections, with assumptions stated.
  • Incremental operating costs and one-time implementation costs.
  • Dependencies, such as hiring, provider availability, or space.
  • A downside case if demand or implementation falls short.
  • A review point and conditions for continuing, modifying, or stopping the spend.

For financial break-even analysis, compare incremental revenue with the costs that actually increase. If using collections instead, account for receipt timing explicitly. Neither calculation replaces clinical or operational judgment about appropriate staffing.

7. Approve a Location Budget With Clear Ownership

Turn the analysis into an approved allocation schedule, not just a meeting discussion. Each location should know its direct expense budget, assigned shared overhead, and authorized incremental spending.

Document whether an approval is recurring, temporary, or conditional. Assign an owner for implementation and record what evidence is needed before releasing conditional funds. If the budget allows flexibility, define which changes managers may make and which require owner approval.

Keep resource approval separate from cash availability. A proposal may be justified economically but still require a phased start because payroll and equipment payments precede payer receipts. Confirm funding in a separate cash forecast rather than treating the budget itself as proof that cash will be available.

For multiple entities, have the accounting team review which entity incurs each cost and how shared services or intercompany balances are recorded. Management allocations and entity-level accounting should reconcile without obscuring the underlying transactions.

8. Review Results Without Moving the Goalposts

At each budget review, explain variances in categories that support a decision:

  • Activity: more or fewer completed services or administrative transactions than planned.
  • Rate: changes in compensation, supplier prices, or receipts per service.
  • Timing: delayed hiring, implementation, or collections.
  • Allocation: changes in shared workload or the allocation method.
  • Execution: approved capacity that has not become usable as expected.

Review incremental projects against their original assumptions. If a hire is in place but the related provider sessions never materialize, the issue is not simply an unfavorable payroll variance. The proposal's dependency failed and needs an operating decision.

Use aggregated operational and financial information where possible. Keep patient-identifiable information out of ordinary Slack discussions and general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains the standard and its exceptions; practices should establish appropriate workflows for their circumstances rather than assume every financial analysis needs patient-level detail.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should every location receive the same budget increase?

No. Start with existing commitments, then evaluate each location's constraints and incremental proposals. Equal increases can overfund unused capacity while leaving a documented coverage problem unresolved elsewhere.

Should shared overhead always be allocated by revenue?

No. Select a driver suited to the cost. Documented staff time, active users, scheduling workload, or another practical measure may better reflect consumption. Use revenue only when you can explain why it is reasonable for that pool.

Should a location with a loss after overhead allocation be cut?

Not solely on that result. Review its contribution before shared overhead, the expenses that would actually disappear, and any transition costs or operational effects. Allocated overhead may remain with the practice even if the location stops operating.

The Allocation Decision to Aim For

A useful location budget makes three things visible: what each office directly consumes, how shared costs are assigned, and what additional spending is expected to change. Fund existing commitments deliberately, test expansion requests against actual constraints, and document the assumptions behind every material decision.
