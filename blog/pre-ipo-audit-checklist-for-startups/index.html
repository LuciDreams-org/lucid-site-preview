Medical Practice Audit Checklist Before Outside Investment

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Before accepting significant outside investment, a private medical practice may be asked for audited financial statements, a financial review, or transaction-specific diligence. These are different engagements. The first decision is not which documents to upload—it is what financial work the investor requires and whether the practice can support it.

For a staffed practice, readiness means being able to explain how payer receipts, patient balances, provider and administrative payroll, debt, leases, and owner transactions flow into the financial statements. A balanced trial balance is only the starting point. The underlying amounts need support.

This medical practice audit checklist helps owners identify evidence gaps, assign responsibility, and discuss the appropriate engagement with their CPA, prospective auditor, transaction advisers, and counsel. It is not IPO preparation, and completing it does not guarantee an audit opinion or an investment closing.

1. Confirm What the Investor Actually Requires

Ask for the financial reporting requirements in writing before commissioning work. An investor's request for “audited numbers” may need clarification about the periods, entities, accounting framework, and level of assurance required.

Engagement What it generally provides What the practice owner should clarify
Financial statement audit Reasonable, not absolute, assurance that financial statements are free from material misstatement; the auditor expresses an opinion Which statements, periods, entities, and reporting framework are covered?
Financial statement review Limited assurance, primarily through inquiries and analytical procedures; substantially narrower than an audit Will a review satisfy the investor's written requirements?
Compilation Presentation of financial information without assurance Does the recipient understand that a compilation does not verify the financial statements?
Transaction diligence Analysis tailored to the transaction, potentially including earnings quality, collections, working capital, and debt-like items What questions will the adviser investigate, and what report or findings will be delivered?

Transaction diligence is not a substitute for a financial statement audit and does not ordinarily provide an audit opinion. An investor may request both because they answer different questions.

Management and the relevant professionals should also agree on:

  • Reporting entities: The operating practice alone, multiple practices, a management entity, or a combined or consolidated reporting group.
  • Reporting periods: The historical years and any interim period required.
  • Accounting framework: The basis the investor will accept and any necessary conversion from the practice's current records.
  • Deliverables and timing: Financial statements, disclosures, schedules, and report expectations.
  • Responsibilities and independence: Who prepares the accounts and schedules, who makes management judgments, and what assistance the auditor can provide under applicable independence requirements.

Do not assume a cash-basis tax return or bookkeeping report meets the investor's financial reporting requirements.

2. Establish a Reliable Starting Point

Before responding to individual requests, identify the version of the books everyone will use. Maintain a dated trial balance, general ledger, and financial statement package for each reporting entity and period.

Record subsequent adjustments separately, including their rationale and management approval. Otherwise, the payroll schedule, revenue reconciliation, and financial statements may all reflect different versions of the accounts.

Opening balances and prior-period support

Opening balances can be a significant obstacle, especially when historical bookkeeping was incomplete or prior statements were not audited. Ask the prospective auditor early what evidence may be needed.

Prepare:

  • Prior-period financial statements, trial balances, and available accountant reports.
  • Beginning bank reconciliations and supporting statements.
  • Beginning receivable and payable detail.
  • Fixed-asset, accumulated depreciation, debt, lease, and equity rollforwards.
  • A record of prior-period adjustments and unresolved differences.

A current bank statement cannot substantiate every historical balance. If records are missing, describe the gap rather than creating unsupported opening entries. The auditor determines whether available procedures can provide sufficient appropriate evidence and how limitations affect the engagement.

Cash and reconciliation discipline

Reconcile each bank account and credit card account to the ledger, including dormant accounts and accounts used by separate entities. Investigate old outstanding items, unidentified deposits, and transfers that appear on only one side of the books.

The IRS business recordkeeping guide supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices help establish a reliable starting point, but they do not replace audit procedures.

3. Assemble Evidence for the Major Account Areas

Use this checklist to identify whether each balance has a supporting schedule, source evidence, and a person who can explain it. The auditor will determine testing and may request additional records or direct confirmations.

Area Prepare and reconcile Issues to resolve before fieldwork
Revenue and receivables Billing-system totals, payer and patient collections, adjustments, receivable aging, and ledger reconciliation Unexplained differences; unsupported allowances; receipts recorded in the wrong period
Payroll and liabilities Payroll registers, provider compensation calculations, payroll tax records, benefit invoices, and liability rollforwards Unpaid bonuses; payroll cutoff; outstanding taxes or benefit obligations
Debt Executed agreements, lender statements, payment schedules, interest calculations, and balances by entity Missing loans; owner guarantees; covenant matters; incorrect classification
Leases Executed leases, amendments, payment schedules, deposits, and accounting schedules Unrecorded amendments; renewal assumptions; related-party leases
Related parties Owner and affiliate transaction schedules, agreements, balances, and repayment terms Personal expenses; undocumented transfers; inconsistent treatment across entities
Ownership and equity Governing documents, ownership ledger, contribution and distribution records, and approved changes Equity records that do not match the ledger; undocumented owner payments
Other assets and liabilities Fixed-asset detail, invoices, payable listings, accruals, and subsequent settlement evidence Disposed equipment still recorded; missing invoices; unrecorded obligations

Revenue: Bridge the Billing System to the Financial Statements

For each reporting period, explain the relationship among services recorded, charges, contractual adjustments, other adjustments, collections, and ending receivables. Gross charges are not interchangeable with recognized revenue or cash receipts.

Separate payer receipts from patient collections where useful. Reconcile payment processor settlements and deposits, identifying fees, refunds, timing differences, and unapplied cash. For accrual-based reporting, support estimates affecting receivable collectibility and revenue under the agreed framework.

Review activity around period-end. A deposit received after year-end may relate to a prior-period receivable rather than new-period revenue. Conversely, a billing-system report may include activity outside the financial reporting period.

For multiple locations, document how revenue is assigned to each location and how location reports reconcile to entity totals. Do not assume location profitability reports are complete financial statements.

Payroll: Explain What Was Earned, Paid, and Still Owed

Reconcile provider and administrative payroll expense to payroll registers, cash payments, and outstanding liabilities. Include compensation earned but not yet paid, such as contractual provider bonuses, where required by the reporting framework.

Keep support for withholding, employer payroll taxes, benefits, and other deductions or obligations. Explain differences between payroll reports and the ledger rather than forcing totals to agree with unexplained entries.

Use employment agreements and approved compensation terms to support calculations. Ask the CPA to help determine applicable record-retention requirements; there is no single retention period that covers every payroll document and circumstance.

4. Resolve Entity, Ownership, and Related-Party Questions

Outside investment can expose accounting ambiguity that routine bookkeeping has carried forward. A practice may pay rent to an owner-controlled property company, share administrative staff with another practice, or receive owner advances that were never clearly documented.

Prepare a legal-entity diagram and reconcile it to the entities in the proposed reporting scope. Counsel should confirm ownership documentation and legal questions; the accounting team should establish how transactions are recorded and disclosed under the selected framework.

For each related-party balance or transaction, document:

  • The parties and their relationship.
  • The business purpose and relevant agreement.
  • Amounts recorded during the period and outstanding at period-end.
  • Whether the amount represents compensation, reimbursement, a loan, a contribution, a distribution, or another category.
  • Any unresolved classification or disclosure question.

If entities share payroll, rent, or administrative costs, explain the allocation method and reconcile intercompany balances. For combined or consolidated statements, identify applicable elimination entries. Do not change an allocation simply to improve a location's reported margin before investor review.

5. Document Accounting Judgments and Evidence Gaps

Some balances depend on judgment, not just invoices. Maintain a short accounting-issues log so management, the preparer, and the auditor can discuss significant questions early.

Judgment or issue Management's support Decision needed
Receivable collectibility Aging, collection history, subsequent receipts, and explanation of unusual balances Whether the estimate is supported under the reporting framework
Provider compensation accrual Contract terms, production or collection inputs, and payment calculations Amount and period of expense recognition
Lease accounting Agreements, amendments, and documented assumptions Appropriate treatment under the selected framework
Owner-funded expenses Source records and documented intent Loan, contribution, reimbursement, or another classification
Historical bookkeeping corrections Original entry, proposed correction, and affected periods Treatment and any disclosure implications

Management remains responsible for the financial statements and its judgments. Mark unresolved matters as open; an unsupported estimate does not become reliable merely because it appears in a polished schedule.

Also document who approves payments, changes payroll information, reconciles accounts, and reviews journal entries. In a smaller practice, one employee may perform several tasks. Describe the actual process and owner review rather than claiming segregation of duties that does not exist. Auditor inquiries may address controls even when the engagement does not include an opinion on internal control.

6. Run an Auditor-Request Tracker

Use one tracker for requests, responsibilities, and evidence status. Assign one practice contact to coordinate responses, while keeping technical responsibility with the staff member who understands the underlying account.

Illustrative example: The tracker below describes a hypothetical staffed practice. Roles and statuses should be adapted to the actual engagement.

Request Responsible staff Evidence status Next action
Opening receivables detail Billing manager and controller Open—historical aging missing Locate archived report and discuss alternatives with auditor
Year-end bank reconciliations Bookkeeper Prepared—not yet reviewed Controller checks ledger tie-out and outstanding items
Provider bonus accrual Payroll administrator and owner Partial—calculation available Obtain approved compensation terms and reconcile inputs
Debt schedule Controller Ready for auditor review Await auditor instructions for any lender confirmations
Lease population Practice administrator Open—one amendment missing Obtain executed amendment and update schedule
Related-party balances Owner and outside CPA Partial—classification unresolved Document purpose and proposed accounting treatment
Ownership rollforward Owner and counsel Ready for auditor review Confirm agreement with equity accounts

“Uploaded” is not the same as “complete.” A request should be marked ready only after the schedule ties to the agreed trial balance, supporting evidence is included, and known exceptions are explained. The auditor may still require additional work.

Do not preselect favorable samples or send confirmations on the auditor's behalf without instructions. Let the auditor control procedures that require independent selection or communication.

7. Protect Patient Information During Evidence Collection

Financial evidence may contain patient information, but many initial reconciliations can use aggregated or de-identified reports. Ask what information is actually necessary before exporting detailed billing records.

Accounting services involving protected health information can raise business-associate responsibilities, as described in the HHS HIPAA Privacy Rule overview. Coordinate appropriate arrangements and transfer methods with the relevant professionals. Follow applicable privacy requirements, including the minimum necessary standard where it applies.

Do not put patient-identifiable reports into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Keep status updates separate from sensitive supporting records.

Decide Whether the Practice Is Ready to Begin

Before fieldwork, hold a readiness meeting with the owner, practice administrator, accounting lead, and prospective auditor. Review opening balances, unreconciled accounts, missing agreements, significant estimates, and staff availability.

Ask whether unresolved items require additional preparation time or could affect the scope, timetable, or report. Discuss these issues before promising an investor a delivery date.
