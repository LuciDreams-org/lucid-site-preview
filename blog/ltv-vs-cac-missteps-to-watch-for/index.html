LTV vs. CAC: Mistakes Medical Practices Should Avoid

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A marketing report can show more inquiries and appointments while the practice’s cash position barely improves. Before renewing a campaign, an owner needs to understand what the channel cost, what services the resulting patient cohort received, and how much contribution the practice actually collected after attributable service costs.

LTV, or lifetime value, and CAC, or customer acquisition cost, offer a useful financial question: Does the economic contribution associated with an acquisition effort justify its cost? But subscription-business formulas can produce misleading answers for a staffed private medical practice.

Patient relationships are not subscriptions. Billed charges are not collections. Repeat visits do not establish a predictable financial lifetime, and an attractive ratio does not prove that additional marketing will improve profit.

A more defensible starting point is to compare documented acquisition spending with collected contribution over a stated observation period, while making collection delays, attribution uncertainty, and capacity limits visible.

Mistake 1: Treating Patient Relationships Like Recurring Subscriptions

A subscription LTV calculation often assumes recurring revenue and a measurable cancellation rate. A medical practice usually cannot make equivalent assumptions about future encounters, payer reimbursement, patient responsibility, or the duration of a relationship.

A patient who does not return may have completed the needed care. Another patient may return after a long interval. Neither pattern establishes a stable financial retention rate. Care decisions must remain based on patient needs, not a projected revenue target.

Avoid turning these observations into unsupported lifetime value:

Observation What it establishes What it does not establish
Several completed visits Services occurred during the observed period The same visit pattern will continue indefinitely
High billed charges Charges were recorded The amount or timing of eventual collections
A scheduled follow-up An appointment is scheduled Attendance, payment, or future contribution
A history of returning patients Some relationships continue over time A reliable subscription-style churn rate

Longer-term scenarios can be useful for planning, but label them as projections and show their assumptions separately. Do not describe a short observation period as measured lifetime value.

Mistake 2: Using Charges or Receipts Alone as Value

Charges can differ from the amounts ultimately collected after contractual adjustments, denials, patient balances, and other payment outcomes. Even collections do not tell the whole story: providers, staff, supplies, and other resources were required to deliver the services.

For channel analysis, define the measures explicitly:

Collected contribution = receipts attributable to the cohort’s included services, net of refunds and recoupments, minus attributable service costs.

Observed contribution after acquisition = collected contribution minus documented acquisition costs.

“Attributable service costs” should follow a written policy. Depending on the practice, they may include provider compensation, clinical support labor, supplies, outside service costs, and billing costs associated with those services. Include an allocation for owner-provided clinical labor when relevant, and explain its basis rather than treating that labor as free.

Do not count acquisition-related administrative time again as a service cost. Likewise, do not allocate the same payroll expense in full to multiple channels.

This contribution measure is not practice net income. Rent, leadership time, shared administration, and other costs may remain outside it. State the exclusions and reconcile the analysis to the practice’s P&L before using it to support a broader profitability claim.

Mistake 3: Calling Ad Spend the Entire Acquisition Cost

Dividing an advertising invoice by new appointments produces a narrow measure, not necessarily a complete acquisition cost.

For a channel cohort, use:

CAC = documented acquisition costs assigned to the cohort ÷ attributable new patients with a completed first visit.

Define “new patient” for this analysis. A returning patient, a transfer between locations, and a genuinely new relationship should not be mixed without explanation. Track inquiries, bookings, cancellations, and no-shows separately rather than counting all of them as acquired patients.

Document the cost scope before comparing channels:

Cost category Practical treatment
Advertising and campaign fees Assign direct costs to the relevant campaign and acquisition period
Agency, creative, and website work Separate channel-specific work from shared or longer-lived work; document allocations
Acquisition-related administrative labor Include supported portions of inquiry handling and first-visit scheduling, with related employer costs
Marketing tools Allocate shared costs using a consistent, explainable basis
Owner or manager outreach time Identify whether it is included; distinguish an estimated time cost from recorded payroll expense
Ongoing patient administration Classify as service support or overhead under the policy, not automatically as acquisition

A direct-spend view can help with an immediate campaign decision. A broader allocated-cost view helps assess sustainability. Label each clearly rather than presenting either as the single indisputable CAC.

Mistake 4: Assuming Attribution Is Certain

A patient may encounter the practice through several sources before booking. A recorded search-ad click or an intake answer does not necessarily prove that the channel caused the visit.

Choose a consistent attribution rule, such as a documented primary source, and disclose its limitations. Keep unknown sources visible. Do not redistribute them across paid channels merely to make the report complete.

Distinguish practice-new patients from location transfers and existing-patient reactivation. For multi-location owners, record both the attributed acquisition channel and the location delivering care. Otherwise, one location may carry the marketing expense while another receives the collections.

Attribution is not the same as incrementality: some attributed patients might have found the practice without the campaign. A cohort worksheet supports a spending decision, but it cannot by itself prove how much additional demand the campaign created.

Build an Aggregate Channel-Cohort Worksheet

A cohort is a defined group sharing an acquisition period and source. Use aggregate financial reporting rather than patient-level detail in the owner’s worksheet. Any necessary record-level matching should occur within an appropriately governed workflow.

The example shows a positive observed contribution after acquisition. It does not establish lifetime value or total practice profit. Remaining overhead, later payment adjustments, and capacity effects still matter.

Acquisition spending may precede the first visit. Document how earlier campaign spending is assigned to the cohort; do not simply divide this month’s invoices by this month’s first visits when the timing differs. If meaningful spending cannot be assigned reliably, show it as unallocated rather than forcing a precise channel result.

Mistake 5: Comparing Cohorts With Different Collection Maturity

A recently acquired cohort may look weak because services have occurred but payer and patient receipts have not arrived. An older cohort has had more time to collect. Comparing their totals directly can reward age rather than channel performance.

Use consistent service windows and collection follow-up periods. Include service costs for all included services, not only those already paid. Match receipts to those services rather than including unrelated deposits received during the same calendar period.

Keep these items separate:

  • Collected receipts through the stated cutoff.
  • Outstanding balances, which are not treated as collected contribution.
  • Unresolved claims and known refunds or recoupments.
  • Later changes to previously reported results.

If a cohort is immature, label it preliminary. Maintain an outstanding-balance schedule rather than assuming every balance will collect or treating every unpaid claim as a permanent loss.

Reconcile aggregate receipts to billing reports and bank activity, explaining differences such as deposit timing and refunds. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping provides a foundation for complete records and bank reconciliation, although it does not prescribe this marketing worksheet.

Mistake 6: Ignoring Capacity and Cash Timing

A favorable historical result does not mean the next increment of spending will perform similarly. A practice with open appointments faces a different decision from one whose clinicians and administrative team are fully occupied.

Before expanding a channel, ask:

  • Can the relevant location accommodate additional appointments?
  • Would growth require overtime, additional staffing, or expanded space?
  • Are scheduling delays, missed calls, or cancellations limiting completed visits?
  • Could the campaign displace demand that would otherwise fill the same appointments?
  • Can available cash support campaign spending and payroll before collections arrive?

Costs also behave differently over time. Existing payroll may not change for a small increase in visits, but another increment of demand could require a new hire. Show incremental costs for the immediate decision alongside allocated service costs for the broader sustainability view.

Observed acquisition payback occurs when cumulative collected contribution covers acquisition spending under the documented cost policy. It does not necessarily establish cash payback if some included costs are allocations rather than cash outflows in that period. A cash forecast should show actual payment timing separately.

Decide Whether to Continue, Limit, or Pause Spending

Do not replace judgment with a universal LTV-to-CAC target. Use a short decision memo that records the evidence and its limits.

Decision Evidence to consider
Continue at the current level Mature results support the spending, cash timing is manageable, and capacity remains available
Run a bounded additional test Attribution, sample size, or collection maturity is uncertain; set a spending cap and review point
Reallocate between channels or locations Comparable cohorts show different contribution, with sufficient capacity where spending would move
Pause expansion or reduce spending Mature results remain unfavorable, or staffing, collections, and capacity problems undermine the economics

Record the owner, next review date, unresolved balances, and conditions that would change the decision. Repeated comparable cohorts are more useful than one apparently strong month.

FAQs

What is a good LTV-to-CAC ratio for a medical practice?

There is no universal target established here. A subscription benchmark does not account for a practice’s collection timing, service costs, capacity, and overhead. Start with observed contribution over a defined period and assess whether the result supports the specific spending decision.

Should repeat visits count in the analysis?

Include completed services within the stated observation window and their attributable costs. Count only receipts associated with those services through the stated collection cutoff. Do not extrapolate observed visits into an assumed lifetime or encourage additional care to improve a financial metric.

What if our practice cannot reliably connect collections to marketing sources?

Do not manufacture a precise channel return. Report known acquisition costs, source-data completeness, and aggregate practice collections separately. Improve source capture and reconciliation, keep unknowns visible, and limit conclusions until a defensible cohort comparison is possible.

Should a new channel be stopped before its claims finish collecting?

Not automatically. Separate preliminary collection results from evidence of poor performance. A bounded test may allow more observation without committing to expansion. However, cash constraints or capacity problems may justify pausing spending even before the cohort matures.
