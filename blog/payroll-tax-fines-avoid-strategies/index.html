Reducing Payroll Tax Penalty Risk in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can pay employees on time and still have an unresolved payroll tax problem. A tax payment may fail, a return may be rejected, or a deposit may be applied to the wrong entity or period. These problems require different evidence than a completed payroll run.

Outsourcing payroll does not eliminate the need for owner oversight. Your practice needs someone responsible for checking that payroll funding, tax deposits, filings and accounting records agree—not simply assuming that the provider completed every step.

The goal is not for a physician-owner to become a payroll tax specialist. It is to build a repeatable control process that identifies missing confirmations, assigns follow-up and gets exceptions to a qualified professional promptly. Have a payroll tax professional determine applicable deadlines, correction procedures and any available relief options.

1. Assign Responsibility Before the Next Payroll

Start with a written division of responsibilities among the practice manager, payroll provider, bookkeeper and owner. Confirm what the provider’s engagement actually covers, including tax payments, returns, amended filings and notice assistance.

A provider’s promise to investigate is not the same as a completed correction. Every unresolved item needs an internal owner and a follow-up date.

Control Primary responsibility Independent review or escalation
Submit approved hours, compensation changes and work-location changes Practice manager Owner or designated approver

For a smaller staffed practice, one employee may perform several tasks. In that case, the owner or an outside accounting professional should review confirmations and exceptions independently. Name a backup for absences, and make sure essential notices do not go only to a former administrator’s email address.

2. Maintain an Entity and Jurisdiction Register

A location and a legal employer are not necessarily the same thing. One employer may operate several offices, while related practices may employ staff through separate entities. Per-location P&L allocations should not obscure which entity owes the payroll taxes.

Maintain a register that connects each payroll account to its employer, funding account and professionally confirmed tax requirements.

Field What to document Why it matters
Legal employer Legal name, EIN and payroll account identifier Helps prevent use of the wrong entity’s account
Locations and staff assignments Offices and employees associated with the employer Connects operational changes to payroll setup
Jurisdictions Federal, state and local accounts confirmed as applicable Makes missing or outdated registrations visible

Review the register when opening an office, moving an employee’s work location, adding remote administrative staff, changing entities or switching providers. Ask the payroll tax professional to evaluate jurisdictional implications rather than assuming that an existing setup covers the change.

If staff costs are shared across locations or entities, keep the management allocation separate from the employer’s tax reconciliation. An allocation that improves location reporting does not itself establish the correct payroll tax treatment.

3. Build a Payroll Tax Control Calendar

A useful calendar tracks more than statutory due dates. It also includes internal approval cutoffs, confirmation reviews and escalation checkpoints.

Keep tax deposits and tax returns as separate calendar items. Evidence that a return was filed does not prove that a payment was completed, and a bank debit does not prove that a return was accepted.

Use the following template for each employer and applicable jurisdiction. The timing below describes an internal workflow, not a tax deadline schedule. Have the payroll tax professional populate and maintain actual due dates.

Use statuses such as not started, submitted, accepted, rejected, under investigation and closed. Avoid a single “done” checkbox that hides whether an item was merely sent or actually completed.

4. Reconcile Payroll, Cash and Tax Liabilities

Payroll reconciliation connects staff costs on the P&L with cash movements and obligations on the balance sheet. It should cover provider and administrative payroll without confusing gross wages, net pay and employer taxes.

At month-end, perform these checks for each employer:

  1. Match payroll registers to the ledger. Compare gross wages, employer payroll tax expense, employee withholding, deductions and net pay. Identify missing, duplicate or misclassified entries.
  2. Match bank activity to payroll funding. Explain provider withdrawals, direct-deposit funding, tax funding, fees, reversals and adjustments. A provider may combine several components in one withdrawal.
  3. Roll forward tax liabilities. For each account, beginning liability plus current-period obligations, less payments and documented adjustments, should equal the ending liability.
  4. Connect payments to supporting evidence. Match tax-payment records to the correct employer, jurisdiction and tax period. Investigate differences rather than clearing them through miscellaneous expense.
  5. Compare period totals with returns. Before filings are finalized, have the accounting team and payroll tax professional resolve differences between payroll records and the proposed returns.

An ending liability is not automatically an error; it may reflect timing. But it should have a documented explanation and expected settlement date. A zero balance is not automatically proof of accuracy either, especially if entries were posted incorrectly.

IRS Publication 583 supports maintaining complete business records and reconciling bank records. The payroll-specific review above applies those recordkeeping principles to the practice’s close process.

5. Review Confirmations, Not Just Dashboard Statuses

For each payment or filing, review enough detail to establish what happened:

  • Which employer and account were used?
  • Which jurisdiction and tax period does the transaction cover?
  • Does the amount match the reconciled obligation?
  • Is the status scheduled, submitted, accepted, completed, rejected or reversed?
  • Is there a reference number and supporting acknowledgment?

A debit from the practice’s bank account may show that the provider collected funds, not that the tax authority received and applied them. Where available and appropriate, corroborate provider reports with tax-account information through authorized access. Ask the payroll tax professional to interpret unclear account activity.

Keep payroll registers, deposit confirmations, filed returns and related evidence organized by employer and period. The IRS employment-tax recordkeeping guidance identifies supporting records to maintain. Have the CPA determine the applicable retention schedule; do not treat one general retention period as a universal destruction date.

6. Escalate Missing Steps Without Creating Duplicate Payments

When a confirmation is missing or a return is rejected, open an exception immediately. Record the affected entity, tax period, amount if known, evidence available and next action.

Ask the payroll provider for a written explanation and completion evidence. If a deadline may have been missed, a notice has arrived or the facts remain unclear, involve the payroll tax professional promptly rather than waiting indefinitely for routine support.

Before authorizing a second payment, establish whether the original transaction failed, remains pending or was applied incorrectly. Duplicate payments can complicate reconciliation and do not resolve an underlying filing error.

Escalate to the owner when funding is unavailable, the provider cannot establish completion, several entities are affected or a response deadline is approaching. The professional should determine the necessary deposit, filing, correction or agency response. Any provider reimbursement discussion should be tracked separately from resolving the tax account.

If payer receipts are delayed, show payroll funding and tax obligations separately in the cash forecast. An expected reimbursement is not available cash. Bring a projected shortfall to the owner before the provider’s funding cutoff.

7. Track Notices Through Documented Closure

Do not assume a payroll tax notice is either correct or a harmless provider error. Log it, preserve the original and have it reviewed against the underlying records.

The following is a hypothetical notice-response tracking sheet. The cases are illustrative workflow examples, not actual practice results or tax determinations.

In the live sheet, also include receipt date, notice identifier, stated response deadline, professional-confirmed action date, provider case number, last contact, next follow-up and evidence location.

A provider ticket marked “resolved” is not enough by itself. The reviewer should verify the relevant payment, filing or account outcome before closing the practice’s case. Ask the professional whether any penalty relief options are available; do not assume eligibility or delay corrective action while relief is considered.

Make Oversight Part of the Financial Close

The owner’s review can focus on exceptions: missing confirmations, unexplained liability balances, rejected filings, unresolved notices and upcoming funding risks. This creates accountability without requiring the owner to review every payroll calculation.

FAQs

Does using a payroll provider remove the need for owner review?

No. The practice still needs an assigned reviewer to check funding, payment confirmations, filing evidence and notices. Confirm the provider’s contractual responsibilities and have a qualified professional address questions about responsibility for a specific failure.

How do we determine deposit and filing deadlines?

Have a payroll tax professional establish the applicable schedule for each employer, tax type and jurisdiction. Enter those dates separately in the control calendar and revisit them when circumstances change. Do not assume all related entities have the same schedule.

What should we do first when a payroll tax notice arrives?

Preserve the notice, log its response deadline, assign an internal owner and send it promptly to the payroll tax professional and relevant provider. Gather payment and filing evidence. Have the professional determine the response rather than assuming another payment or amended return is necessary.

Can one reconciliation cover all practice locations?

A consolidated overview can help management, but the supporting reconciliation must preserve employer-level obligations and applicable jurisdiction detail. Location cost allocations should not conceal tax liabilities or payments belonging to a particular legal employer.
