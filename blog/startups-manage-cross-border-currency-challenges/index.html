Managing Foreign-Currency Costs in Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

A US private medical practice can face foreign-exchange exposure without operating overseas. A vendor might invoice equipment, replacement parts, or a software subscription in euros, pounds, or another currency while the practice collects payer receipts and patient balances in US dollars.

The owner's decision is practical: How many dollars should the practice reserve, when should it pay, and how will it explain the difference between the original estimate and the final bank debit?

The goal is not to predict currency markets. It is to make genuine vendor obligations visible, compare payment choices on a consistent basis, and keep exchange-rate movements separate from fees and accounting adjustments.

Identify the Actual Currency Obligation

A foreign vendor does not automatically create direct foreign-currency exposure. Start with the contract and invoice—not the vendor's address.

  • Fixed US-dollar price: If the vendor will accept a stated dollar amount in full settlement, the practice generally has a dollar obligation for that purchase. Check quote expiration and repricing terms.
  • Fixed foreign-currency price: If the practice owes a specified amount of another currency, its dollar cost can change until conversion occurs or the dollar payment amount is otherwise fixed.
  • Dollar estimate of a foreign-currency price: A displayed dollar equivalent may be informational rather than binding. Ask which currency and amount actually settle the invoice.

Also identify when conversion happens. A card purchase, bank transfer, and payment funded from an existing foreign-currency balance may have different conversion dates and charges. The invoice date is not necessarily the date the dollar cost becomes fixed.

For occasional foreign-currency purchases, a payment worksheet may be enough. Recurring or larger obligations warrant a register tied to the practice's cash forecast.

Build a Foreign-Currency Exposure Register

Keep one row per obligation or scheduled installment. Separate approved commitments from tentative purchases so the owner can distinguish money already promised from spending that remains optional.

Obligation Entity and cost owner Status Foreign amount Budget rate Budget dollars before fees Payment requirement
Equipment component Practice A; North location Approved order; invoice pending EUR 10,000 $1.10/EUR $11,000 Vendor must receive funds in week 6
Annual software renewal Practice A; shared administration Renewal under review GBP 2,000 $1.30/GBP $2,600 Due in week 9 if renewed
Replacement parts Practice B; South location Invoiced EUR 3,000 $1.10/EUR $3,300 Vendor must receive funds in week 4

Add these working fields to the register:

  • Quote expiration, cancellation terms, and invoice reference.
  • Expected conversion date and required vendor receipt date.
  • Estimated fees, fee currency, and who bears intermediary charges.
  • Current payment quote and stressed dollar estimate.
  • Paying bank account, approver, and person responsible for reconciliation.
  • Final dollars debited, foreign amount delivered, and settlement evidence.

Keep the original currency visible. A single dollar total can conceal which obligations change together when a currency moves. Do not add euro and pound amounts into one foreign-currency total.

For multi-entity practices, identify the contracting entity and paying account before payment. If another entity pays, ask the CPA how to record the intercompany balance. For shared costs, use a documented allocation method rather than charging whichever location has the most cash.

Compare a Dollar Quote With a Foreign-Currency Quote

Ask the vendor for comparable terms in both currencies when available. Confirm that the quotes cover the same goods or services, payment schedule, delivery terms, and cancellation rights.

Illustrative example: A vendor offers the same equipment component for either $11,600 in a fixed, all-in dollar payment or EUR 10,000. Assume the foreign-currency payment has a separate $50 fee and no other charges. The assumed rates below represent the conversion rate actually available to the practice, including any rate markup.

Assumed conversion rate Dollars to purchase EUR 10,000 Separate payment fee Total dollar outflow Compared with $11,600 fixed quote
$1.05/EUR $10,500 $50 $10,550 $1,050 less
$1.10/EUR $11,000 $50 $11,050 $550 less
$1.20/EUR $12,000 $50 $12,050 $450 more

The foreign-currency quote is not inherently cheaper. It transfers uncertainty about the dollar cost to the practice. A fixed-dollar quote may be worth accepting when cash headroom is tight or the owner values certainty more than a possible saving.

Use this comparison:

Total dollar outflow = foreign amount × executable conversion rate + separate charges paid by the practice.

Do not add a rate markup twice. If it is already embedded in the quoted conversion rate, it is already included in the calculated dollar cost. If fees are deducted from the amount delivered, ask how much must be sent for the vendor to receive the full invoice amount.

Compare quotes obtained close together in time. A rate from yesterday and a payment quote from today do not establish which provider is cheaper.

Trace the Obligation From Commitment to Settlement

A useful payment file distinguishes the purchase decision, invoice recognition, conversion, and final settlement. These can occur on different days—and sometimes in different accounting periods.

1. At commitment: reserve cash without assuming an accounting entry

Record the approved foreign amount and expected payment date in the register. Use a documented budget rate and a separate allowance for known charges.

An approved order belongs in cash planning even if it is not yet a recognized payable. Ask the CPA whether the contract, deposit, or delivery creates an accounting entry. Do not treat every purchase order as an expense.

2. At invoicing or receipt: establish the accounting basis

Send the invoice and evidence of what was received to the bookkeeping team. Equipment, inventory, prepaid services, and current-period expenses may require different treatment.

For accrual books, ask the CPA which recognition date and exchange rate apply. For cash-basis reporting, do not copy accrual entries without review. Cash planning still needs to show the obligation before payment, regardless of the accounting basis.

3. At period-end: review unpaid balances

An unpaid foreign-currency payable may require remeasurement for accrual financial statements. A change in its dollar carrying amount is not a payment-provider fee, and it does not mean cash has already left the bank.

4. At settlement: reconcile the complete transaction

Match the invoice to the conversion confirmation, bank debit, separate charges, and evidence of the amount delivered. If conversion happens before the vendor receives funds, record both events rather than treating them as one date.

Illustrative accrual-accounting example: Assume a EUR 10,000 payable is properly recognized at $1.10/EUR, remains unpaid at a reporting date with a rate of $1.12/EUR, and is settled at $1.15/EUR. Assume a separate $50 payment fee. This simplified example excludes other adjustments and requires CPA review.

Stage Dollar amount Explanation
Initial payable $11,000 EUR 10,000 × $1.10
Period-end payable $11,200 $200 increase from remeasurement; no settlement cash paid
Dollars used to settle invoice $11,500 Further $300 increase since period-end
Separate payment fee $50 Distinct from the exchange-rate movement
Total cash outflow $11,550 Conversion cost plus separate fee

Across the illustrated transaction, the payable's dollar cost increases by $500, with a separate $50 charge. The CPA should confirm the exchange-gain-or-loss treatment and fee classification.

A budget variance is not automatically the accounting exchange loss. If the original planning rate differed from the recognition rate, those comparisons would start from different dollar amounts.

Schedule Payments Around Practice Cash Needs

Place each obligation in a rolling cash forecast alongside payer receipts, expected patient collections, provider and administrative payroll, rent, and other committed spending.

Use both a base estimate and a less favorable exchange-rate assumption. These are planning scenarios, not predictions. The owner needs to know whether the practice can absorb a higher dollar cost without disrupting payroll or other essential obligations.

For each payment:

  1. Work backward from vendor receipt. Ask the provider about cutoffs, processing time, holidays, and possible delays.
  2. Set an internal approval date. Allow time to verify instructions and resolve missing information.
  3. Check cash headroom by entity. A consolidated cash balance does not show whether the contracting entity has enough available cash.
  4. Refresh the quote before approval. Confirm total dollars required and the foreign amount the vendor will receive.
  5. Update the forecast after execution. Replace the estimate with the actual debit and leave unresolved fees or shortfalls visible.

Paying early may fix the conversion cost sooner, but it also uses cash sooner and can increase exposure to vendor nonperformance. Paying later preserves liquidity but leaves the dollar cost uncertain longer. Make that tradeoff deliberately rather than waiting for a hoped-for favorable exchange rate.

Questions to Ask Banks and Advisers

You do not need to begin with specialized currency products. First compare straightforward payment methods and contract terms.

Question Why it matters
What exact dollar amount leaves our account, and what amount reaches the vendor? Reveals whether the quote fully funds the obligation
Is the conversion rate fixed now, at processing, or at settlement? Establishes when uncertainty ends
Which charges are included, separate, or potentially deducted in transit? Helps prevent fee surprises and vendor shortfalls
When does the quote expire? Prevents approval based on an unavailable price
What happens if the payment is delayed, rejected, canceled, or refunded? Identifies possible extra charges and reconversion exposure
Can this be paid directly from the correct entity's existing account? Avoids unnecessary account and intercompany complexity
If foreign currency is held before payment, what costs, restrictions, and reconciliation work apply? Tests whether added complexity serves a real need

Ask the CPA how each option affects the books and reporting. Do not open foreign-currency accounts merely because a vendor is overseas. Holding currency can shift the exposure rather than eliminate it, especially if the purchase is canceled.

Close the Loop Each Month

Review open commitments, unpaid invoices, payments in transit, and settled items. Explain differences using four categories: exchange-rate movement, provider charges, timing differences, and purchase changes. A larger invoice caused by an added component is not an FX loss.

Keep quotes, invoices, approvals, payment confirmations, and bank records together. IRS Publication 583 supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation; it is not a substitute for transaction-specific accounting advice.

Use vendor and entity information for this workflow; patient-identifiable information is generally unnecessary for an FX exposure register.

FAQs

Does every overseas vendor create foreign-currency exposure?

No. A binding fixed-dollar invoice generally creates a dollar obligation for that purchase. Check whether the dollar amount is final, when the quote expires, and whether the vendor can reprice it before payment.

Should a medical practice pay early when the exchange rate looks favorable?

Not solely on a market prediction. Compare the certainty of converting now with the cash needed for payroll and other commitments, the vendor's terms, and the consequences of cancellation. Use an approved payment schedule rather than informal currency speculation.

Is the difference between the budget and bank debit an exchange loss?

Not necessarily. The difference may include an exchange-rate movement, separate fees, a different payment date, or a changed purchase amount. Accounting exchange gains and losses also depend on the recognized balance and reporting basis. Reconcile those components before the CPA determines treatment.

Does an occasional foreign-currency invoice require a multi-currency account?

Not automatically. Compare a direct payment from the existing business account with other available methods using total dollar cost, timing, vendor receipt amount, and administrative effort. An additional account should solve a documented recurring need, not add complexity without a clear benefit.
