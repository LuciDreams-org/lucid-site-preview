Break-Even Planning for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A full schedule does not necessarily mean a profitable medical practice. Contractual adjustments, patient balances, staffing commitments and service-specific costs all affect how much completed visits contribute toward overhead.

Break-even planning helps owners and managers answer a practical question: How many completed visits, at our expected payer and service mix, will cover the costs of operating the practice?

That answer can inform a hiring decision, a new location, changes to session availability or a review of recurring expenses. It must also pass a capacity test: a target that exceeds available provider time is not a workable operating plan.

The basic formula is:

Break-even completed visits = Monthly fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per completed visit

Contribution margin is expected net revenue minus the variable costs associated with delivering those visits. Separately, a cash forecast shows whether receipts will arrive in time to cover payroll and other payments.

1. Define What You Want the Model to Cover

Start with a specific decision and a consistent planning period. For example, you might estimate monthly operating break-even for an existing location or determine the additional volume needed to support a new provider.

Write down the model’s scope before collecting numbers:

  • Operating unit: The whole practice, one location or a particular entity.
  • Time period: A representative month, with adjustments for holidays, provider leave and seasonality.
  • Included costs: Provider and administrative compensation, occupancy, supplies and other operating expenses.
  • Owner compensation: Whether the model includes compensation for the owner’s clinical and management work.
  • Profit objective: Whether the goal is simply covering costs or also generating a planned surplus.

Owner distributions are not a substitute for identifying the cost of the owner’s work. A practice can appear to break even while providing no economic compensation for substantial owner labor. Make that distinction explicit with your finance team.

Also define whether you are measuring operating break-even or a broader accounting result that includes interest and other expenses. Loan principal, equipment purchases and owner distributions belong in a separate cash plan rather than being treated automatically as operating expenses.

2. Separate Fixed, Variable and Step Costs

Costs should be classified according to how they behave within the volume range you are evaluating—not simply by their account names.

Cost type Medical-practice examples Modeling approach
Fixed within the planning range Rent, base administrative payroll, insurance, recurring software fees Include the monthly amount even if completed visits decline
Variable Visit-specific supplies, outside services tied to particular procedures, compensation that genuinely varies with production Estimate the cost associated with each visit or service category
Mixed Compensation with a base plus a production component; contracts with minimum charges and usage fees Separate the fixed and variable portions
Step cost Another front-desk employee, an additional provider session, expanded leased space Add the cost when the relevant capacity threshold is crossed

Provider and administrative payroll deserve particular attention. Salaried staff do not become variable costs merely because they support patient visits. Conversely, production-based compensation may vary with revenue or activity, depending on the agreement.

Include applicable employer payroll costs and benefits in the appropriate category. Avoid counting compensation once in fixed payroll and again in per-visit variable costs.

A break-even estimate is valid only within its assumed operating range. If reaching the target requires another employee or additional paid sessions, add those costs and recalculate.

3. Estimate Net Revenue per Completed Visit

Use expected net revenue, not gross charges. The amount charged for a visit may differ substantially from what the practice expects to receive from the payer and patient combined.

Build the estimate from sufficiently mature service-period data, using summarized billing reports alongside the financial records. Consider contractual adjustments, expected collection shortfalls, refunds and other adjustments relevant to the practice.

Keep the numerator and denominator aligned:

Expected net revenue per completed visit = Expected eventual net collections attributable to a visit cohort ÷ Completed visits in that cohort

Do not divide this month’s bank deposits by this month’s visits and assume the result measures visit economics. Deposits may include payments for older services, while recent visits may still be awaiting adjudication or patient payment.

Useful segmentation may include payer group, service category, provider or location. Choose categories that materially change revenue, variable costs or required appointment time. Excessive detail can make the model difficult to maintain without improving the decision.

If the practice has significant revenue not linked to visits, model it separately rather than forcing it into an unreliable per-visit average. Document any amount assumed to contribute toward fixed costs.

4. Calculate Contribution Margin and Break-Even Visits

The following is an illustrative example, not a medical-practice benchmark.

Assume a practice has:

Monthly planning input Illustrative amount
Fixed operating costs, including defined provider/admin payroll $90,000
Expected net revenue per completed visit $150
Variable cost per completed visit $30
Contribution margin per completed visit $120

The calculations are:

Contribution margin = $150 − $30 = $120 per completed visit

Break-even visits = $90,000 ÷ $120 = 750 completed visits per month

At that assumed mix, 750 visits generate $112,500 in expected net revenue. After $22,500 in variable costs, $90,000 remains to cover fixed costs.

For a revenue-based view:

Contribution margin ratio = $120 ÷ $150 = 80%

Break-even net revenue = $90,000 ÷ 80% = $112,500

Round a fractional visit target up. If contribution margin is zero or negative, adding visits at that same economics will not cover fixed costs. The underlying revenue and cost assumptions need attention first.

Additional visits above break-even contribute toward profit only while the assumed revenue, variable costs and fixed-cost capacity remain valid. They are not automatically “pure profit.”

5. Weight the Model for Payer and Service Mix

A single average can hide important differences. Two visits may have different expected payments, supply requirements and provider time.

The table below expands the same illustrative example. The categories are hypothetical planning groups, not reimbursement estimates.

Payer/service group Share of completed visits Expected net revenue per visit Variable cost per visit Contribution margin Weighted contribution
Group A 60% $140 $25 $115 $69.00
Group B 30% $180 $45 $135 $40.50
Group C 10% $120 $15 $105 $10.50
Weighted result 100% $150 $30 $120 $120.00

Multiply each group’s contribution margin by its share of completed visits, then add the results. Here, the weighted contribution margin remains $120, producing the same 750-visit break-even estimate.

Use visit shares, not revenue shares, when weighting contribution margin per visit. If you instead model contribution margin ratios by revenue category, use the corresponding revenue weights.

Recalculate when the mix changes. Higher revenue per visit does not necessarily mean higher contribution margin, and higher contribution per visit may still require substantially more appointment time.

Use this analysis to assess financial feasibility—not to direct clinical care. Scheduling, service availability and patient access decisions need operational and clinical judgment alongside the financial model.

6. Test the Target Against Real Capacity

A mathematically correct target can still be operationally impossible. Compare required completed visits with capacity after allowing for appointment lengths, provider availability, room constraints and unfilled or missed appointments.

Continuing the illustrative example, assume the following:

Capacity input Illustrative assumption
Providers available 2
Clinic days per provider per month 20
Appointment slots per provider per day 24
Total available slots 960
Expected completed visits as a share of available slots 85%
Expected completed-visit capacity 816
Break-even completed visits 750
Visits above break-even at expected capacity 66

These assumptions are invented for demonstration and are not recommended scheduling levels.

The 750-visit target fits within expected capacity, but the margin is limited. Provider leave, a shift toward longer visits or lower appointment completion could eliminate it.

Before adding fixed costs, test a downside case. In another illustrative scenario, fixed costs rise to $102,000 and contribution margin falls to $110. Break-even becomes approximately 928 completed visits, rounded up—more than the assumed capacity of 816.

That gap calls for a revised plan, not simply a higher visit target. Options might include phasing in a hire, changing the expansion timetable, reducing avoidable overhead or addressing collection problems. Any capacity expansion must include its associated costs.

7. Keep Cash Timing Separate from Profitability

Break-even analysis measures operating economics. It does not establish that the bank balance will cover the next payroll.

Payer receipts may arrive after services are delivered. Patient balances may remain outstanding longer than expected. Meanwhile, payroll, rent and supply payments follow their own schedules.

Maintain a separate cash forecast that includes:

  • Opening cash by entity or business account.
  • Expected payer and patient receipts, using realistic timing assumptions.
  • Provider and administrative payroll payments.
  • Rent, vendor payments and other scheduled obligations.
  • Debt payments, equipment purchases, taxes and planned owner distributions.
  • Documented transfers between entities, where applicable.

Do not count an outstanding patient balance as both available cash and a future receipt. Likewise, an equipment purchase can reduce cash even when its accounting expense is recognized over time.

Complete records and reconciled accounts make both models more dependable. IRS Publication 583 discusses business recordkeeping, separate business accounts and reconciling bank records.

8. Use Location and Entity Views Without Double Counting

For a multi-location practice, calculate each location’s direct contribution before allocating shared overhead. Then apply a documented allocation method for central administration and other shared expenses.

Reporting view Decision it supports
Location revenue less variable and direct fixed costs Whether the location covers its directly attributable operating costs
Location result after allocated shared costs How the location contributes after a defined share of central overhead
Consolidated practice result Whether the overall organization covers total costs without duplicate internal charges
Entity-level cash forecast Whether each entity has funds available for its obligations

An allocation does not necessarily represent a cost that disappears if a location closes. Distinguish genuinely avoidable costs from shared costs that would remain elsewhere.

Keep allocation methods consistent and explain changes. A location’s apparent performance should not improve merely because overhead was moved to another location.

9. Turn the Model into a Monthly Decision Process

Use a spreadsheet with separate areas for assumptions, cost classifications, payer/service mix, capacity and scenarios. Assign an owner to each input and record the period it represents.

At each monthly review:

  1. Reconcile the books and investigate unusual or misclassified expenses.
  2. Update visit economics using mature data, rather than treating a temporary receipt delay as a permanent revenue change.
  3. Compare planned and actual mix, completed visits and staffing costs.
  4. Refresh capacity assumptions for leave, recruitment and scheduling changes.
  5. Review base and downside cases, then identify the decisions each would require.
  6. Check the cash forecast separately before committing to new spending.

Use aggregated financial and operational data wherever possible. Avoid placing patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. HHS guidance on the minimum necessary standard provides context for limiting information use where that standard applies; the appropriate workflow depends on the circumstances.

Conclusion

A useful break-even plan connects expected net revenue, visit-related costs, staffing commitments and realistic capacity. It tells an owner whether the current operating structure can support itself—and whether a proposed change remains feasible under less favorable conditions.

Build the model around completed visits and expected collections attributable to those services. Test payer and service mix, include step costs, and keep cash timing separate. The result is a decision tool, not just a target on a dashboard.

FAQs

Should a medical practice use charges or collections to calculate break-even?

Use expected net revenue after relevant adjustments and expected collection shortfalls, not gross charges. For a completed-visit model, estimate eventual collections attributable to those visits. Cash received this month may relate to earlier services, so use actual receipt timing in the separate cash forecast.

Is provider payroll a fixed or variable cost?

It depends on the compensation arrangement and planning period. Base salaries generally remain fixed within the modeled range, while production-based compensation may vary. Separate mixed arrangements into their components, include associated employer costs and avoid counting the same compensation twice.

What if our break-even target exceeds appointment capacity?

Recheck the assumptions, including appointment duration, expected completion, staffing and payer/service mix. If the gap remains, the operating plan needs to change. Model the costs of any capacity expansion before assuming more visits will solve the problem.

Does breaking even mean the practice has enough cash?

No. A practice can cover its modeled expenses while waiting for payer receipts or patient payments. Debt principal, equipment purchases and distributions can also consume cash without appearing as operating expenses in the same way. Use a separate cash forecast to evaluate payment timing and available funds.
