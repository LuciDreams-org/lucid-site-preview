Recurring Revenue Metrics for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can receive deposits every week without having subscription revenue. Insurance payments and patient collections may be repeat sources of income, but their amounts and timing depend on services delivered, payer adjudication, patient responsibility and collection activity.

That distinction matters when an owner asks: How much revenue can we rely on when planning payroll, staffing or a location expansion? Calling all regular receipts monthly recurring revenue can make an uncertain cash forecast look more dependable than it is.

For most insurance-based practices, the useful starting point is a forecast of services, expected collections and payment timing—not SaaS-style monthly recurring revenue (MRR) or annual recurring revenue (ARR). Where a practice already has lawful membership arrangements, recurring-fee metrics can supplement that forecast. They still do not replace accounting revenue or available cash.

Why Ordinary Practice Revenue Is Not MRR

MRR is a management measure that expresses active recurring subscription fees as a monthly amount. ARR generally annualizes that amount. Neither is a substitute for a financial statement or a cash forecast.

An established patient relationship does not, by itself, create a fixed recurring fee. Likewise, a payer deposit received this month may relate to services performed in an earlier period.

Revenue or receipt type Why it is not automatically MRR More useful practice measure
Insurance payments for visits and procedures Payments depend on services, allowed amounts, adjudication and timing Expected collections by service period and payer
Patient payments toward visit balances Collections can be delayed, partial or related to prior periods Patient receivables, aging and collection timing
Installments on an existing patient balance Scheduled payments settle an existing balance; they do not necessarily represent new revenue Remaining receivable and expected cash receipts
One-time administrative or other fees The fee does not recur under an ongoing arrangement Separately classified fee revenue
Fees under an existing lawful membership arrangement These may support recurring-fee reporting, subject to the arrangement's terms Normalized recurring fees, recognized revenue and cash collected

A practice with no membership revenue does not need to manufacture an MRR number. It needs a collection forecast that distinguishes current activity from collections of older balances.

This article addresses financial measurement for existing arrangements. It does not recommend that practices offer memberships or address their legal permissibility, contract design or pricing.

Separate Four Numbers Before Building a Dashboard

Even when recurring fees exist, owners should keep four measures distinct.

1. Normalized monthly recurring fees

This is a management measure of the active recurring-fee base, expressed monthly. To avoid confusion with accounting revenue, a practice can label it monthly recurring fee run rate rather than simply “revenue.”

For an annual arrangement, normalization spreads the annual recurring fee across its coverage months for this metric. That calculation does not determine the accounting treatment.

2. Recurring fees billed

This is the amount invoiced or charged during the reporting period. Annual renewal timing can make billing uneven even when the active fee base is stable.

Keep one-time charges separate. Identify credits, discounts and billing corrections so that a billing increase is not mistaken for growth in recurring arrangements.

3. Recognized revenue

Revenue on the financial statements follows the practice's accounting basis and the substance of the arrangement. Under accrual accounting, receiving an advance payment does not necessarily mean the entire amount is earned immediately.

Have the accounting team document how fees are recognized, including advance payments, refunds and any distinct obligations. Do not assume that dividing an annual fee by twelve is always the correct accounting policy.

4. Collected cash

Cash collected is what has actually been received. It may differ from billed fees because of unpaid charges, payment failures, refunds or settlement timing.

Processor deposits also may be net of fees. A bank deposit therefore should not automatically be recorded as the gross amount of revenue.

Calculating MRR and ARR for Existing Membership Arrangements

For internal reporting, a practice can calculate:

Monthly recurring fee run rate = Sum of each included arrangement's recurring fee ÷ its coverage months

Annualized recurring fee run rate = Monthly recurring fee run rate × 12

Use consistent inclusion rules. Define when an arrangement becomes active, when a cancellation takes effect, how pauses are handled and when delinquent arrangements are excluded or separately flagged.

An illustrative example: annual billing is not monthly revenue

Illustrative example only—not a pricing recommendation: A practice has 80 active monthly arrangements at $60 per month and 24 active annual arrangements at $600 per year.

Component Monthly recurring fee run rate
Monthly arrangements: 80 × $60 $4,800
Annual arrangements: 24 × $600 ÷ 12 $1,200
Total monthly recurring fee run rate $6,000
Annualized recurring fee run rate: $6,000 × 12 $72,000

The $72,000 figure is an annualized snapshot. It is not proof of future collections, a guaranteed annual contract value or a forecast that accounts for cancellations and renewals.

Now assume all annual arrangements renew and are paid at the start of the same month. Of the monthly fees billed, $300 remains unpaid. For this illustration, assume the accounting team has determined that the annual fees are earned evenly over twelve months, with no refunds or other adjustments.

Measure for the month Monthly arrangements Annual arrangements Total
Fees billed $4,800 $14,400 $19,200
Accrual revenue recognized $4,800 $1,200 $6,000
Cash collected before processing fees $4,500 $14,400 $18,900

Under those assumptions, the unpaid $300 remains a receivable. The unearned portion of the annual collections remains deferred revenue. Neither changes the active monthly fee run rate automatically.

The owner should not treat the large renewal-month deposit as a permanent increase in monthly spending capacity. It includes advance cash associated with future obligations.

Explain Changes in the Recurring-Fee Base

A monthly total becomes more useful when the practice can explain its movement.

Ending recurring fee run rate = Beginning run rate + New arrangements + Increases − Reductions − Cancellations

Each change should reflect its effective date and the reporting policy. Do not count an arrangement once when enrolled and again when its first payment settles.

Change category What to capture What the owner can learn
New arrangements Monthly-equivalent fees becoming active Whether additions explain the increase
Changes to existing arrangements Incremental increases or reductions Whether the fee base changed without a change in member count
Cancellations Monthly-equivalent fees removed How much of the opening base ended
Paused or delinquent arrangements Status, amount and treatment under the reporting policy Whether reported fees may overstate collectible amounts
Corrections Duplicate records, wrong terms or classification errors Whether apparent growth was actually a data repair

If reporting a cancellation rate, define the denominator. A rate based on the number of arrangements answers a different question from one based on their recurring fees. Do not interpret membership cancellation metrics as measures of clinical outcomes or patient retention across the whole practice.

Build a Monthly Close That Connects Metrics to the Books

A recurring-fee dashboard should be reproducible, not a separate spreadsheet that no one can reconcile.

Step 1: Keep revenue streams separate

Use separate reporting categories for insurance-based services, patient service balances, recurring membership fees and one-time fees. Avoid counting the same activity in more than one category.

For insurance-based revenue, distinguish service activity from receipts. A strong collection month may reflect older receivables rather than increased current production.

Step 2: Maintain an arrangement schedule

For existing memberships, maintain a controlled schedule with the information needed for financial reporting: an internal identifier, responsible entity, location, effective dates, coverage period, recurring fee, billing frequency and status.

Use aggregate or non-identifying information wherever practical. Patient-identifiable details should not be placed in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. Where financial work involves protected health information, assess the applicable responsibilities and appropriate workflow; the HHS overview of covered entities and business associates provides relevant background.

Step 3: Reconcile billing, payments and deposits

Tie billed fees to the billing records, collections to payment reports and deposits to bank activity. Explain processing fees, refunds, payment failures and settlement timing instead of forcing the numbers to match.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those fundamentals also make recurring-fee reports more dependable.

Step 4: Reconcile to the general ledger

Under accrual reporting, reconcile relevant receivables and deferred revenue as well as recognized revenue. Under cash-basis reporting, label the reports accordingly; do not present a management run-rate measure as cash-basis revenue.

Ask the accounting team to explain differences among the arrangement schedule, invoices, ledger and bank. An unresolved difference should be visible, not buried in a dashboard adjustment.

Step 5: Review exceptions before publishing

Check expired terms, duplicate arrangements, missing cancellations, failed payments, unusual credits and annual fees incorrectly treated as monthly charges. Record the reporting date and any policy changes so comparisons remain meaningful.

Use the Right Measure for Each Owner Decision

Recurring fees are one input to planning—not a reason to ignore payer receipts, patient balances or operating costs.

Owner decision Primary financial view Role of recurring-fee metrics
Can we cover the next payroll? Near-term cash forecast, available cash and payment obligations Helps estimate one portion of collections
Can we add a provider or administrator? Expected revenue, compensation, overhead and cash needs Shows whether an existing fee base is stable, not whether the hire pays for itself
Is a location financially sustainable? Location P&L with documented shared-cost allocations Identifies the location's recurring-fee contribution
Can owners take a distribution? Entity-level cash needs, obligations and accountant review Does not establish distributable cash
Are annual renewals masking a cash shortfall? Renewal calendar and forward cash forecast Separates advance collections from ongoing monthly inflows

For a multi-location practice, assign fees consistently to the appropriate location. For a multi-entity group, record revenue in the entity responsible under the arrangement. Transfers between entities are not new external revenue, and consolidated reporting should avoid double-counting them.

Keep the fee-base analysis alongside a P&L. Stable recurring fees do not establish profitability after provider payroll, administrative payroll, occupancy and shared costs.

A Practical Reporting Package

A useful monthly owner packet can stay focused:

  • Practice revenue summary: insurance-based services, patient service revenue and membership revenue, with the accounting basis stated.
  • Recurring-fee bridge: opening balance, changes and closing monthly-equivalent fees.
  • Billing-to-cash reconciliation: charges, collections, refunds, fees and unresolved differences.
  • Forward cash forecast: expected payer receipts, patient collections, membership receipts and operating payments, including annual renewal timing.

FAQs

Can an insurance-based medical practice report MRR?

Regular payer deposits do not make insurance revenue MRR. Forecast those collections using service activity, receivables, payer behavior and timing. If the practice also has lawful recurring membership arrangements, report that fee stream separately.

Is ARR the same as annual revenue?

No. ARR typically annualizes the current recurring-fee base. Annual accounting revenue reflects the reporting period and accounting basis. Future cancellations, renewals, fee changes and collection problems can make actual results different from the annualized run rate.

Should an annual membership payment be counted entirely in the month received?

It belongs in that month's cash receipts when received. Its treatment as accounting revenue depends on the accounting basis and arrangement. Under accrual accounting, some or all of an advance payment may initially be deferred. Ask the accounting team to document the treatment.

Should failed payments immediately reduce recurring fees?

Track the failed collection immediately, but apply a documented policy to the run-rate metric. A temporary payment failure and an ended arrangement are different events. Show delinquent amounts separately so owners can see collection risk even before an arrangement is excluded.

What if the practice has no membership arrangements?

Skip MRR and ARR. Focus on expected collections, receivable aging, revenue by service period, payroll commitments and location or entity profitability. Those measures address the practice's actual financial decisions without implying subscription-like certainty.
