Green-Tech Investment Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 10 min read

A solar installation at your practice and an investment in a solar developer may share an environmental theme, but they expose your money to different risks. One changes your practice’s operating costs and financing obligations. The other depends on an outside business or investment vehicle generating a return and eventually returning your capital.

Start by identifying what you are funding, how cash returns to you, and what happens if the proposal underperforms. An environmental claim is not evidence of repayment capacity, and a technically successful project is not necessarily a sound financial investment.

For owners of staffed US private medical practices, the decision must also protect provider and administrative payroll, routine operating expenses and the cash needed while payer receipts and patient balances are collected. This guide provides a due-diligence framework for evaluating green-tech proposals and preparing evidence a capital provider may request.

Separate a Practice Project From an External Investment

Before comparing projected returns, classify the proposal. Equipment ownership, an equipment lease, a power-purchase agreement and an outside equity investment do not create the same rights or obligations.

Decision area On-site practice capital project External green-tech investment
What you fund Equipment or improvements serving a practice location An interest in an outside company, fund or project entity
Source of financial benefit Lower operating outflows or documented project receipts Distributions, interest, sale proceeds or another contractual payment
Main exposure Installation, actual performance, utility costs, financing and occupancy Business execution, counterparties, capital structure, valuation and exit conditions
Control Some control over vendor selection and operating arrangements Control depends on investment terms and may be limited
Liquidity Installed equipment may be difficult or costly to sell or relocate Sale, redemption or transfer may be restricted or unavailable
Practice connection Direct effect on location expenses and cash requirements Capital may be unavailable to support the practice when needed

A third arrangement deserves separate review: a vendor may own the equipment while your practice commits to buying power or paying a recurring service charge. That is not equivalent to owning an asset. Evaluate payment escalators, minimum commitments, termination rights and responsibility for repairs.

For an external investment, establish whether the proposed investor is the owner personally, the practice entity or another entity. Ask qualified legal and tax advisers to review the structure and authority to invest before committing funds. This article does not recommend any security or investment product.

Turn Investor Concerns Into an Owner’s Due-Diligence Worksheet

Use the following worksheet before paying a deposit or signing a financing commitment. For each row, record the evidence received, its date, who reviewed it and any unresolved condition.

Question Evidence to request Decision it informs
Where does the cash come from? Utility baseline and production assumptions for an on-site project; customer contracts and cash-flow statements for an external investment Whether returns depend on observable activity or unsupported projections
Has the technology worked under comparable conditions? Operating history, independent testing where available, maintenance records and performance limitations Whether the proposal introduces maturity or deployment risk
Who must perform or pay? Installer credentials relevant to the work, warranty terms, service arrangements and counterparty financial information where available Whether a failure by another party could interrupt benefits or repayment
What is the complete capital requirement? Written scope, installation costs, site work, financing fees, contingency and replacement assumptions Whether the quoted price understates the cash commitment
What happens when timing slips? Construction milestones, payment schedule and financing commencement terms Whether payments begin before benefits arrive
Can capital be recovered early? Transfer restrictions, redemption terms, termination charges and equipment removal obligations Whether the commitment fits the owner’s liquidity needs
What supports environmental claims? Baseline, measurement method, reporting period and ownership of any associated environmental attributes Whether claims are measurable and attributable to the practice
What depends on incentives or a future exit? Separately identified assumptions and adviser verification Whether the project remains acceptable without uncertain proceeds

An unanswered question is not automatically a rejection. It is a reason to pause, price the uncertainty or make approval conditional on receiving evidence.

Test the Cash Flows Before the Headline Return

For an on-site project, establish a defensible baseline

Collect utility bills over a period that captures seasonal variation. Identify consumption, demand-related charges where applicable, fixed charges and tariff terms. A reduction in energy consumption does not necessarily reduce every component of a utility bill.

Ask the proposal’s preparer to show how equipment output translates into avoided cash payments. Separate energy used at the site from energy exported elsewhere, and identify the applicable assumptions for each. Do not count the same benefit twice.

Build the forecast around the practice’s expected occupancy. An installation with a long useful life may still be a poor fit if the lease ends sooner and transfer rights are unclear. Review landlord consent, roof condition, restoration obligations and who receives the equipment’s benefits if the practice moves.

For an external investment, trace the route from revenue to distributions

Projected customer demand is not the same as cash available to investors. Review operating costs, debt service, working-capital needs, future construction requirements and the order in which different capital providers receive payments.

Ask whether projected distributions depend on refinancing, selling the project or raising another funding round. A forecast that requires new capital should show what happens if that capital arrives late or not at all.

Keep tax and incentive assumptions separate

Do not assume a proposal qualifies for a credit, rebate, deduction or transferable benefit. Have your CPA and other relevant advisers verify the applicable rules, eligibility, timing and documentation for the specific transaction.

Maintain a case without unverified incentives. If the proposal only works when uncertain proceeds arrive on schedule, make that dependency explicit rather than presenting those proceeds as available cash.

Model the Practice’s Downside, Not Just the Project’s Average

Illustrative example—not a savings forecast or financing offer: A single-location practice considers a $120,000 energy project. It would contribute $20,000 and finance $100,000. The proposed model assumes $24,000 in annual avoided utility spending, $4,000 in annual maintenance and related costs, and $18,000 in annual loan payments.

Annual cash item Illustrative base case Illustrative downside case
Avoided utility spending $24,000 $16,000
Maintenance and related cash costs ($4,000) ($5,000)
Loan payments ($18,000) ($18,000)
Net annual cash effect before taxes $2,000 ($7,000)

The $20,000 initial contribution is separate from these annual figures. Neither case includes tax benefits, resale proceeds or every possible project cost. Loan payments are shown as cash outflows, not as a calculation of accounting profit.

The base case leaves little room for error. The owner should ask whether the practice can comfortably fund the downside while meeting payroll and ordinary operating commitments. A simple payback calculation using only installation cost and estimated utility reductions would miss that financing pressure.

Also model a delay before benefits begin, an uninsured repair and a practice relocation. For external investments, test delayed distributions and an inability to sell—not merely a lower estimated investment value.

Set approval conditions before reviewing the final sales presentation. These might include a minimum operating cash reserve, a maximum initial commitment and acceptable contract terms. The appropriate limits depend on the practice’s finances; they are not universal benchmarks.

Check Technology Maturity and Counterparty Strength

A familiar technology can still be deployed poorly. Assess the specific equipment, installation design, site conditions and service arrangements rather than relying on the broad category’s reputation.

Request evidence from comparable installations. Ask who prepared the performance estimate, what assumptions were used and whether an independent technical review is appropriate for the project’s size and complexity.

A warranty is only as useful as its coverage and the party responsible for honoring it. Review exclusions, labor and removal costs, claim procedures and what happens if the installer or manufacturer stops operating. For equipment marketed as backup power, obtain technical confirmation of what it can actually support; do not infer continuity of practice operations from a storage label alone.

For an external investment, examine both the project and the organization managing it. Review available financial statements, existing debt, related-party arrangements, fees and material customer concentrations. If information is unavailable, record the limitation instead of filling the gap with a favorable assumption.

Prepare the Evidence a Capital Provider May Request

Requirements vary by lender, lessor, investor and transaction. Ask for the provider’s actual checklist rather than treating a generic list as an approval standard.

A practical preparation file can include:

  • Reconciled financial statements and current cash balances.
  • A schedule of existing debt, proposed payments and any guarantees.
  • Project scope, vendor quotes, payment milestones and contingency assumptions.
  • A cash forecast showing installation timing, operating benefits and downside cases.
  • Site-control documents and required consents or approvals, as applicable.
  • Performance support, warranty terms, insurance responsibilities and maintenance arrangements.
  • A schedule separating verified funding sources from conditional incentives or future capital.

The IRS guide to starting and maintaining business records supports complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. These are useful foundations for evaluating a financing proposal, but they do not establish that a lender will approve it.

For a multi-location practice, show the affected location’s economics as well as the entity’s ability to make payments. Identify which entity owns the equipment, signs the contract and pays the debt. If a property entity and practice entity share project costs or benefits, document the arrangement with adviser support rather than assigning all benefits to one and ignoring the other’s obligations.

Verify Environmental Claims Without Confusing Them With Returns

Keep the financial model and the environmental measurement schedule separate. A project may reduce energy use but offer an unattractive financial return; a profitable arrangement may provide less environmental benefit than its marketing suggests.

For each claim, record:

  • The baseline and comparison period.
  • Whether the result is measured or estimated.
  • The calculation method and material assumptions.
  • Who checks the result and how often.
  • Who owns any associated environmental attributes and may make the claim.

Distinguish a contractual performance commitment from a marketing estimate. Neither a “green” label nor an environmental projection substitutes for evidence of cash generation.

Treat Investor-Preference Claims as Dated Evidence

Do not select a practice project because a presentation says investors currently favor solar, storage or another sector. Sector interest does not establish that your location, contract or financing terms are attractive.

Use this guide as a proposal-review framework; a current ranking of 2026 green-tech investor preferences would require separate, dated market evidence. Any market observation used in your decision file should identify its source, publication date, observation period, geography and transaction type.

A completed financing is different from an announced fundraising target. A developer’s equity raise is different from a loan available to a medical practice. Ask a prospective capital provider to confirm current terms directly, and date that confirmation.

Make the Approval Decision Explicit

Conclude the review with a short decision memo: approve, approve subject to named conditions, defer or decline. Summarize the cash commitment, downside exposure, unresolved evidence and person responsible for each follow-up.

FAQs

Is installing solar at my practice the same as investing in a solar company?

No. An on-site installation primarily creates operating, equipment and financing exposure. An outside investment adds exposure to another business’s management, capital structure and distribution or exit terms. Evaluate each separately, even when both involve the same technology.

What should I request before seeking project financing?

Start with the capital provider’s requirements. Prepare reconciled financials, existing debt details, written project costs, a payment schedule and a cash forecast with downside cases. Include support for performance assumptions and identify any conditions that must be met before installation or funding.

Should projected tax incentives make an otherwise weak proposal acceptable?

Do not treat unverified incentives as certain cash. Ask your advisers to verify the specific transaction, then show the timing and conditions separately. Compare the result with a case that excludes those proceeds so the dependency is visible.

How much practice cash should remain available after a commitment?

There is no universal amount. Base the reserve on payroll, fixed costs, debt payments, receipt variability and foreseeable operating needs. Test the combined effect of project underperformance and slower practice collections before committing funds to equipment or an illiquid outside investment.
