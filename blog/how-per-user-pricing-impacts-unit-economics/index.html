Per-User Software Pricing and Medical Practice Unit Economics

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Adding an employee can increase more than payroll. It may also trigger another software license, a higher subscription tier or a new location charge. For a staffed medical practice, understanding those contract terms is part of deciding what a new hire or office will actually cost.

Per-user pricing is a vendor expense driver—not a model for treating patients as subscription customers. The relevant question is how software commitments change when your practice adds providers, administrative staff or locations.

A useful analysis separates three things: what you currently pay for, what each role needs and what the contract requires you to buy next. That distinction helps an owner avoid both understating expansion costs and assuming every new employee creates the same software expense.

Start With the Contract’s Definition of a User

“Per user” is not a complete pricing description. A vendor might charge for named accounts, active accounts, providers, administrators or a minimum block of licenses. Different roles may carry different charges, and some contracts combine seat charges with fixed fees.

Before evaluating a hiring decision, document the terms that determine the invoice.

Use the signed agreement, amendments and current invoices together. An invoice shows what was billed; it may not explain the next renewal obligation or whether an unused license can be reassigned.

Reconcile People, Accounts and Paid Capacity

Build a roster using employee identifiers, roles, locations and work status. Patient information is not needed for this analysis.

Keep these counts separate:

  • Active staff: People currently working in the practice, including part-time staff.
  • Required accounts: Accounts those people need for their assigned duties.
  • Licensed seats: Paid capacity under the agreement, including unused committed seats.
  • Staffed full-time equivalents: Staffing capacity based on a consistently defined full-time schedule.

Investigate differences rather than assuming they are waste. Unused paid capacity might reflect a contract minimum, an upcoming hire or an account awaiting review. Conversely, a staff member without a paid license may legitimately have no role requiring that software.

Do not assume account sharing is an acceptable way to reduce costs. Confirm permitted account use and appropriate access with the vendor and the practice’s responsible personnel.

A Worksheet for Hiring and Location Decisions

Evaluate the proposed staffing plan against the existing commitment before requesting more licenses. The first question is whether available paid capacity can cover the new role under the contract.

The worksheet below uses illustrative staffing counts and hypothetical contract terms, not actual vendor pricing or a practice case study. All scenarios start from the same current practice; they are alternatives, not cumulative changes.

Assume this illustrative agreement has:

  • A minimum commitment of five provider seats and eight administrative seats.
  • Separate recurring rates for provider and administrative seats.
  • A fixed practice fee and a recurring fee for each location.
  • No additional tier or module changes in these scenarios.
  • Permission to assign available seats to new staff in the same applicable role.

For the formulas, use monthly-equivalent amounts:

  • P: Rate per provider seat.
  • A: Rate per administrative seat.
  • F: Fixed practice fee.
  • L: Fee per location.

The half-time administrator and full-time provider use existing paid capacity in this example. Their incremental recurring cost for this contract is zero, but the practice still pays the existing subscription. Zero incremental expense does not mean the software is free.

The second location exceeds both seat minimums and adds a location fee. Its recurring increase is therefore one provider seat, two administrative seats and one location fee.

Actual terms may produce a different result. If the second office belongs to another entity, the vendor might require a separate agreement with its own minimums. Confirm that before applying the existing contract’s spare capacity to the expansion.

Add Timing and One-Time Costs

The worksheet shows recurring cost, not the complete funding requirement. Add separate lines for:

  • One-time setup, training or onboarding charges specified in the proposal.
  • Temporary overlap between outgoing and incoming staff accounts.
  • Additional modules required by the new role or location.
  • The payment due when seats are added.
  • Recurring expense after any introductory period ends.

Keep cash timing distinct from the monthly-equivalent cost. An annual payment can create a substantial cash requirement even when the monthly expense appears manageable. Your bookkeeper or CPA can determine the appropriate accounting treatment; the decision worksheet should show both the recurring run rate and expected payment dates.

Measure the Right Kind of Unit Economics

No single software ratio answers every staffing question. Use different measures for contract efficiency, staffing capacity and affordability.

Software Cost per Staffed FTE

Software cost per staffed FTE = software expense for the period ÷ average staffed FTEs for that period.

This measures expense relative to labor capacity. Define whether the denominator includes providers, owners working in the practice, administrative employees and temporary staff. Apply that definition consistently.

Its main limitation is that vendors often bill by headcount, not hours. Two part-time employees may require two named licenses even if together they represent one FTE. A practice relying on part-time coverage can therefore have a higher cost per FTE without having unnecessary accounts.

Use average FTEs over the reporting period rather than a year-end staffing snapshot. Also distinguish a practice-wide software measure from a ratio covering only one contract.

Software Expense Relative to Net Collections

Software expense relative to net collections = software expense ÷ net collections for the same period.

For this management measure, document what net collections includes—typically payer and patient receipts, reduced by refunds and reversals—and apply the definition consistently. Do not substitute gross charges or include owner contributions and loan proceeds.

This ratio provides affordability context, but it is not a standalone efficiency score. Collections can move because of payment timing, payer mix or patient-balance receipts, while software commitments remain unchanged. A new location may also incur software expense before its collections stabilize.

Review the ratio over a consistent, meaningful period alongside the absolute expense. Do not treat a temporary decline in collections as proof that the software contract is inefficient.

Incremental Software Cost per Hiring Decision

Incremental recurring software cost = proposed recurring contract cost − current recurring contract cost.

This is usually the most useful measure when approving a hire. Calculate it across every affected contract, then add it to the broader staffing budget alongside compensation, benefits, equipment and training.

For a new provider, compare the resulting cost plan with a separately developed collections forecast. For an administrative hire, assess the operational purpose and expected capacity contribution without assuming the employee directly generates a fixed amount of revenue.

A hiring decision should not depend on a blended average that conceals a new contract minimum or tier jump.

Allocate Shared Software Without Distorting Location Results

For multi-location practices, separate the vendor’s billing structure from the internal allocation method.

Directly assign location-specific charges where possible. Allocate shared seat charges using the roles or accounts that drive those charges. Allocate a shared fixed fee using a documented method suited to the practice, such as staffed FTEs when appropriate.

Avoid automatically allocating everything by collections. That can make a higher-collection office appear to consume more software even when its staffing requirements are lower.

Also separate allocation from incremental cost. Moving an existing shared fee onto a new location’s P&L changes reported location profitability, but it does not necessarily increase the practice’s total spending.

For multi-entity owners, confirm which entity holds the contract, pays the invoice and bears the expense. Have the finance team review the treatment of shared costs rather than assuming a vendor invoice settles the accounting question.

Make Contract Review Part of Staffing Approval

A practical workflow puts the review before the hiring commitment:

  1. Practice manager: Identifies required accounts, roles, locations and start dates.
  2. Contract owner: Confirms spare capacity, minimums, tier changes and reduction rights.
  3. Finance team: Calculates incremental expense, payment timing and location/entity allocation.
  4. Practice owner: Reviews the complete hiring or expansion budget.
  5. Account administrator: Provisions authorized access and reviews departing staff accounts promptly, while finance separately checks billing consequences.

Retain the worksheet, vendor confirmation, agreement and invoice support with the financial records. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping explains the importance of complete records supporting business transactions; it does not establish the commercial terms of a software contract.

At renewal, compare paid capacity with expected staffing—not just today’s headcount. Ask whether role-specific licenses, transferable seats or different minimums are available, without assuming the vendor offers them.

Common Questions

Does removing a user immediately lower the bill?

Not necessarily. Deactivating access and reducing the financial commitment are separate actions. Minimums, annual commitments and renewal notice provisions determine when the bill can change.

Should a part-time employee count as half a software seat?

Only if the agreement permits it. FTE measures work capacity; a named-user license may still be required for each person regardless of scheduled hours.

Is a lower software-cost-to-collections ratio always better?

No. Collections timing can change the ratio without any change in contract efficiency. Review required access, staffing mix, absolute expense and incremental costs before drawing a conclusion.

The Decision to Make

Before adding staff or opening a location, identify the next contractual cost step. Determine whether the proposed roles fit existing paid capacity, trigger additional licenses or require a separate commitment. Then place the recurring expense and payment schedule into the full staffing budget. That is how per-user pricing becomes a useful practice-finance decision rather than just another subscription line.
