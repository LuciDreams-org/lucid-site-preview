Equity Education for Medical Practice Owners

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Owning 25% of a medical practice does not, by itself, tell you how much cash you will receive, which decisions you can influence or what you will be paid when you leave. Those answers depend on the entity, its governing documents, compensation arrangements and transaction terms.

For existing and prospective owners of staffed private medical practices, equity education starts with separating ownership, control, compensation and cash distributions. These concepts can be related without being interchangeable.

Before discussing a buy-in, admitting another owner or planning a departure, assemble a financial and ownership record package. Then have healthcare counsel and tax advisers explain how the documents apply to your situation. This article provides a framework for that conversation—not a determination of ownership eligibility, legal rights or tax treatment.

Understand What Your Ownership Percentage Measures

An ownership percentage describes an interest in a particular entity using a particular denominator. Ask both: “A percentage of what?” and “As of when?”

A practice may have one operating entity, or separate entities for different operations, real estate or shared services. An interest in one entity does not establish ownership in the others. Similarly, a location-level profit-and-loss statement is a management report, not proof that the location is a separate entity or separately owned.

Concept What it describes What to verify
Ownership percentage Your recorded interest in a specified entity Units or shares held, total outstanding interests and effective date
Voting rights Your role in approving decisions Voting classes, approval thresholds and matters requiring special consent
Economic rights Your entitlement to allocations, distributions or transaction proceeds Applicable provisions, priorities and conditions
Compensation Payment for clinical, administrative or management work Employment or service agreements and compensation policies
Capital contribution Money or property contributed as owner capital Contribution records, approvals and accounting treatment
Owner loan A separate lending arrangement between an owner and the practice Loan documents, balance and repayment terms

Do not assume equal ownership produces equal voting power, equal compensation or equal distributions. Have counsel identify the controlling provisions and explain how the documents fit together.

Separate Practice Earnings From Owner Cash

A profitable practice may still need to retain cash for provider and administrative payroll, rent, equipment or other obligations. Payer receipts and patient-balance collections may arrive at different times from the work that generated them. Accounting profit therefore is not the same as cash available for distribution.

Owners should distinguish four questions:

  1. What did the practice earn? Review the income statement and the accounting method used.
  2. What cash is available? Review bank balances, upcoming obligations and the cash forecast.
  3. Who is entitled to receive it? Review the governing documents and relevant agreements.
  4. Who can approve payment? Confirm the required decision process with counsel.

Compensation for an owner's work also needs separate treatment. A physician-owner's clinical compensation may differ from another owner's because their responsibilities or compensation agreements differ. That difference does not necessarily change either person's ownership percentage.

Likewise, an allocation of taxable income is not necessarily a cash distribution. Ask the tax adviser to explain the treatment applicable to the entity and each owner rather than assuming the ownership schedule answers every tax question.

Build the Ownership Record Package

A useful ownership discussion requires more than a percentage spreadsheet. Assemble the following records before asking advisers to evaluate a transaction or resolve a discrepancy.

Record Why it matters
Current governing documents and amendments Establish the provisions counsel must interpret
Ownership register or capitalization schedule Identifies recorded holders, interests, classes and changes
Signed purchase, subscription and transfer agreements Documents how interests were acquired or transferred
Contribution and owner-loan schedules Separates invested capital from lending arrangements
Distribution history and approvals Shows payments made and the stated basis for them
Employment and management agreements Separates payment for work from ownership economics
Entity-level financial statements and reconciliations Connects ownership discussions to reliable financial records
Buy-sell agreements and valuation materials Supports discussion of entry, departure and payment terms

For multi-location practices, include location-level P&Ls and the method used to allocate shared administrative costs. For multi-entity groups, include intercompany balances and identify which entity holds each bank account, asset and obligation.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports keeping complete business records, separating business and personal accounts, and reconciling bank records. Those practices help make the financial package reliable; they do not establish legal ownership rights.

If the ownership schedule conflicts with signed documents, flag the discrepancy. Do not silently change the books or assume a spreadsheet supersedes an agreement.

A Fictional Ownership Statement: Same Percentage, Different Economics

Illustrative example only: The following statement describes a fictional practice and hypothetical document provisions. It is not a recommended structure or a conclusion that these terms are available for any particular practice. Healthcare counsel and tax advisers would need to evaluate eligibility, enforceability and consequences.

Fictional practice ownership summary

The operating entity has 100 outstanding units. Its hypothetical documents specify the following arrangements:

Item Owner A Owner B Owner C
Units held 50 25 25
Ownership percentage 50% 25% 25%
Recorded cash contribution $100,000 $80,000 $40,000
Ordinary voting rights One vote per unit One vote per unit One vote per unit
Special approval right None specified here Consent required for new equity issuance None specified here
Hypothetical distribution provision Share of residual cash $20,000 priority payment for this period, then share of residual cash Share of residual cash

Assume the practice has approved $120,000 for distribution after reviewing operating cash needs. Under the hypothetical provision, Owner B receives the $20,000 priority payment first. The remaining $100,000 is distributed according to the stated ownership percentages:

Owner Priority payment Share of remaining cash Total distribution
A $0 $50,000 $50,000
B $20,000 $25,000 $45,000
C $0 $25,000 $25,000

Owners B and C each own 25%, but their cash distributions differ. Their approval rights also differ. Their original contributions alone do not explain either result; the hypothetical governing provisions do.

This statement intentionally leaves out compensation, tax allocations, sale proceeds and buyout calculations. None should be inferred from the distribution example. Each requires its own document review.

Capital Contributions and Dilution

A capital contribution is not automatically a purchase of additional ownership. An existing owner might contribute cash without receiving more units, receive newly issued interests, or lend money under a separate agreement. The actual arrangement must be documented and reflected appropriately in the records.

Before moving funds, establish:

  • Which entity will receive them.
  • Whether the payment is capital, a loan or consideration paid to a selling owner.
  • Whether ownership interests or rights will change.
  • Which approvals and documents are required.
  • How the transaction will be recorded and reported.

How dilution changes the denominator

Illustrative example only: An owner holds 25 of 100 outstanding units, or 25%. If the entity issues 25 additional units to someone else and the existing owner receives none, that owner then holds 25 of 125 units, or 20%.

That arithmetic shows percentage dilution. It does not establish the value of the interest, voting consequences or future distributions. Those depend on the transaction and applicable documents.

A transfer of existing units between owners is different from an issuance of new units. A buy-in may send money to the practice, a selling owner or both. Ask advisers to show the before-and-after ownership schedule and trace where the money goes.

Read Buy-In and Buyout Terms Before Discussing Price

A proposed price is only one part of an ownership transaction. The scope of the interest, payment terms and future obligations can materially affect the decision.

For a buy-in, clarify which entity is involved, what interests are being acquired and whether the buyer receives the same rights as existing owners. Determine how existing receivables, cash, debt and liabilities enter the transaction calculation. Do not assume the purchase includes interests in related entities.

For a buyout, review the events that trigger it, the valuation process, payment timing and dispute provisions. A retirement, voluntary departure, death or other event may be addressed differently in the documents. Have counsel explain the actual provisions rather than relying on an informal understanding among owners.

The finance team should also model the practice's ability to make payments while meeting payroll and operating obligations. A contractual amount and an affordable payment schedule are separate questions.

Adviser-Question Checklist

Use this checklist in a joint conversation with healthcare counsel, the CPA and, where appropriate, a valuation adviser.

Topic Questions to resolve
Eligibility and structure May this person own this interest in this entity? Which jurisdiction-specific requirements need review?
Percentage What is the denominator, effective date and treatment of any unissued or contingent interests?
Control Which decisions use ordinary voting, special consent or another approval process?
Economics Can identical percentages receive different distributions or sale proceeds? Which provisions explain the difference?
Contributions Is additional funding required? What happens if an owner does not contribute?
Compensation Which payments compensate work, and which arise from ownership?
Taxes How may allocations, distributions and the proposed transaction affect each party?
Dilution What issuance rights, approval requirements or participation rights need review?
Departure What triggers a buyout, how is value determined and how are payments funded?
Records Do signed documents, the ownership schedule and accounting records agree?

Request a written list of unresolved points, responsible advisers and next actions before signing or transferring funds.

Keep the Records Current After the Decision

Ownership education should continue after a transaction closes. Update the ownership schedule, contribution and loan records, accounting entries and relevant approvals. Keep an effective-date history rather than overwriting prior ownership positions.

At distribution discussions, present the entity-level financial statements, cash needs, applicable distribution provisions and proposed payments separately. This makes it easier to identify whether a disagreement concerns financial performance, available cash or document interpretation.

Ownership analysis generally needs financial summaries, not patient-identifiable detail. Keep payer and patient-balance information aggregated where possible, and avoid placing patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

FAQs

Does owning 25% mean receiving 25% of every distribution?

Not necessarily. Distribution rights may depend on priorities, classes or other provisions in the governing documents. Compensation and owner-loan repayments are also separate from ownership distributions. Ask advisers to reconcile each payment category to its supporting terms.

Is my capital account the same as the value of my ownership interest?

No. A capital account is an accounting or tax record whose meaning depends on the entity and reporting context. It is not, by itself, a market valuation or a promised buyout amount. Have the CPA explain the account and counsel identify the applicable buyout provisions.

What should a prospective owner request before buying in?

Request the governing documents, current ownership schedule, relevant financial statements, contribution and loan records, compensation terms, distribution history and buy-sell provisions. Have healthcare counsel and tax advisers review the package before committing. A percentage and purchase price alone are not enough to understand the interest.

Make the Ownership Discussion Specific

The goal of equity education is to turn “I own part of the practice” into a clear understanding of the entity, rights, obligations and records involved. Start with reliable financials, distinguish ownership from compensation and cash, and ask advisers to explain the governing provisions. That foundation supports a more informed buy-in, ownership change or exit discussion.
