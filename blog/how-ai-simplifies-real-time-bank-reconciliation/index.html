Bank Reconciliation for Private Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice can have a full schedule and still struggle to explain its cash balance. Payer deposits may combine multiple payments, patient card receipts may arrive net of fees, and payroll withdrawals may clear on different dates from the related accounting entries.

Bank reconciliation connects those movements to the books. It verifies that recorded cash agrees with bank activity after documented timing differences and corrections. It does not establish that every billed service has been paid or that every payer adjustment is correct; those questions require separate billing and receivables review.

For owners and managers of staffed private practices, the goal is a repeatable close: staff collect the evidence, bookkeeping prepares the reconciliation, billing resolves receipt questions, and a CPA reviews significant judgments and signs off on the completed close package. AI can assist with comparisons and exception lists, but it should not replace that accountability.

Define What You Are Reconciling

Start with a list of every bank account, its legal owner, its purpose and the person responsible for reconciling it. Include operating, payroll, savings and other accounts that hold practice funds.

Each account needs its own reconciliation to a bank statement. A consolidated cash report is useful for management, but it cannot replace account-level work.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping discusses maintaining complete records, separating business and personal accounts, and reconciling bank records. For a practice, those habits also make it easier to distinguish operating receipts from loans, owner contributions and transfers.

Charges, receipts and deposits are different

Keep these three records distinct:

  • Billing charges: Amounts entered for services. They are not evidence that cash was collected.
  • Receipt records: Payments recorded by billing staff or payment systems, including payer and patient payments.
  • Bank deposits: Funds that actually reached the bank, sometimes grouped into batches or reduced by fees and refunds.

The reconciliation should connect receipt and settlement records to deposits—not compare total billed charges directly with bank deposits. Differences between charges and collections belong in a separate revenue-cycle analysis.

Assign Ownership Before Starting the Close

A workflow breaks down when everyone assumes someone else will investigate an unmatched deposit. Give each part of the close a named owner and backup.

Role Responsibility
Practice manager or administrator Coordinates document delivery, assigns exceptions and follows up on missing evidence
Billing or revenue-cycle staff Explains payer batches, patient receipt totals, refunds and recoupments
Bookkeeper or finance preparer Matches activity, prepares supported corrections and maintains the reconciliation schedule
Owner or authorized manager Reviews unusual payments, transfers and unresolved business decisions
CPA reviewer Reviews significant accounting judgments, proposed corrections and the close package before sign-off

Where staffing allows, separate payment authorization from reconciliation review. If one administrator performs several tasks, arrange an independent owner or finance review rather than treating self-review as sufficient.

Set a recurring monthly close schedule. More frequent checks can help resolve deposit questions while staff remember them, but they do not replace the month-end reconciliation to the statement.

A Practical Bank Reconciliation Workflow

1. Assemble a complete source package

For each account and statement period, gather:

  • The bank statement and accounting cash ledger.
  • The prior reconciliation, including outstanding checks and deposits in transit.
  • Payer receipt summaries and electronic payment references.
  • Patient payment batch totals and merchant settlement reports.
  • Refund, chargeback and processing-fee reports.
  • Payroll funding reports and relevant vendor payment records.
  • Supporting documents for transfers, financing and owner transactions.

Use batch totals and payment references wherever possible. The reconciliation package usually does not need patient names, clinical details or full billing records. If staff must investigate a payment at the patient level, keep that work within an appropriately controlled process and return only the necessary financial explanation to the close file.

2. Confirm the opening balance and statement period

Verify that the opening book balance agrees with the prior completed reconciliation. Review prior outstanding items before matching new activity.

An old uncleared check is not automatically an expense to record again. A prior deposit in transit should either clear in the current statement or remain an exception requiring investigation. If the opening balance is unreliable, resolve the historical gap before declaring the current month reconciled.

Use consistent cutoff dates. A month-end receipt that settles in the next month may be a timing item, but only if the receipt and later settlement can be traced.

3. Match payer deposits to receipt batches

For each payer deposit, compare the bank amount and reference with the related payment or receipt summary. One deposit may cover multiple providers, service dates or billing batches.

Document the relationship when a match is not one-to-one. For example, a reconciliation schedule can show which receipt batches make up a combined deposit. Where a payer withholds or recoups an amount, obtain the supporting payment detail rather than assuming the difference is a bank fee or contractual adjustment.

If the bank shows a deposit but billing staff have not identified it, assign an owner to investigate. Do not force it into patient revenue simply to clear the exception. Any temporary accounting treatment should be documented, reviewed and tracked to resolution.

4. Separate gross card receipts from net settlements

Patient card payments often reach the bank after deductions. Recording the net deposit as the entire receipt can understate collections or leave clearing accounts unexplained.

Illustrative example: A merchant report shows $12,000 of card receipts, $300 of patient refunds and $240 of processing fees. The bank receives $11,460.

Settlement component Illustrative amount
Gross card receipts $12,000
Less patient refunds ($300)
Less processing fees ($240)
Net bank deposit $11,460

The settlement report explains the difference; the bank deposit alone does not. Refunds and fees need separate treatment under the practice's accounting policies.

If gross receipts are already recorded in the books, settlement should clear the related balance rather than create revenue a second time. A clearing account can help connect receipts, deductions and deposits, provided someone reviews its remaining balance each close.

Check the actual processor report: fees, refunds and chargebacks may settle separately rather than within the same deposit.

5. Match payroll, vendor payments and transfers

Payroll may produce separate bank withdrawals for net pay, taxes and other funding obligations. Compare those withdrawals with payroll reports and existing accounting entries. Do not record every bank debit as new payroll expense if the payroll entry has already recognized it.

For vendor payments, confirm the payee, amount and supporting bill or payment record. Review duplicates and unfamiliar withdrawals promptly.

For transfers, match both sides. Moving cash between two practice-owned accounts does not create revenue or expense. Loan proceeds, repayments and owner transactions also require classification beyond a bank-feed description.

6. Resolve differences instead of forcing a match

Keep an exception log with the amount, account, likely cause, evidence needed, assigned owner and next action.

Difference Next step
Receipt recorded before month-end; deposit arrives afterward Verify the later settlement and document the timing difference
Check recorded but not yet cleared Confirm issuance and monitor subsequent clearing
Deposit is lower than the receipt batch Inspect fees, refunds, recoupments and batch composition
Same receipt appears twice in the books Trace its source and correct any duplicate posting
Unfamiliar withdrawal appears Escalate for authorization review and contact the bank when appropriate
Old clearing-account balance remains Trace the original receipt, deduction or settlement; do not simply write it off

A timing difference has a specific explanation and supporting evidence. “Probably timing” is not a completed investigation. An unusual transaction is also not proof of fraud, but it should receive human review rather than an automatic match.

7. Prove the balance and obtain sign-off

The adjusted bank balance and adjusted book balance should agree. Typically, the bank side reflects supported deposits in transit and outstanding payments; the book side reflects necessary corrections or previously unrecorded activity. A bank error requires documentation and follow-up, not an unsupported adjustment to the books.

Save the reconciliation, source reports, correction entries and open-item schedule together. The preparer should identify unresolved items explicitly. The CPA reviewer should evaluate significant judgments and document sign-off, with remaining exceptions assigned for follow-up.

A reconciliation that balances through an unexplained adjustment is not a reliable close. CPA review here is part of the practice's financial workflow, not an audit opinion or guarantee that every error has been detected.

Keep Entities Separate and Locations Visible

For a multi-entity practice, reconcile each bank account in the books of its legal owner. A transfer between entities should have corresponding records in both sets of books, with classification reviewed by the CPA. Do not net unrelated entity balances together to make a reconciliation work.

Location reporting is a separate question. One entity may operate several locations through a shared bank account. In that case, reconcile the bank account at the entity level, then use supported location coding to prepare location-level reporting.

Shared costs need an agreed allocation method. Central administrative payroll, for example, may require allocation across locations for management reporting, but that allocation does not create another bank transaction. Keeping cash reconciliation separate from cost allocation prevents duplicate expenses and misleading location P&Ls.

Use AI for Assistance, Not Final Authority

Depending on the tools and data available, AI-assisted work can help suggest transaction matches, group similar descriptions, compare batch totals and draft an exception list. These are useful preparatory tasks—not proof that the accounts are correct.

Human review remains necessary when:

  • Several deposits have identical amounts.
  • A payer combines batches or applies a recoupment.
  • A merchant settlement includes refunds or chargebacks.
  • An item may belong to another entity or location.
  • A proposed entry affects revenue, payroll, financing or owner balances.

Before accepting a suggested match, require supporting references and a traceable explanation. Test the workflow against previously reviewed transactions, including exceptions, rather than judging it only on straightforward matches. Do not allow an unexplained difference to trigger an automatic journal entry merely to balance the account.

Limit patient information in financial analysis

Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or a general-purpose AI tool. Use bank references, batch identifiers and summarized financial amounts where practical.

HHS guidance on covered entities and business associates explains that accounting services involving protected health information can raise business-associate responsibilities. Its minimum necessary guidance also provides context for limiting information where that standard applies, including exceptions. Have the appropriate privacy and legal advisers assess the actual workflow; using AI does not establish compliance.

Turn Reconciliation Into an Owner-Ready Close

The owner should receive more than a checkmark. A useful close summary identifies reconciled accounts, significant corrections, aged outstanding items and decisions that still need attention. It should also distinguish available cash from funds committed to payroll, vendor payments or other obligations.

If the books are behind, establish a supported starting point and work through missing periods in order. Current bank feeds do not repair historical duplicates or missing settlements by themselves.

FAQs

How often should a private medical practice reconcile bank accounts?

Use a monthly statement-based reconciliation as the core close process. More frequent checks may be useful for high-volume receipts or accounts with frequent payments. Choose a cadence that lets staff investigate exceptions promptly without treating a daily bank-feed review as a completed monthly close.

Why don't bank deposits equal billed charges?

Charges represent billed services, while deposits represent cash received. Payment timing, contractual adjustments, unpaid balances, refunds and settlement deductions can all affect the relationship. Reconcile deposits to receipt and settlement records; analyze charges and receivables separately.

Can AI fully reconcile practice bank accounts?

AI can assist with matching and identifying exceptions, but the practice should retain human approval for uncertain matches and accounting corrections. A suggested match does not establish authorization, correct entity ownership or appropriate accounting treatment.

What should staff do with an unidentified deposit?

Record its bank reference, date and amount, then assign billing or finance staff to obtain supporting detail. Use a documented, reviewer-approved temporary treatment if needed. Do not classify it as revenue solely because cash arrived, and keep it on the exception log until resolved.

Does a reconciled bank account mean the practice's P&L is correct?

No. Reconciliation supports the accuracy of recorded cash, but expenses, revenue, liabilities and location allocations can still be misclassified. Pair bank reconciliation with a broader close review before relying on the P&L for staffing, distributions or expansion decisions.
