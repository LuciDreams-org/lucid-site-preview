10 Financial KPIs for Staffed Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A busy schedule does not necessarily mean a private medical practice can comfortably fund payroll, replace equipment or open another location. Owners need a financial scorecard that connects services delivered, payer receipts, patient balances and operating costs.

These ten KPIs answer different questions: Is revenue growing? Is the practice profitable? Is that profit becoming cash? Can each location support its costs? Together, they help owners and managers make staffing, spending, borrowing and distribution decisions without relying on the bank balance alone.

Establish the scorecard before calculating it

Use a consistent monthly close, with rolling trends for context. Document each KPI’s definition, reporting period, accounting basis and responsible reviewer.

Start with reconciled bank and credit-card accounts, payroll reports, a categorized general ledger, an accounts-receivable aging report and debt schedules. The IRS guidance on business records supports maintaining complete records, separating business accounts and reconciling bank records. Those controls also make management reporting more dependable.

For multi-entity practices, calculate entity-level results before consolidating. Eliminate intercompany activity so transfers and shared-cost reimbursements do not inflate revenue or expenses. A consolidated cash balance does not establish that every entity can meet its own obligations.

Collections are not the same as earned revenue

Collections are cash received from payers and patients. Earned revenue reflects services delivered during the period, adjusted to the amount the practice expects to retain under its accounting policies. Cash received this month may settle earlier services; current services may remain unpaid at month-end.

Cash-basis books generally recognize receipts and payments when cash moves. Accrual reporting recognizes earned revenue and incurred expenses. A cash-basis practice can still maintain an accrual-style management scorecard, but its CPA should reconcile the differences rather than treating deposits as earned revenue.

Use the definitions below consistently. They are management-reporting conventions, not universal lender definitions or industry targets.

1. Net revenue

Formula: Gross service revenue − contractual adjustments − other applicable revenue reductions.

Source: Revenue reports, adjustment summaries and the reconciled general ledger.

Accounting-basis caveat: On an accrual scorecard, measure services earned during the period using reasonable estimates of expected reimbursement and collectibility. Gross billed charges are not net revenue. Apply a documented policy for refunds, concessions and credit losses so they are neither omitted nor deducted twice. On cash-basis reports, label receipts as collections rather than silently substituting them for earned revenue.

Decision it informs: Whether changes in staffing, provider availability, payer mix or service volume are generating additional revenue. Compare net revenue with prior periods and budget, then investigate the drivers rather than assuming every increase reflects higher productivity.

2. Operating margin

Formula: Operating income ÷ net revenue × 100, where operating income equals net revenue less operating expenses.

Source: Monthly profit-and-loss statement and supporting payroll and expense schedules.

Accounting-basis caveat: For this scorecard, operating expenses include provider and administrative compensation, benefits, occupancy, supplies and depreciation; they exclude interest and income taxes. Cash-basis results can swing with payment timing. Owner distributions are not payroll expense. If owner-clinician compensation is absent from the P&L, show a separate, clearly labeled compensation-adjusted view before comparing locations or periods.

Decision it informs: Whether operations can support existing costs and proposed commitments. A declining margin calls for separating revenue changes from labor, supply, occupancy and other expense changes—not automatically reducing headcount.

3. Operating cash flow margin

Formula: Net cash provided by operating activities ÷ net revenue × 100.

Source: Cash-flow statement reconciled to the general ledger and bank balances.

Accounting-basis caveat: This deliberately compares operating cash generation with earned revenue. Disclose that basis difference. Operating cash flow reflects collection timing and working-capital movements; it excludes borrowing proceeds, equipment purchases and owner distributions. State how interest and tax payments are classified and keep that treatment consistent.

Decision it informs: Whether reported earnings are turning into spendable cash. Strong operating margin with weak cash flow should trigger a review of receivables, vendor-payment timing and other working-capital changes before approving distributions.

4. Days cash on hand

Formula: Unrestricted operating cash ÷ average daily cash operating expense.

Calculate average daily cash operating expense as operating expenses excluding depreciation and other noncash items, divided by days in the measurement period.

Source: Reconciled bank balances, cash restrictions and the expense ledger.

Accounting-basis caveat: Exclude restricted cash and undrawn credit lines. This scorecard’s denominator excludes interest, debt principal, capital purchases and distributions. It therefore measures operating-expense coverage—not the number of days until every obligation exhausts cash. Use a representative expense period and supplement the ratio with a dated cash forecast.

Decision it informs: How much cushion exists for payer delays, payroll and unexpected expenses. Set the reserve policy around the practice’s actual payment cycles and obligations, not an unsupported universal target.

5. Receivable days

Formula: Ending net service accounts receivable ÷ net service revenue for the period × days in the period.

Source: Reconciled receivables report and service-revenue report covering the same entities and services.

Accounting-basis caveat: Match net receivables with net revenue; do not divide gross billed balances by net revenue. A cash-basis ledger may omit receivables, so maintain a separate management schedule. Ending balances can be distorted by rapid growth or seasonality; a longer revenue period or consistently calculated average balance can provide context.

Decision it informs: Whether more working capital is tied up in unpaid services. Review payer and patient balances separately to identify where investigation or follow-up is needed. This ratio is an estimate, not the actual average payment time for individual claims.

6. Aged-receivable share

Formula: Receivables older than the selected aging cutoff ÷ total receivables × 100.

Source: Payer and patient receivable-aging reports.

Accounting-basis caveat: Document the cutoff and whether aging starts from service date, claim date or another defined date. Keep numerator and denominator on the same valuation basis. Netting, credit balances, write-offs and aging resets can change the ratio without generating cash. Use a consistently valued aging schedule even if statutory or tax books are cash basis.

Decision it informs: Where unpaid balances are becoming concentrated. Assign review responsibility by payer, balance type and age bucket. A lower aged share is not automatically improvement if it results from write-offs rather than collections.

7. Staff cost ratio

Formula: Total staff cost ÷ net revenue × 100.

Source: Payroll register, benefits invoices, employer payroll-tax accounts and contract-labor expense records.

Accounting-basis caveat: Include employed providers, clinical support and administrative staff, plus employer taxes, benefits and contract labor used in staffing. Separate these groups for analysis. Accrue earned compensation and bonuses when using accrual revenue. Disclose how owner compensation is treated; distributions should not be disguised as staff costs.

Decision it informs: Whether staffing commitments fit revenue and operating needs. Review vacancies, overtime, temporary coverage and provider availability before concluding that an increased ratio means excess staffing.

8. Overhead ratio

Formula: Nonstaff operating expenses ÷ net revenue × 100.

Source: Categorized P&L, vendor records, fixed-asset schedule and shared-cost allocation schedule.

Accounting-basis caveat: Here, overhead includes all operating expenses outside the staff-cost definition, including supplies, occupancy, technology, insurance and depreciation. Other reports may define overhead differently, so publish the account mapping. Do not count payroll in both ratios. Allocate prepaid and annual expenses appropriately for accrual comparisons.

Decision it informs: Which nonstaff commitments are absorbing revenue. Investigate vendor contracts, supply purchasing and space utilization. Under these definitions, staff cost ratio plus overhead ratio plus operating margin equals 100%, subject to rounding.

9. Debt-service coverage

Formula: Cash available for debt service ÷ scheduled principal and interest payments.

For an internal cash-based view, define cash available as operating cash flow before interest payments included in operating activities.

Source: Cash-flow statement, loan statements and amortization schedules.

Accounting-basis caveat: Principal repayments do not appear as P&L expenses. The internal formula above is not necessarily a lender’s covenant formula; lenders may use adjusted earnings or other definitions. Use matching periods and include balloon payments when applicable. If there is no scheduled debt service, report “not applicable.”

Decision it informs: Whether cash generation can support existing debt or a proposed equipment loan. Historical coverage is not a borrowing limit: model future collections, capital needs and other cash demands separately.

10. Location contribution margin

Formula: (Location net revenue − directly attributable location operating costs) ÷ location net revenue × 100.

Source: Location-level revenue reports, tagged expenses, payroll assignments and occupancy records.

Accounting-basis caveat: Match location revenue and costs on the same basis. Include costs attributable to the site, such as assigned provider and support payroll, rent and supplies. Define treatment of staff working across sites. This scorecard excludes central shared costs from contribution margin; show those allocations separately to reach fully allocated operating profit.

Decision it informs: Whether a site contributes toward central costs and profit. Use it when evaluating schedules, leases or expansion, but do not treat contribution margin as cash available for distribution or assume all site costs disappear upon closure.

These results do not establish that the practice is financially healthy. The owner should investigate the gap between earnings and cash, review the older receivables, and forecast upcoming obligations before approving new spending. Equal location margins also do not mean equal dollar contributions.

Turn the scorecard into a monthly decision process

  1. Close and reconcile first. Resolve missing payroll, unposted adjustments and intercompany differences before interpreting changes.
  2. Show trends and explanations. Compare with budget, prior months and comparable seasonal periods. Annotate unusual payments or accounting changes.
  3. Assign one action per material exception. Name the owner, required investigation and next review date.
  4. Forecast before committing. Test proposed hiring, equipment purchases and distributions against expected receipts and payment dates.

Use aggregate financial data wherever possible. Do not place patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. When financial work requires patient information, confirm appropriate handling arrangements; HHS explains that accounting services involving protected health information can create business-associate responsibilities.

FAQs

Why not use ARR, subscription churn and SaaS LTV/CAC?

Most practice revenue depends on services delivered, payer terms, patient responsibility and collection timing—not a uniform subscription contract. Annualizing one month’s revenue does not make it contracted annual recurring revenue. A patient’s lack of a recent visit is not necessarily subscription churn. SaaS lifetime-value formulas based on subscription margins and cancellation rates therefore do not transfer directly. Practices can evaluate marketing costs separately using clearly defined, appropriately aggregated measures.

Can a cash-basis practice use these KPIs?

Yes, but receivable days and earned-revenue margins require supporting management schedules. Keep cash collections visible, label each reporting basis and have the CPA reconcile management reporting to the books. Do not mix cash receipts with accrual expenses without explaining the resulting limitations.

What targets should the owner set?

Start with reliable historical results, the operating budget, payer timing, staffing plans and debt obligations. Set practice-specific review thresholds and revisit them when the business changes. The illustrative scorecard is a calculation example, not a set of targets or evidence that a particular ratio is good or bad.
