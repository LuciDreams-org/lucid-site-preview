AI-Assisted Debt-to-Equity Review for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

Before borrowing for equipment, contributing owner funds or approving a distribution, a medical practice owner needs a reliable balance sheet—not just a ratio that looks favorable.

The debt-to-equity ratio compares a defined amount of debt with recorded owners’ equity. It can help you understand the practice’s financing structure, but it does not establish how much the practice can safely borrow or distribute. A calculation based on unreconciled loans, misclassified owner transfers or very small equity can be misleading.

AI can assist with arithmetic, comparisons and exception lists. The useful workflow is to reconcile the records, agree on definitions, model proposed transactions and then test cash-service capacity. Your CPA, lender and legal counsel should resolve classification, covenant, ownership and tax questions before you act.

What the debt-to-equity ratio actually measures

A common calculation is:

Debt-to-equity ratio = interest-bearing debt ÷ recorded owners’ equity

Under this definition, debt generally includes outstanding borrowing such as equipment loans, credit lines and qualifying owner loans. Owners’ equity is the residual amount shown on the balance sheet:

Recorded equity = recorded assets − recorded liabilities

However, some analyses use total liabilities divided by equity, rather than interest-bearing debt divided by equity. A lender may also use its own definitions of debt, tangible net worth or adjusted equity. These are not interchangeable calculations.

Every report should state:

  • The balance-sheet date and accounting basis.
  • The entity or reporting group covered.
  • Which accounts are included in debt.
  • Whether equity is recorded book equity or a lender-defined adjustment.
  • Any unresolved balances or proposed adjustments.

For a multi-location practice operating within one entity, location-level P&Ls help explain operating performance, but locations may not have independent legal debt or meaningful standalone equity. For multiple entities, do not combine balance sheets without reviewing intercompany balances and the lender’s reporting requirements.

There is no universal optimal ratio

A practice with predictable collections, modest capital needs and substantial cash may have a different borrowing capacity from one opening a new location or replacing major equipment. The same ratio can describe very different repayment risks.

Use the ratio to ask better questions, not to select a universal target. Relevant comparisons include the practice’s own history using consistent definitions, the lender’s stated requirements and the effects of a specific proposed transaction.

Why recorded equity needs a closer look

Book equity is not the market value of the practice, the value of an ownership interest or cash available for withdrawal.

Its amount depends on recorded assets and liabilities, accumulated earnings or losses, owner contributions and distributions, and the accounting basis used. For example, a cash-basis balance sheet may not include patient and payer receivables in the same way as an accrual-basis balance sheet. A building or equipment asset may have a carrying value different from its current economic value.

Balance-sheet issue Why it matters to the ratio Review before relying on the result
Unposted current-period earnings Equity may be incomplete if the report omits current income Confirm that the reporting format includes current-period results

Do not add an estimated practice value to equity simply to improve the ratio. Any adjustment needs a defined purpose and an appropriate accounting or lender basis.

Reconcile the balance sheet before modeling a decision

Start with a closed reporting period. A practice manager can assemble the supporting records, while the accounting team determines necessary corrections.

1. Tie cash to bank reconciliations

Match each entity’s operating, payroll and other bank accounts to its reconciliation. Investigate stale checks, unmatched deposits and transfers posted to only one side.

Payer receipts and patient collections should be summarized appropriately for financial analysis. A debt review generally does not require patient-identifiable records.

2. Reconcile every borrowing account

Compare loan and credit-line balances with lender statements and schedules. Separate principal payments from interest expense. Include the current and long-term portions of a loan when calculating total outstanding borrowing; do not count the same balance twice.

Review credit cards, equipment financing and lease-related balances explicitly rather than assuming every liability belongs in the selected debt definition.

3. Roll equity forward

Reconcile opening equity to closing equity using current-period earnings, contributions, distributions and approved adjustments. Investigate unexplained changes rather than treating equity as a balancing plug.

4. Confirm the reporting boundary

Identify the borrower, any guarantors and the entities included in the review. A property entity’s mortgage and an operating practice’s equipment loan should not be casually combined. If consolidated reporting is needed, have the accounting team review intercompany eliminations.

5. Document the calculation

Keep a short schedule listing each included account, its balance and the reason it belongs in the numerator or denominator. Save unresolved questions separately so they remain visible to the decision-makers.

The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices provide the foundation for a useful ratio review; they do not establish a lender’s ratio definition.

Resolve owner loans before calling them debt or equity

An owner deposit is not automatically an equity contribution, and a ledger account labeled “owner loan” does not settle its classification.

Ask the CPA and legal counsel to review the underlying documents, repayment terms, interest provisions and how the transaction has actually been handled. Also ask the lender how it treats owner debt. Subordinated owner borrowing may receive different treatment under a particular agreement, but that treatment must be confirmed—not assumed.

Separate two directions of funding:

  • Amounts the practice owes an owner: potentially a liability, subject to proper classification and documentation.
  • Amounts an owner owes the practice: potentially an asset, with separate questions about recoverability and lender treatment.

A proposed conversion of an owner loan into equity requires more than changing a spreadsheet label. Obtain advice on approvals, ownership implications, documentation and tax consequences before recording it.

Use AI to compare proposed transactions—not to authorize them

Once the baseline is reconciled, an approved AI-assisted workflow can help prepare a comparison table and flag inconsistent assumptions. Use summarized account balances and transaction descriptions without patient-identifiable information. Do not put patient-identifiable data into ordinary Slack or a general-purpose AI tool.

Give the analysis a narrow assignment:

Using these reconciled balances and the stated debt definition, calculate each proposed transaction independently. Show the change in cash, debt, equity and debt-to-equity. Identify missing assumptions and do not infer accounting, tax or covenant treatment.

A human reviewer should verify the formulas, account mapping and transaction entries. AI output is a draft analysis, not accounting evidence or lender approval.

Illustrative example: one practice, several alternatives

All amounts in this example are illustrative, not benchmarks or recommendations. Assume a practice has $600,000 in recorded assets, $360,000 in total liabilities, $300,000 in interest-bearing debt, $240,000 in equity and $140,000 in cash.

Its interest-bearing debt-to-equity ratio is 1.25. Its total-liabilities-to-equity ratio is 1.50—showing why the definition matters.

The following alternatives are independent, measured immediately after each transaction. They exclude fees, tax effects, operating results and other simultaneous changes.

Illustrative alternative Cash after transaction Interest-bearing debt Book equity Debt-to-equity
No transaction $140,000 $300,000 $240,000 1.25
Borrow $60,000 and retain the cash $200,000 $360,000 $240,000 1.50
Receive a $60,000 owner equity contribution $200,000 $300,000 $300,000 1.00
Repay $60,000 of loan principal with cash $80,000 $240,000 $240,000 1.00
Make a $60,000 owner distribution $80,000 $300,000 $180,000 1.67
Convert $60,000 of included owner debt into equity, if properly approved and classified $140,000 $240,000 $300,000 0.80

The table exposes the ratio’s limits. A contribution and a principal repayment produce the same ratio here, but leave very different cash balances. Converting owner debt improves the ratio without adding cash. A distribution reduces both liquidity and equity.

Borrowing for equipment also needs a second step: model the equipment purchase and the resulting cash balance, not just the loan proceeds sitting in the bank.

Low or negative equity can make the ratio unhelpful

When positive equity is very small, modest accounting adjustments or distributions can produce large ratio swings. That volatility does not necessarily represent an equally large change in operating performance.

With zero equity, division is undefined. With negative equity, a negative ratio can be calculated mathematically, but it should not be presented as favorable leverage. Label the result clearly and explain why the conventional comparison is not meaningful.

The next step is to investigate the cause: accumulated losses, past distributions, accounting errors, asset adjustments or another documented factor. Then assess liquidity and financing needs directly. A contribution may change the balance sheet, but it does not by itself fix recurring operating losses.

Pair every ratio comparison with cash and lender checks

Before approving borrowing, repayment or a distribution, prepare a cash forecast that covers expected payer receipts, patient collections, provider and administrative payroll, rent, shared costs, taxes and scheduled debt payments.

Test slower collections and operating disruptions. For a multi-location group, review both entity-level cash obligations and location-level operating performance; a profitable location does not automatically make cash available to another entity.

Decision check Question to resolve
Debt service Can forecast cash cover principal and interest while funding operations?
Liquidity What cash remains after the transaction and near-term commitments?
Collection timing What happens if payer receipts arrive later than expected?
Financing terms Are there variable rates, balloon payments or near-term maturities?
Covenant definitions Which debt, equity and cash-flow adjustments does the agreement require?
Transaction restrictions Does the agreement restrict distributions, additional borrowing or owner-loan repayment?

If a lender requires a debt-service coverage calculation, use its specified numerator, denominator and measurement period. Do not substitute the debt-to-equity ratio for repayment capacity. Have the lender and advisers resolve covenant interpretation before relying on a transaction’s apparent benefit.

Turn the review into an owner decision packet

A useful packet contains the reconciled balance sheet, documented ratio definition, owner-loan questions, independent transaction comparisons, cash forecast and lender requirements. End with a decision, outstanding approvals and responsibility for follow-up—not an AI-generated recommendation to “optimize” leverage.

Frequently asked questions

Does a lower debt-to-equity ratio always mean a healthier practice?

No. Paying down debt can lower the ratio while leaving too little cash for payroll and other obligations. Assess liquidity, operating performance and debt-service capacity alongside the ratio.

Should accounts payable count as debt?

They count in a total-liabilities-to-equity calculation, but are generally outside an interest-bearing-debt calculation unless the chosen definition says otherwise. State the definition and confirm any lender-specific treatment.

Can AI decide whether an owner deposit is a loan or contribution?

No. AI can flag inconsistent descriptions or missing documents, but classification requires professional review of the transaction’s substance and documentation. Refer accounting and tax questions to the CPA and ownership or legal questions to counsel.

How often should the practice review the ratio?

Review it using reconciled reporting periods, before material financing or owner transactions, and whenever a lending agreement requires reporting. Use consistent definitions so changes are explainable.
