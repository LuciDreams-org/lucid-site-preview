AI-Assisted Fee Schedule Analysis for Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A medical practice’s listed fees do not tell its owner how much revenue the practice can collect. Payer agreements, patient responsibility, adjustments, unresolved balances and payment timing all sit between the approved charge and the money received.

That makes fee schedule analysis a diagnostic task before it becomes a pricing decision. The useful question is not simply, “Should we raise our fees?” It is, “Which discrepancies affect our economics, what explains them, and who needs to review them?”

AI can help organize comparisons and flag exceptions when the source data is suitable. It should not decide what a payer agreement permits, classify every shortfall as lost revenue or automatically change prices. This workflow is about understanding an existing fee schedule—not dynamic pricing, demand-based price changes, elasticity modeling or recommendations about which services to provide.

Define the Financial Question First

Start with a specific owner decision: whether an apparent fee discrepancy deserves accounting correction, payer-contract review or a separate pricing review.

Keep these amounts distinct:

Measure What it means What it does not establish
Approved charge The practice’s authorized listed charge for the relevant service and period The amount a payer or patient must pay
Actual posted charge The charge recorded for a completed service Proof that the correct fee schedule was used
Contractual allowed amount The amount determined under the applicable payer agreement and payment rules Cash received or guaranteed collection
Realized collections Payer and patient receipts, net of applicable refunds or reversals Final collection performance while balances remain unresolved
Attributable cost Costs assigned to the activity using a documented method Full practice profitability unless all relevant costs are included

A wide difference between charges and receipts may reflect normal contractual allowances rather than an incorrect fee. Conversely, a small difference can hide a posting error or an unfavorable cost relationship.

For some agreements, billed charges can affect payment calculations. Have a qualified payer-contract reviewer confirm the applicable terms rather than assuming every listed-fee increase produces additional collectible revenue.

Build a Comparable Dataset

Ask the practice manager, billing team and finance team to define one review scope. Use a consistent service period, payment cutoff, entity, location and payer grouping. Start narrowly enough that someone can verify the exceptions against source records.

Separate service dates from receipt dates

Comparing this month’s charges with this month’s bank deposits mixes different groups of services. Some deposits relate to earlier work, while recent services may not yet have been adjudicated or paid.

Instead, choose a service-date cohort and track its charges, adjustments, collections and outstanding balances through a stated cutoff. Recent cohorts may be useful, but label them as incomplete. Use older, more fully resolved cohorts to assess realized collection patterns.

Collect the inputs needed to explain differences

Input Useful fields or supporting detail Validation question
Approved fee schedule Service identifier, charge, effective dates, location or entity applicability Which version was authorized when the service occurred?
Posted charges Service date, units, relevant modifiers, location and payer category Are units, identifiers and scope comparable?
Contract reference Applicable agreement, amendments and verified payment terms Has a qualified reviewer confirmed the relevant provision?
Adjustment detail Contractual adjustments, other approved adjustments and reversals Are adjustment categories being used consistently?
Receipts and balances Aggregate payer receipts, patient receipts, refunds and open balances Are payments attributed to the correct service cohort?
Cost support Relevant labor, supplies and allocation assumptions Is the cost assigned consistently without double-counting?

Do not fill missing contract terms with an AI estimate. Mark them as unverified and exclude them from conclusions that depend on a confirmed allowed amount.

Reconcile the financial totals

The billing ledger explains charges, adjustments, payments and balances. The accounting records explain recorded revenue, cash and expenses under the practice’s accounting method. Bank deposits help validate cash movement, but do not by themselves identify the service period or explain every difference.

Reconcile aggregate receipts to the books and bank activity, allowing for documented timing differences, refunds, unapplied cash and payment-processing deductions where relevant. The IRS guidance on business recordkeeping supports complete records and bank reconciliation; it is not a payer-contract interpretation guide.

Use a Fee-to-Collection Bridge

A bridge makes the economics visible without calling every dollar below the listed fee a loss.

Illustrative example only: A practice reviews 100 comparable service units for one payer and one service-date cohort. All amounts below are invented to demonstrate the method.

Bridge item Amount Interpretation
Approved and correctly posted charges $20,000 100 units at a listed charge of $200
Less verified contractual allowances ($8,000) Reduction supported by the applicable payment terms
Allowed amount $12,000 Amount before other approved adjustments and collection outcomes
Less separately reviewed noncontractual adjustments ($300) Assumed valid for this illustration; not automatically contractual
Net amount remaining to collect or already collected $11,700 Provisional amount after the reviewed adjustments
Payer receipts through cutoff $9,000 Cash attributed to this cohort
Patient receipts through cutoff $1,800 Cash attributed to this cohort
Total realized collections $10,800 Combined receipts through cutoff
Remaining receivable $900 Unresolved balance, not automatically a loss

The bridge reconciles: $10,800 collected plus $900 outstanding equals $11,700 after adjustments.

The practice has not demonstrated a $9,200 pricing problem simply because collections are $9,200 below listed charges. Most of that difference is a verified contractual allowance. The remaining difference includes a separately reviewed adjustment and an unresolved receivable.

Now add costs carefully. Suppose attributable costs are $7,000 in this same illustrative example. Collections less those costs equal $3,800. If the remaining $900 is collectible, the provisional net amount less those costs would be $4,700.

Neither figure is automatically accounting profit. The first mixes receipts through a cutoff with assigned service-period costs; the second depends on the remaining balance being collectible. Both may exclude shared overhead. Use them as clearly labeled management measures, not substitutes for the practice’s P&L.

Turn AI Flags into a Review Queue

With validated inputs, AI-assisted analysis can help group exceptions, compare effective fee versions, summarize variances and draft questions for reviewers. Calculations should remain reproducible in a spreadsheet or other controlled analysis, with links or references to supporting records.

Each flag needs a category before anyone proposes a price change.

Queue Example discrepancy First reviewer Appropriate next step
Data or posting error Actual charge differs from the approved schedule Practice manager and billing lead Verify effective date, units and posting; correct only through an authorized process
Contractual constraint Listed charge exceeds the verified allowed amount Qualified payer-contract reviewer Confirm terms; do not treat the allowance as recoverable revenue
Payment exception Receipt differs from the verified expected payment Billing lead with contract support Investigate adjudication, adjustments and payment attribution
Timing or unresolved balance Recent cohort shows low collections and substantial open balances Billing and finance teams Separate pending amounts from final collection outcomes
Owner-controlled pricing question An authorized fee appears outdated in an area where the practice may have discretion Owner with qualified contract and applicable-requirements review Evaluate separately before approving any change
Cost or allocation question Similar collections produce different apparent contributions by location Finance lead or CPA Validate staffing costs, service mix and shared-cost allocation

Prioritize supported dollars, not dramatic percentages

A large percentage variance on a small or incomplete cohort may matter less than a recurring, well-supported discrepancy across many comparable services.

For each queue item, record:

  • The affected service period, payer, entity and location.
  • The verified amount involved, or a clearly labeled estimate.
  • Whether the cause is confirmed, suspected or unknown.
  • Whether it appears isolated or recurring.
  • The evidence needed, responsible reviewer and next action.

Keep “financial exposure under review” separate from “recoverable amount.” A flagged difference is not proof that more money is owed or can be collected.

Compare Costs Without Misreading Location Performance

Fee schedule analysis becomes more useful when it explains why similar collections produce different financial results. It becomes misleading when inconsistent allocations make one office appear less profitable.

Use a consistent cost definition. Relevant costs may include provider compensation, support-staff time and supplies attributable to the reviewed activity. State whether the analysis uses incremental costs, assigned operating costs or fully allocated costs; these answer different questions.

For multi-location practices, show shared administrative payroll, rent or other overhead separately before applying a documented allocation method. Avoid treating an allocation as an exact measure of resource consumption.

For multi-entity owners, retain entity-level visibility. Do not combine receipts from one entity with expenses from another without a supported reconciliation. Review the findings alongside location and entity P&Ls before concluding that a fee schedule explains a margin difference.

A negative contribution estimate should trigger validation of collections, costs and scope—not an automated recommendation to change clinical services.

Keep Human Approval and Data Boundaries Explicit

Use AI to prepare questions and organize evidence, not to approve contractual interpretations, balance adjustments or fee changes.

A practical approval sequence is:

  1. Billing review: Validate charges, units, adjustments, payment attribution and unresolved balances.
  2. Finance review: Reconcile receipts and confirm cost assumptions and accounting treatment.
  3. Qualified contract and requirements review: Confirm payer terms and applicable requirements before proposing pricing or patient-balance changes. Obtain legal advice where needed.
  4. Owner approval: Decide whether the evidence supports a separate pricing review, an operational correction or no change.

Minimize patient information in financial analysis. Aggregate data where practical, and use appropriately reviewed environments for any sensitive information. Do not place patient-identifiable data in ordinary Slack channels or general-purpose AI tools.

The HHS minimum necessary guidance explains that the standard has exceptions. The HHS Privacy Rule summary also explains covered entities and business associates. These sources support careful workflow review, not a blanket claim that a particular tool or provider is compliant.

Make the Final Output a Decision Memo

The owner should receive a short memo supported by the bridge and exception queue. It should explain which differences are verified, which remain uncertain, who owns each next step and whether a separate fee review is warranted.

If a correction or approved change follows, compare later results using the same definitions and sufficiently developed service cohorts. Account for payer mix, service mix and payment timing before attributing a change in collections to the fee schedule.

FAQs

Does raising a medical practice’s listed fees increase collections?

Not necessarily. Collections depend on applicable payer terms, patient responsibility and collection outcomes. A higher listed charge may have no effect on a contractually determined payment, though some payment provisions can depend on billed charges. Verify the specific agreement and applicable requirements before considering a change.

Can AI determine whether a payer underpaid the practice?

AI can help identify differences between recorded payments and verified expectations. It cannot establish an underpayment from a charge-to-payment gap alone. A qualified reviewer must validate the applicable terms, units, adjustments and payment attribution before treating a discrepancy as actionable.

How should outstanding patient balances appear in the analysis?

Show them separately from collected cash, contractual allowances and approved noncontractual adjustments. Use a stated cutoff and distinguish pending balances from resolved collection outcomes. Do not assume every open balance will be collected or that every unpaid balance is a pricing problem.

What should an owner do when the analysis shows weak margins?

First validate the fee-to-collection bridge and cost assignments. Then separate payment issues, unresolved balances, contractual constraints and overhead allocation effects. Only after those checks should the owner consider a distinct pricing review with qualified review of agreements and applicable requirements.
