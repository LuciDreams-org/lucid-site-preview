Investment Treaty Questions for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A proposed investment from an overseas owner can raise questions that ordinary bookkeeping cannot answer. The same is true when a U.S. medical practice owner considers investing in a business abroad. Before assuming an investment treaty matters, establish who is investing, where the relevant entities are located, what interest is being acquired, and which legal text could apply.

A foreign connection does not automatically create treaty protection. For a wholly domestic private practice with domestic ownership and investment, investment-treaty analysis may have no role. Paying an overseas supplier also is not, by itself, a reason to commission a treaty review.

The useful owner decision is narrower: Does this particular ownership or capital arrangement warrant specialist legal advice, and what information will let counsel answer efficiently? This article explains that intake workflow and the financial records that support it. It does not determine treaty coverage, healthcare ownership permissions, tax treatment, or available remedies.

What Investment Treaties Have to Do With Practice Ownership

Investment treaties are agreements between countries that may address the treatment of qualifying foreign investments. They can be bilateral or multilateral; investment provisions may also appear in broader agreements. Their relevance depends on the actual parties, jurisdictions, investment structure, timing, and applicable text—not simply on a transaction being described as international.

Some agreements contain investor-state dispute provisions. Those provisions should not be confused with an arbitration clause in a purchase agreement or a routine dispute with a lender, landlord, or business partner. Counsel must evaluate the particular agreement and circumstances before concluding that any treaty-based process is available.

For general background, the Columbia Center on Sustainable Investment’s primer on investment treaties and investor-state dispute settlement explains the broader subject. It does not establish coverage for a particular medical practice transaction.

Treaty review is also separate from determining whether a proposed practice ownership or management arrangement is permissible. Healthcare counsel should evaluate applicable ownership, licensing, and transaction requirements. A treaty analysis is not permission to proceed with a structure that otherwise fails legal review.

First Decide Which Review You Actually Need

Start with a short transaction description rather than a treaty search. Identify the proposed investor, recipient entity, countries involved, funding instrument, intended use of proceeds, and expected closing sequence.

Then route the question to the right advisers:

Practice situation Initial review Role of investment-treaty advice
A domestic practice adds a domestic owner, with no identified cross-border investment element Healthcare transaction counsel and CPA review May be absent; do not assume a treaty issue exists
A practice pays an overseas equipment supplier Purchasing, payment, accounting, and any relevant legal or tax review An overseas invoice alone does not establish a treaty question
An overseas investor proposes an interest in a U.S. practice-related entity Healthcare ownership counsel, transaction counsel, and tax advisers Ask whether the actual parties and structure warrant specialist screening
A U.S. owner proposes an investment in an operating business abroad Counsel familiar with the destination jurisdiction and cross-border transactions A specialist can assess whether an applicable treaty merits further analysis
An existing cross-border investor reports government action affecting its investment Prompt legal review of the facts and chronology Counsel should assess any potentially relevant treaty provisions without assuming coverage or a remedy

This screening is not a substitute for legal analysis. Its purpose is to avoid two errors: overlooking a potentially relevant cross-border investment issue and turning every foreign payment into an unnecessary treaty project.

Build an Adviser Intake Packet, Not Just a Question List

A list of treaty questions cannot substitute for evidence about the transaction. Counsel needs a coherent account of ownership, funding, documents, and timing. Assign a practice manager or finance lead to assemble the packet, while counsel identifies missing legal information.

Entity chart and operating footprint

Prepare a dated chart showing existing entities and the proposed post-transaction structure. Distinguish legal entities from practice locations. Two offices may belong to one entity, while a single location may involve several entities with different roles.

Show which entity employs providers and administrative staff, receives payer and patient receipts, holds relevant assets, and enters material contracts. Identify any management or shared-services entity and explain its actual role without assuming the arrangement is legally permissible.

Label proposed entities and unsigned arrangements clearly. Do not present a planned structure as an established fact.

Investor identities and ownership history

Provide the legal identities of the proposed investors and available information about direct and indirect ownership. Include formation jurisdictions and the citizenship, nationality, residence, or control information counsel requests. These concepts are not interchangeable, and the relevant legal test must come from counsel’s analysis.

Do not stop at a fund’s brand name or a holding company’s mailing address. Flag incomplete ownership information rather than making assumptions about the ultimate investor.

Capital-flow history

Trace relevant funding from its source to the recipient entity. For each contribution, advance, loan, transfer, repayment, or distribution, identify the parties, date, currency, purpose, supporting document, and accounting entry.

Keep capital transactions separate from operating receipts. Payer reimbursements and patient collections are not investor contributions. An owner advance should not be labeled equity merely because it funded payroll; its classification needs support from the documents and CPA review.

Transaction documents and chronology

Collect executed agreements, current drafts, amendments, ownership records, financing documents, approvals, and material correspondence. Include a dated chronology of relevant formation events, acquisitions, funding, restructuring, and any government measures or disputes already identified.

The packet should make gaps visible:

Packet component What to include Why it helps the review
Entity chart Current and proposed ownership, jurisdictions, operating roles Identifies the parties and structure counsel must assess
Investor identity file Legal names and requested ownership or nationality information Supports analysis of who might qualify under the applicable text
Capital-flow schedule Transfers tied to bank records, agreements, and ledger entries Connects funding to the claimed investment and recipient entity
Transaction file Signed documents, drafts, amendments, approvals Establishes the rights and obligations actually agreed or proposed
Event chronology Material dates and supporting correspondence Allows counsel to assess timing questions and identify missing facts
Open-items log Conflicting records, missing documents, unresolved classifications Prevents uncertain information from being treated as settled

The IRS guidance on starting a business and keeping records supports complete business records, separate business accounts, and bank reconciliation. Those practices help produce reliable financial evidence; they do not establish treaty eligibility.

Ask Counsel for a Scoped Coverage Assessment

Once the intake packet is assembled, request an initial assessment with a defined scope. The deliverable should identify whether further treaty analysis is warranted, what facts remain unresolved, and which transaction decisions depend on the answer.

Useful questions belong in several categories rather than a generic numbered checklist.

Coverage and applicable text

Ask counsel which agreement, if any, could be relevant and why. Which parties and jurisdictions matter? Does the proposed interest fall within the applicable definition of investment? What ownership, control, nationality, timing, exclusions, or other conditions require investigation?

Ask for the analysis to distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions. An answer based on an incomplete ownership chart should not become an unconditional statement in an investment presentation.

Relationship to healthcare and transaction review

Ask which issues require separate healthcare, local-law, corporate, or tax advice. Confirm that any treaty discussion is not being treated as approval of ownership rights, management authority, clinical control, or the underlying transaction.

Do not create intermediary entities or reroute capital based on an assumed treaty advantage. Evaluate the proposed commercial arrangement on its actual facts rather than treating treaty shopping as a financing strategy.

Dispute provisions and practical limits

If a potentially relevant agreement is identified, ask counsel to explain its dispute provisions and limitations. Topics may include consent, notice, procedural conditions, timing, interaction with other proceedings, costs, and uncertainty. Do not assume arbitration is available, private, faster, or preferable to another process.

If a dispute already exists or government action is anticipated, promptly provide the chronology and communications to counsel. Do not send notices, make claims, or restructure ownership in an attempt to create rights without advice.

Written conclusions and next steps

Request a short written outcome: no relevant treaty identified on the stated facts, additional information needed, or further specialist analysis recommended. Ask what changes in the transaction would require reassessment.

The goal is a usable decision record—not a promise of protection or an abstract catalogue of treaty provisions.

Keep the Practice’s Financial Decision Separate From Legal Coverage

Even if counsel identifies a potentially relevant treaty, the practice must still decide whether the capital arrangement works financially. Do not build a financing plan around a presumed legal recovery or an assumed improvement in funding terms.

Prepare a cash forecast showing when funds are expected, which entity receives them, and how proceeds would be used. Include transaction expenses and a delayed-closing scenario. For a staffed practice, the immediate concern may be keeping provider and administrative payroll funded while payer receipts continue on their normal collection cycle.

Financial question Supporting analysis Owner decision
Can the practice operate if funding arrives late? Cash forecast using expected receipts, payroll, rent, and other obligations Whether to delay spending or revise the closing plan
Which entity needs the money? Entity-level balances and documented proposed transfers Whether funding matches the entity incurring the obligations
Will a new location require support from existing offices? Per-location P&Ls and cash needs, with shared costs explained Whether expansion is affordable without obscuring existing performance
What does the arrangement cost before any operating benefit? Adviser estimates, financing terms, and transaction expenses Whether to proceed, renegotiate, or stop

Keep legal conclusions out of accounting assumptions unless advisers have supplied a clear basis. The CPA should separately assess accounting and tax treatment; treaty screening does not establish either.

Give Each Adviser a Clear Role

The practice owner should retain responsibility for the commercial decision. The practice manager can coordinate documents and open items. The finance team can reconcile accounts, trace capital, and prepare entity- or location-level reporting. Healthcare and transaction counsel assess their respective legal issues, while investment-treaty specialists interpret potentially relevant treaty provisions.

Clean books, catch-up bookkeeping, and location or entity financial visibility can support the financial side of an adviser intake. They do not replace specialist treaty advice.

Keep the packet focused on business and investment records. Aggregate financial reports will often be more useful than patient-level detail. HHS explains business-associate responsibilities and the minimum necessary standard and its exceptions. Have the appropriate adviser assess any proposed sharing of protected information; do not put patient-identifiable records into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools.

A Practical Stopping Point

The immediate objective is not to obtain a favorable treaty answer. It is to know whether a specialist review is relevant, what remains uncertain, and whether the financing decision can proceed on a sound legal and financial basis.

Start with the actual transaction, assemble the evidence, and obtain a scoped assessment. Keep the ownership review, treaty analysis, tax advice, and cash forecast distinct but coordinated. For a domestic practice with no relevant cross-border investment element, the appropriate outcome may simply be to document that screening result and focus on the transaction issues that do apply.

FAQs

Does a medical practice need treaty advice whenever it makes an overseas payment?

No. A foreign supplier payment is not the same as a cross-border ownership or capital investment. Review the payment’s accounting, contractual, and other relevant requirements through the usual advisers. Ask about treaty screening when the actual investment parties, jurisdictions, structure, or circumstances create a substantive reason to do so.

Does a foreign investor automatically give a U.S. private practice treaty protection?

No. Counsel must examine the actual investor, ownership chain, investment, jurisdictions, timing, and applicable text. A foreign investor’s involvement also does not establish that the proposed healthcare ownership arrangement is permissible.

What should the practice prepare before an investment-treaty consultation?

Prepare a dated entity chart, investor identity information, capital-flow history, transaction documents, and a chronology of material events. Add an open-items log showing missing records and unresolved facts. This substantive intake packet gives counsel a basis to assess coverage and dispute provisions; a standalone list of questions does not.

Can the practice’s CPA decide whether a treaty applies?

The CPA can help establish reliable financial facts, trace funding, assess accounting and tax issues, and model cash needs. Investment-treaty coverage and dispute provisions require appropriately qualified legal counsel. Coordinate their work without treating financial reporting as a legal coverage opinion.
