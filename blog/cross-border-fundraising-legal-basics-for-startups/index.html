Cross-Border Fundraising Preparation for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

An overseas investor or lender may offer capital for a new location, equipment, an acquisition or working capital. Before discussing a commitment, however, a private medical practice needs to answer a more basic question: Can this particular source fund this particular entity on acceptable, legally supportable terms?

For a staffed U.S. practice, that decision involves more than an investor presentation or international wire transfer. Ownership eligibility, control rights, transaction structure, tax treatment and the practice’s ability to support repayment or distributions all need separate review.

The owner’s first task is to prepare an adviser-led feasibility review—not to select a structure independently. This guide explains what to organize, which questions to assign and when to pause before approaching or accepting overseas funds.

Start With a Feasibility Review, Not a Funding Pitch

U.S. medical-practice ownership and control restrictions require jurisdiction- and fact-specific legal assessment. Do not assume that a structure used by another business, another specialty or a practice in another state is available to yours.

Engage healthcare counsel familiar with the relevant states, together with transaction or securities counsel and a CPA with appropriate cross-border tax experience. Ask them to identify any additional advisers needed in the proposed funder’s jurisdiction.

Give the team a short transaction summary covering:

  • The legal entity seeking funding and the locations it operates.
  • Current owners, ownership interests and decision-making rights.
  • The proposed funder’s identity, location and ownership structure.
  • The amount, currency, timing and intended use of capital.
  • Whether the proposal involves debt, equity, conversion rights or another arrangement.
  • Any promised repayment, distributions, security, approval rights or operational influence.

Identify unknowns explicitly. “Terms not yet discussed” is more useful than filling gaps with assumptions that advisers may mistake for agreed facts.

A feasibility review should produce a decision: proceed to carefully scoped discussions, revise the proposal, obtain more information or stop. It is not a blanket approval for later changes in terms.

Assemble the Practice’s Funding Review Packet

Create a central document index with a responsible person and status for each item. Distinguish executed agreements from drafts, and label financial statements with their reporting period and accounting basis.

Packet section What to organize Why it matters
Entity documents Formation documents, governing agreements, amendments and a legal-entity chart Establishes which entity would receive capital and what authority it has
Ownership and control Current ownership records, transfers, voting arrangements and relevant professional-owner information Allows counsel to assess eligibility and decision-making rights
Existing obligations Loans, security agreements, guarantees, leases and agreements containing relevant consent provisions Helps advisers identify restrictions or approvals that may affect a transaction

Keep practice entities distinct from an owner’s personal finances and from any separate property or administrative entities. An organization chart should show relationships; it should not imply that money can move freely between them.

The IRS’s business recordkeeping guidance supports complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliation. Those practices also provide a useful financial foundation for a funding review, although they do not establish transaction eligibility.

Define the Capital Need Before Comparing Offers

A proposal can look attractive while solving the wrong problem. Determine whether the practice needs temporary liquidity, long-term investment capital or a change in ownership—and whether an operating problem is being mistaken for a financing need.

For example, delayed payer receipts can create a different cash requirement from a new location that must cover provider and administrative payroll before reaching sustainable collections. A recurring operating shortfall needs its own plan; a capital injection does not resolve its cause.

Prepare a capital-use schedule that separates:

  • One-time spending, such as equipment or location setup.
  • Working capital for payroll, occupancy and other operating expenses.
  • Existing debt repayment, if contemplated.
  • Transaction and advisory costs.
  • A separately identified contingency allowance.

An illustrative practice scenario

Illustrative example: A two-location practice is considering overseas funding for a third location. Its review packet separates build-out and equipment costs from opening-period payroll and operating expenses. It also shows existing-location cash needs, rather than assuming all current cash is available for expansion.

The owner asks the finance team to model delayed opening, slower payer receipts and higher staffing costs. Counsel separately reviews the proposed funder’s requested rights. A forecast showing enough cash does not establish that the proposed ownership or control arrangement is permissible.

For a multi-location practice, show location-level results alongside entity-level statements. Explain shared-cost allocations so advisers can distinguish actual location performance from accounting presentation.

Evaluate Debt and Equity as Different Commitments

Debt and equity are not interchangeable routes to the same outcome. Each changes the practice’s economics, obligations and potentially its governance. Neither should be assumed permissible merely because it is described as a loan or a minority investment.

Decision area Debt questions Equity questions
Cash burden When would principal, interest and fees become payable? Can collections support them? What distributions or other economic returns are contemplated?
Ownership and control Do covenants, security rights or default remedies raise control concerns? Is the proposed owner eligible, and are the requested voting and approval rights permissible?
Downside exposure What happens if receipts fall or the practice breaches a covenant? How are losses, disputes and additional capital needs addressed?
Exit What are the repayment, refinancing and early-exit terms? What transfer, redemption, valuation or exit provisions are proposed?
Currency Could repayment obligations change relative to U.S.-dollar collections? In what currency are contributions, valuations and future payments expressed?

Ask counsel to review all side agreements and proposed conversion features. A short term sheet may omit rights that materially change the analysis. Ask the CPA to model the financial and tax consequences of the actual proposed terms, not a generic debt-versus-equity comparison.

Use an Adviser Question Matrix

Keep distinct questions in separate workstreams. A favorable answer from one adviser does not resolve another adviser’s area.

Map jurisdictions by function: where the practice operates, where each entity is organized, where the funder and its owners are located, where discussions would occur, and where money would originate and arrive. Let advisers determine which connections matter legally.

Document the Funding Source Without Overcollecting Data

Ask counsel and the receiving bank what information they need about the proposed funder and source of funds. Do not assume that every practice has the same investor-verification obligations or that a generic checklist satisfies them.

The requested information may include the funding entity’s legal identity, ownership chain, authorized signatory, originating account and explanation of how the funds were obtained. Have advisers specify the evidence required and the appropriate collection channel.

Pause for review if the proposed sender differs from the named funder, ownership information is incomplete, or payment routing changes without a clear explanation. Administrative convenience is not a reason to bypass questions raised by counsel or the bank.

Keep patient information out of ordinary fundraising materials. Aggregate payer receipts, patient-balance trends, staffing costs and location results generally provide a starting point for financial analysis without patient-level records. If a request involves protected health information, obtain privacy guidance before sharing it. HHS explains that accounting services can involve business-associate responsibilities when PHI is involved.

Separate Fundraising Approval From Moving Money

Three distinctions help prevent false confidence:

A bank transfer is not transaction approval. An account’s ability to receive an international wire does not establish that the underlying ownership, lending or offering arrangement is permissible.

Treaty analysis is not an ownership solution. Ask the tax adviser whether any treaty provision is relevant to the particular parties and payments. Do not assume treaty benefits apply or that they resolve healthcare ownership, control or securities questions.

Organized records are not a legal conclusion. A document folder helps advisers review facts. It does not establish eligibility, satisfy every obligation or replace transaction-specific advice.

Before money moves, obtain an adviser-defined closing checklist identifying required documents, approvals, bank instructions and responsibilities. Confirm the receiving entity and account rather than routing funds through an owner’s personal account for convenience.

Set a Clear Go, Revise or Stop Decision

Maintain an open-issues log with an owner for each question, the information needed and the next decision. Do not let an unresolved item disappear because a funding deadline is approaching.

A useful review ends with:

  1. A summary of the proposal actually assessed.
  2. Unresolved legal, tax, banking and financial questions.
  3. Conditions that must be met before discussions, commitments or closing proceed.
  4. A downside cash forecast and assessment of operational affordability.
  5. A list of changes that require renewed adviser review.

FAQs

Can a U.S. medical practice accept funding from an overseas investor?

There is no universal answer. The relevant jurisdictions, entity, ownership eligibility, professional facts, funding instrument and control rights require legal assessment. Prepare the facts for healthcare and transaction counsel before approaching investors or accepting funds.

Is an overseas loan simpler than selling equity?

Not necessarily. A loan creates repayment obligations and may include security, guarantees, covenants or rights that need legal review. Equity raises different ownership, governance and economic questions. Evaluate each proposal on its actual terms with counsel and a qualified tax adviser.

What financial information should the owner prepare first?

Start with reconciled entity-level financial statements, debt schedules, current cash balances, a capital-use plan and a cash forecast. Add location-level results where relevant, explain shared costs and identify unresolved bookkeeping items. Use aggregate financial information rather than patient-identifiable data.

Does a completed international wire mean the fundraising was approved?

No. Funds movement, ownership eligibility, transaction documentation, securities questions and tax reporting are separate workstreams. Follow an adviser-defined closing process before accepting or moving transaction funds.
