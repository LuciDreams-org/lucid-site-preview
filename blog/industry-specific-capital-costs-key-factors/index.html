Medical Practice Cost of Capital: Key Industry Factors

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A medical practice’s cost of capital is not just the interest rate on a loan. It also reflects financing fees, repayment timing, collateral requirements, owner exposure and the economic expectations attached to money owners contribute.

For a staffed private practice, those costs depend heavily on how reliably collections cover provider and administrative payroll, rent, equipment obligations and existing debt. Two practices with similar reported revenue can receive different proposals because their cash collections, payer mix and dependence on individual providers differ.

The practical goal is to compare the full financing obligation against the cash the practice can reliably generate—not simply select the lowest advertised rate. This guide explains the practice-specific drivers and provides an input sheet for reviewing actual proposals with your CPA and lender.

What Cost of Capital Means for a Medical Practice

For borrowing, distinguish three questions:

  • What does the financing cost? Interest, origination charges, required third-party expenses and other contractual fees.
  • What cash must the practice pay, and when? Principal repayment is not an interest expense, but it is a real demand on cash. Monthly installments, variable payments and balloon payments create different pressures.
  • What exposure or restrictions accompany the money? Liens, personal guarantees, cash requirements and limits on distributions can matter even when they do not appear in the interest rate.

Owner capital has a different economic cost. An owner who contributes funds gives up alternative uses of that money and bears the risk that it will not be recovered. Expectations about distributions, repayment priority and eventual exit should be explicit rather than treated as free funding.

There is no single required return that fits every private practice. A simplified weighted average cost of capital can also obscure important differences among owner funding, guaranteed debt and loans secured by specific assets. For a financing decision, start with the actual cash flows and contractual terms.

Why Financing Costs Differ Across Medical Practices

Collection uncertainty: earned revenue is not available cash

A lender needs to understand the path from services performed to cash received. Gross charges, reported revenue and collectible patient balances are not interchangeable.

Review payer receipts, accounts receivable aging, adjustments and patient collections together. A growing receivable balance may reflect increased activity, slower payments, unresolved denials or balances that are unlikely to be collected. Those explanations imply different repayment risks.

Prepare an aggregate collection history that explains unusual receipts and delays. If a proposed repayment schedule depends on clearing an old backlog, separate that assumption from ordinary ongoing collections. Do not count the same receivable recovery as both recurring revenue and a separate cash release.

Payer concentration: dependable receipts can still be concentrated

A practice may collect consistently while relying heavily on one payer. A payment interruption, reimbursement change or contract disruption affecting that relationship could then have an outsized effect on cash.

Show each major payer’s share of collected revenue, not just billed charges. Explain material changes in the mix and distinguish recurring collections from unusual settlements or adjustments.

The useful question is not whether a particular concentration percentage is universally acceptable. It is whether the practice can continue meeting payroll and financing obligations if a significant source of receipts slows or declines.

Equipment collateral: purchase price is not collateral value

Equipment-backed financing can give a lender a specific asset to secure the obligation. However, specialized equipment may have limited resale demand, costly removal requirements or a useful life that differs from the repayment term.

Ask what assets secure the loan and whether the lien extends beyond the equipment being purchased. Also identify installation, maintenance and replacement costs that the loan does not cover.

Equipment should be evaluated through its expected cash contribution after operating costs, not its projected collections alone. Staff time, supplies, service agreements and space costs may affect whether it supports the required payments.

Lease commitments: fixed costs compete with debt service

Office rent, equipment leases and contractual occupancy obligations reduce the cash available for new borrowing. A lower loan payment does not solve an underlying mismatch between fixed commitments and collections.

For each location, list rent, scheduled increases, equipment lease payments and material remaining commitments. Identify whether the borrower or another related entity holds each agreement.

For a multi-location practice, a profitable consolidated income statement may conceal a location that requires continuing support. Review location-level results using a consistent shared-cost allocation, then reconcile them to entity-level cash and obligations. Allocated profit does not necessarily mean cash is available in the entity that must repay the loan.

Owner guarantees: practice debt can create personal exposure

A personal guarantee can affect a lender’s willingness to extend credit, but it shifts exposure to the owner. A lower quoted rate is not automatically a better outcome if the accompanying guarantee is materially broader.

Ask which owners must guarantee the obligation, whether the guarantee is limited, and what conditions permit release. Also ask about pledged personal assets and obligations that could be triggered by defaults on related financing.

Have legal counsel review the actual documents. The CPA can help assess cash capacity and economic consequences, but a term-sheet summary does not establish the legal scope of a guarantee.

Provider dependence: repayment may rely on a small number of people

A practice with several clinicians may still depend on one provider for a substantial share of collections or contribution margin. An absence or departure can reduce receipts while rent and administrative payroll remain largely unchanged.

Assess provider-level collections alongside compensation and directly associated costs. Gross production alone does not show the cash contribution available for debt service.

Explain how the repayment plan changes if an important provider reduces hours or leaves. Replacement recruitment, credentialing delays and any existing contractual commitments should be discussed as assumptions—not treated as certain solutions.

Financing-Input Sheet: Compare Actual Proposals

Use one column for each proposal. Ask the lender to confirm unclear terms in writing, and identify whether each figure comes from a preliminary quote or final documents. Do not compare a headline rate from one lender with a fully documented repayment schedule from another.

Compare cash flows, not just total dollars

Illustrative example: A practice receives two proposals, each showing $200,000 of principal. Proposal A withholds a $4,000 fee, leaving $196,000 available. Proposal B withholds $1,000, leaving $199,000 available.

The proposals do not provide the same usable funding. If the project needs $200,000, the practice must identify how it will cover either shortfall. Then compare the dated payments, other charges and outstanding balances—not just the interest rates.

Ask your CPA to calculate an annualized financing cost from actual net proceeds and scheduled payments when appropriate. The calculation should state its assumptions, particularly for variable rates or optional early repayment. It does not capture every consequence of guarantees, collateral or operating restrictions, so retain those as separate decision factors.

Test Repayment Against Practice Cash Flow

Build a cash forecast that starts with expected collections, rather than charges or accounting profit alone. Deduct provider and administrative payroll, occupancy, supplies, other operating payments, existing debt service and the proposed financing payments. Show owner distributions separately so their effect is visible.

Then test specific vulnerabilities:

  • Delayed collections: Move affected receipts into later periods rather than automatically treating them as permanently lost.
  • Lower collections: Reduce receipts where there is a plausible decline in activity or reimbursement, without assuming all expenses fall proportionately.
  • Provider disruption: Adjust both collections and the costs that would actually change.
  • Variable-rate exposure: Apply alternative rates to the contractual payment calculation rather than assuming today’s payment continues.

Illustrative example: A practice forecasts $42,000 of cash available for debt service after operating payments and before owner distributions. Existing and proposed loan payments total $30,000, leaving $12,000. If $20,000 of expected payer receipts moves into the following month, that month has an $8,000 shortfall before distributions.

This is not an industry benchmark or a lending standard. It demonstrates why an apparently affordable obligation can still require a cash reserve. Review the full sequence of months: a later collection recovery does not pay an earlier payroll obligation.

Bring a Reconciled Package to Your CPA and Lender

Prepare current financial statements, reconciled bank balances, receivable summaries, debt and lease schedules, and the completed proposal sheet. For multiple entities, show intercompany balances and explain how shared costs are allocated. Do not assume cash can move freely among entities to cover an obligation.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete business records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those practices also provide a clearer foundation for a financing review; they do not guarantee approval or a lower rate.

Use aggregate payer and provider financial summaries wherever possible. Avoid including patient-identifiable information in ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. If a review requires patient information, establish an appropriate handling process with qualified advisers before sharing it.

Make the Decision on Cost, Capacity and Exposure

A useful financing recommendation should explain three things: the cash cost of the proposal, the practice’s ability to make payments under credible collection scenarios, and the assets or owner interests exposed if the plan fails.

There may be no proposal that wins on every dimension. Document why the chosen combination of usable proceeds, payment structure, fees and security requirements fits the practice—and which assumptions must hold for it to remain manageable.

FAQs

Why can two medical practices receive different financing terms?

Similar revenue does not mean similar repayment risk. Collection reliability, payer concentration, provider dependence, equipment collateral, existing leases and owner guarantees can differ substantially. Lenders also have different underwriting policies. Compare written proposals rather than assuming a single medical-practice rate applies.

Is the lowest interest rate always the least expensive option?

No. Fees, net proceeds, repayment frequency, maturity and early-payoff terms affect the financing cost. Collateral, guarantees and restrictions add exposure that an interest-rate comparison cannot fully express. Review both the payment calculation and the contractual obligations.

Should owner contributions be treated as free capital?

No. Owners commit funds that could be used elsewhere and accept the possibility of loss. Document whether funds are intended as equity or an owner loan, along with repayment or distribution expectations. Have the CPA and legal adviser review the treatment; do not assign a universal required return or assume tax consequences.

What should a practice ask its CPA before accepting a proposal?

Ask whether the books support the collection assumptions, whether the proposed payments remain manageable under realistic downside scenarios, and how fees affect usable funding and financing cost. For multi-location or multi-entity practices, also ask whether the borrowing entity has access to the cash expected to repay the obligation.
