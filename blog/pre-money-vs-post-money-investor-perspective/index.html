Pre-Money vs. Post-Money Valuation for Medical Practices

November 4, 2026 · 9 min read

An investor’s proposed valuation does not tell you, by itself, how much ownership you would give up—or how much cash your medical practice would receive. You also need to know whether the number is pre-money or post-money, which entity is being valued, and whether the investor is funding the business or buying an existing owner’s interest.

For a staffed private medical practice, those distinctions can affect the money available for provider and administrative payroll, a new location, equipment or a working-capital reserve. A payment to a departing owner does not fund those needs.

This article explains how to read the valuation arithmetic in a proposed term sheet. It does not determine fair market value, recommend an ownership structure or establish that a transaction is permissible. Have healthcare counsel assess the proposed arrangement before treating an investor’s offer as executable.

What Pre-Money and Post-Money Mean

Pre-money valuation is the negotiated equity value of the specified business immediately before new equity capital enters it.

Post-money valuation is that equity value immediately after the new capital enters, under the transaction’s stated assumptions.

In a simple transaction where an investor buys newly issued equity, all interests have equivalent economic rights, and no other ownership changes occur:

Calculation Formula
Post-money equity value Pre-money equity value + new primary equity capital
Pre-money equity value Post-money equity value − new primary equity capital
Investor’s ownership from the new issuance New primary equity capital ÷ post-money equity value
Existing owners’ combined remaining ownership Pre-money equity value ÷ post-money equity value

These are pricing relationships, not a promise about the practice’s future sale price or cash balance. The documents must define what the investor receives and what is included in the ownership denominator.

First confirm what is being valued

A medical practice owner may have interests in a professional entity, a separate management business, a real-estate entity or several location-level entities. A valuation for one is not automatically a valuation for all of them.

Ask the investor to identify:

  • The exact entity whose interests would be issued or transferred.
  • The assets, liabilities and operations included in the valuation.
  • Whether every location is included.
  • Whether the quoted figure is equity value or enterprise value.
  • Whether cash, debt and working-capital adjustments are included or handled separately.

Enterprise value and equity value are not interchangeable. If the offer starts with enterprise value, request a written bridge to equity value showing the treatment of debt, cash and other negotiated adjustments. Do not divide an equity investment by an enterprise-value headline and assume the result is the investor’s ownership.

A Hypothetical Term-Sheet Comparison

Illustrative example only: A private medical practice receives three proposed offers. Each involves a $2 million total investor payment. Assume the same entity, equivalent ownership rights, no outstanding convertible instruments and no other equity issuances. In Offer C, the investor buys the secondary interest at the same per-unit price as the primary investment.

Term Offer A: Pre-money quote Offer B: Post-money quote Offer C: Mixed primary and secondary
Headline valuation $8 million pre-money $8 million post-money $8 million pre-money
New capital paid into the practice $2 million $2 million $1 million
Payment to an existing owner None None $1 million
Implied pre-money equity value $8 million $6 million $8 million
Post-money equity value $10 million $8 million $9 million
Investor’s total ownership after closing 20% 25% Approximately 22.22%
Gross cash received by the practice $2 million $2 million $1 million

Offers A and B use the same headline number but produce different ownership results. In Offer A, the investor’s $2 million is divided by a $10 million post-money value. In Offer B, it is divided by an $8 million post-money value.

Offer C needs a separate calculation. The $1 million primary investment buys approximately 11.11% of the post-closing equity. The $1 million secondary purchase, at the assumed same per-unit price, transfers another approximately 11.11% from an existing owner. The investor ends with approximately 22.22%, but only $1 million enters the practice.

These offers are not interchangeable. Comparing them requires looking at ownership, cash destination and contractual rights—not just the headline valuation.

Separate New Capital From Owner Proceeds

Primary capital is money paid to the entity for newly issued equity. Secondary proceeds are money paid to an existing holder for an existing interest.

That distinction matters when an owner says, “The investor is putting money into the practice.” Some or all of the payment may instead be buying out an owner.

Before discussing price, request a sources-and-uses schedule that separates:

Cash destination or use Question for the owner
Practice operating account How much new funding will actually reach this entity?
Selling owner How much is purchase consideration rather than practice funding?
Debt repayment Which obligations will be repaid, and by whom?
Transaction fees Which party pays each cost?
Escrow or holdback What proceeds are unavailable at closing, and under what release terms?
Planned operating uses What remains for payroll, equipment, location costs and reserves?

A secondary purchase alone does not add capital to the practice. In a mixed transaction, do not use the investor’s total check as the numerator in the simple primary-investment formula. Model the new issuance and the transfer separately, using their actual prices and terms.

Why Fees and Other Terms Change the Economics

The simple valuation formula does not explain every dollar an owner or practice receives.

Fees reduce usable cash without necessarily changing ownership

Illustrative example only: Under Offer A above, assume the practice receives $2 million and pays $100,000 of transaction expenses after closing. Usable proceeds would be $1.9 million before any other uses.

If the agreed equity pricing remains unchanged, that expense does not automatically recalculate the investor’s 20% stake. It reduces available cash. The agreement must specify whether expenses instead change the purchase price, capital contribution or another closing adjustment.

Keep two calculations separate:

  1. Ownership pricing: What interest does the investor receive?
  2. Net funding: What cash remains after fees, repayments and other closing uses?

Ownership percentage does not describe every right

An investor with a minority economic interest may still negotiate approval rights over budgets, borrowing, distributions or future transactions. Different equity classes may also have different distribution or sale-proceeds rights.

Ask advisers to identify terms that affect:

  • Voting and management authority.
  • Distribution priorities and preferred returns.
  • Sale proceeds, redemption rights or buyback obligations.
  • Future issuance rights and possible dilution.
  • Required owner employment or compensation arrangements.
  • Contingent payments, earnouts and performance conditions.

A post-money label alone does not lock every future ownership outcome. Additional issuances, conversions and other contractual provisions can change the result.

Build a Practice-Specific Financial Review Packet

You do not need to build a full transaction administration system to evaluate an initial valuation proposal. You do need reliable financial information for the entity being discussed.

Financial information Why it matters to the discussion
Reconciled entity-level balance sheet and P&L Establishes the financial starting point and identifies obligations
Per-location P&Ls Shows whether consolidated results conceal materially different location performance
Shared-cost allocation schedule Explains how administrative payroll, rent and other common costs are assigned
Payer receipts and patient-balance summaries Helps explain collection timing and differences between reported activity and cash receipts
Provider and administrative compensation Helps advisers assess current earnings and proposed compensation changes
Debt and owner-related balances Identifies items that may affect the equity-value bridge or closing uses
Cash forecast Tests whether net proceeds support the stated operating plan

Distinguish billed charges, recognized revenue and collected cash. Also distinguish owner distributions from compensation and other expenses. A proposed adjustment to earnings should have a documented rationale rather than simply making the valuation look stronger.

The IRS guide to starting and keeping business records supports maintaining complete records, separate business accounts and bank reconciliations. Those basics help make the financial packet traceable; they do not establish a transaction valuation.

Use aggregate financial summaries wherever practical. Do not place patient-identifiable information in an ordinary Slack discussion or a general-purpose AI tool to explain a valuation issue.

Questions for Your Advisers

Valuation arithmetic, accounting analysis and legal permissibility are separate workstreams. Bring the same proposed terms to each adviser so their conclusions address the same transaction.

Ask healthcare counsel

  • Can this investor own the proposed interest in this entity under the applicable requirements?
  • Do the proposed governance, management, compensation or referral-related arrangements raise concerns?
  • Are any approvals, consents or contractual restrictions relevant before proceeding?
  • Do the ownership percentage and the investor’s control rights tell different stories?
  • Which provisions should change before the owner signs anything, including preliminary commitments?

A correct percentage calculation is not evidence that the arrangement is lawful.

Ask your CPA

  • Do the financial statements reconcile to the underlying records?
  • Which proposed earnings adjustments are supportable?
  • How much cash goes to the entity versus selling owners?
  • What is the expected cash remaining after transaction costs, debt repayment and other uses?
  • What tax consequences should the practice and individual owners evaluate under the proposed structure?

Do not assume primary funding and owner sale proceeds receive identical accounting or tax treatment.

Ask a qualified valuation professional

  • What exactly is being valued, as of what date and for what purpose?
  • What standard of value and valuation methods are appropriate?
  • How are compensation, shared costs, debt and working capital treated?
  • How do the rights attached to the proposed interest affect its value?
  • What additional analysis is needed to assess fair market value where relevant?

Negotiated pre-money and post-money figures do not, by themselves, establish fair market value. A term sheet communicates proposed pricing; a professional valuation analysis addresses a defined valuation question.

A Practical Review Sequence Before Negotiating

  1. Identify the entity and valuation basis. Resolve whether the quote is equity value or enterprise value.
  2. Label the headline clearly. Write “pre-money” or “post-money” beside the amount.
  3. Separate the payments. Identify primary capital, secondary proceeds and any other consideration.
  4. Recalculate ownership. Confirm the denominator, equity classes and all concurrent ownership changes.
  5. Bridge gross proceeds to usable cash. Include fees, debt payments and holdbacks.
  6. Review rights and restrictions. Do not negotiate percentage ownership in isolation.
  7. Obtain adviser review. Resolve legal, valuation, accounting and tax questions before committing.

This is a workflow for interpreting an offer—not a guide to maintaining ownership records across transactions or choosing between debt and equity financing.

FAQs

Is a higher pre-money valuation always a better offer for a medical practice owner?

Not necessarily. With the same primary investment and otherwise identical terms, a higher pre-money valuation means less ownership is issued to the investor. But fees, control rights, distribution preferences, contingent payments and owner obligations can change the overall economics. Compare complete terms and net proceeds.

Does money paid to a selling physician increase post-money value?

Not as new primary capital. A payment for an existing interest goes to the selling holder rather than the practice. In a mixed transaction, calculate the primary issuance and secondary transfer separately. The secondary price may also differ from the primary price.

Can I use the proposed post-money valuation as proof of fair market value?

No. It is a negotiated pricing figure under stated assumptions. It does not independently establish fair market value or determine whether the proposed ownership arrangement is permissible. Ask a qualified valuation professional and healthcare counsel what analysis the transaction requires.

What should I request if a term sheet only states a valuation and investment amount?

Request the valuation basis, named entity, pre-money or post-money designation, primary-versus-secondary split, resulting ownership, rights attached to the interest and a sources-and-uses schedule. Without those details, you cannot reliably determine either dilution or the cash available to the practice.
