PCI DSS Basics for Medical Practice Owners

November 3, 2026 · 9 min read

A private medical practice may accept cards at the front desk, take payments by phone, offer an online patient-payment portal, and maintain card-on-file arrangements. Those workflows can involve different systems, vendors, and staff responsibilities—even when the money ultimately reaches the same bank account.

PCI DSS responsibilities depend on how card information actually moves through the practice, not simply on which payment company it uses. For an owner, the starting point is to document those workflows, clarify who is responsible for each part, and confirm validation requirements with the acquiring bank or a qualified PCI specialist.

This guide addresses domestic U.S. practice card-payment workflows. It is a planning aid, not a compliance determination, self-assessment eligibility guide, or technical implementation checklist.

What PCI DSS Means for a Medical Practice

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, or PCI DSS, establishes security requirements for protecting payment account data. The PCI Security Standards Council maintains the standard and related resources.

For a practice owner, three distinctions matter:

  • Security responsibilities: What the practice and its providers must do to protect payment account data.
  • Scope: Which people, processes, systems, and connections are relevant to the PCI DSS assessment. A qualified specialist should evaluate this; an owner’s inventory is only a starting point.
  • Validation: How the practice must demonstrate that applicable requirements are met. Confirm the required documentation, submission process, and timing with the acquiring bank or its designated compliance contact.

A payment provider’s compliance documentation does not establish that your entire practice is compliant. Its documentation may cover only particular services, configurations, or responsibilities. Similarly, a terminal, hosted portal, or tokenization feature does not by itself determine which validation route applies.

PCI DSS and HIPAA are separate questions

PCI DSS concerns payment account data. HIPAA addresses protected health information and the responsibilities of covered entities and business associates. Meeting one framework does not establish that the other has been satisfied.

The HHS overview of the HIPAA Privacy Rule provides context for those separate responsibilities. Questions about a particular vendor’s HIPAA role require their own review; a PCI statement is not a substitute.

For the planning artifacts below, record workflow descriptions and vendor roles—not patient names, account balances tied to individuals, card numbers, security codes, or screenshots of actual transactions.

Start With a Card-Payment Flow Inventory

Ask the practice manager to walk through every way staff accept or retrieve a card payment. Include each location and legal entity, rather than assuming the main office represents the whole practice.

Trace two related but different paths:

  1. The information path: Where card details are entered, handled, transmitted, or potentially stored.
  2. The money path: Which merchant arrangement receives the transaction, which entity owns it, and where settlements, fees, refunds, and disputes appear.

The information path supports the PCI discussion. The money path supports financial accuracy. One cannot substitute for the other.

Inventory template

Use one row per distinct workflow and repeat rows when locations or entities use different arrangements. The examples below are prompts, not statements about your practice’s setup.

Payment workflow What to document without sensitive information Questions to resolve
Front-desk terminal Location and entity aliases, terminal model, provider, staff role using it, settlement-account alias Does card information enter any workstation or other system? Who supports the device?
Telephone payment Whether staff enter details directly into a terminal or virtual payment interface; whether calls are recorded Could recordings, notes, or other systems capture card details? What workflow has been approved?
Online payment portal Portal provider, how patients reach the payment page, and which practice systems connect to it Is payment entry hosted elsewhere, embedded, or handled another way? Who can confirm the actual design?

Use aliases such as “North Office—Terminal A” or “Entity B—Settlement Account,” rather than full bank or merchant account numbers. Mark uncertain answers as unknown and assign someone to obtain confirmation. Do not fill gaps with assumptions.

Look beyond the usual payment screen

The normal transaction may be straightforward while exceptions create uncertainty. Ask staff about handwritten messages, scanned forms, call recordings, shared folders, email, and vendor support requests. The purpose is to identify possible handling or storage locations—not to gather examples containing real card information.

If staff report that sensitive payment data may be present somewhere unexpected, escalate through the practice’s approved security and payment-provider contacts. Do not copy it into the inventory, forward it through ordinary messaging, or attempt an improvised cleanup that could interfere with incident handling.

Identify Who Does What

Several organizations may participate in one payment flow. Product names alone do not explain their responsibilities, and one provider may perform several roles.

Party Role to clarify Confirmation to request
Practice owner or manager Oversees payment workflows, vendor decisions, and internal accountability Named internal owner for each workflow and unresolved question
Acquiring bank or designated merchant-services contact Explains the practice’s merchant validation obligations and submission route Required validation approach, documents, timing, and escalation contact
Processor or gateway Supports transaction processing or routing Services provided, applicable compliance documentation, and division of responsibilities
Portal or practice-system vendor Provides the patient-facing interface or connection to payment services Where payment entry occurs and which components the vendor manages
IT provider Supports relevant devices, networks, accounts, or connections Which systems it manages and which tasks fall outside its agreement
Qualified Security Assessor or other qualified PCI specialist Evaluates PCI questions within an agreed engagement Scope of advice, findings, and issues needing confirmation with the acquirer

Request documentation relevant to the specific service your practice uses. A broad statement that a vendor is “PCI compliant” leaves important questions unanswered: Which service is covered? Does the documentation match the deployed arrangement? Which responsibilities remain with the practice?

Record who supplied the answer, when it was supplied, and what remains unresolved. Keep detailed technical documentation in the appropriate controlled location rather than attaching it indiscriminately to finance workpapers.

An Adviser Question Sheet Based on Your Actual Setup

Bring the completed inventory to the acquiring bank’s designated contact and, where needed, a qualified PCI specialist. This produces a more useful discussion than asking only, “Are we compliant?”

Use the following sheet without adding card details or patient-identifiable information.

Workflow accuracy Does our inventory accurately describe each payment channel and its connections? Corrections, unanswered questions, and responsible person
Assessment scope Which systems, people, processes, and service relationships need further evaluation? Adviser’s scope explanation and supporting documentation

Do not choose a questionnaire from a marketing description

A Self-Assessment Questionnaire, or SAQ, is a validation document used in certain circumstances. Eligibility depends on the actual payment environment and the applicable criteria—not merely on practice size or a vendor’s sales description.

Statements such as “we outsource payments,” “we only use a terminal,” or “we never save card numbers” are not enough to establish eligibility. Describe every channel and exception, then obtain confirmation of the appropriate validation route. Do not select a questionnaire because it appears shorter or easier.

Connect Payment Oversight to Financial Accuracy

PCI planning should not turn the accounting file into a repository for card data. Finance generally needs settlement reports, fees, refunds, dispute adjustments, and bank activity—not full card details.

The IRS guidance on business records supports maintaining complete records and reconciling bank activity. For a practice, apply that discipline to the settlement process while keeping the PCI assessment separate.

Reconcile the money without collecting sensitive details

For each merchant arrangement, identify:

  • The legal entity and location associated with the activity.
  • The bank account receiving settlements, identified by an internal alias in shared workpapers.
  • Whether fees are deducted from deposits or charged separately.
  • How refunds, disputes, and timing differences appear in reports.
  • Who investigates unmatched deposits and approves financial adjustments.

Keep payer receipts separate from patient card-payment activity when tracing these flows. An insurance payment arriving in the same operating account does not make it part of the card-payment workflow.

Where locations share a merchant arrangement, document how finance assigns receipts and related costs to location-level reporting. Where entities have separate arrangements, confirm that the settlement mapping reflects those entities. These are accounting controls, not proof of PCI compliance.

Budget from confirmed responsibilities

Request itemized proposals for specialist advice, required validation work, payment-service changes, and any identified remediation. Separate recurring expenses from one-time work. Do not assume a universal compliance cost or that a provider change will automatically reduce obligations.

Keep the Inventory Useful as the Practice Changes

Assign a manager to maintain the inventory and coordinate follow-up. Revisit it when the practice adds a location, changes a portal or processor, introduces card-on-file payments, alters telephone handling, or changes relevant IT arrangements.

Ask the acquiring bank or qualified adviser whether a proposed change affects scope or validation before treating the old answers as applicable. Maintain a follow-up register with the issue, decision-maker, next action, and status.

The owner’s immediate goal is practical: know how payments work, know who can answer the open questions, and fund the work that has actually been identified. An inventory is the starting point—not a compliance certificate.

FAQs

Does using a third-party payment provider remove our practice’s PCI DSS responsibilities?

No. Outsourcing can change the responsibilities and systems involved, but it does not establish that the practice has no remaining obligations. Confirm the provider’s service coverage, the practice’s responsibilities, and the required validation with the acquiring bank or qualified specialist.

Can we determine our SAQ from this article?

No. This article does not establish SAQ eligibility. Use the inventory to describe your actual setup, including phone payments, online payments, stored credentials, and exceptions. Ask the acquiring bank or its designated contact to confirm the appropriate validation route, with specialist input where needed.

Should we include transaction screenshots in the inventory?

No. Use workflow descriptions, system names, aliases, and staff roles. Do not include actual card details or patient-identifiable information. If an adviser needs supporting evidence, agree on its purpose and an appropriate handling method separately.

Does each office need a separate PCI review?

Do not assume that one office’s documentation covers every location or entity—or that every office necessarily requires a separate submission. Map the differences in merchant arrangements, systems, and workflows, then confirm how those differences affect scope and validation with the acquiring bank and qualified adviser.
