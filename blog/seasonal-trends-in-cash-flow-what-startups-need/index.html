Identifying Seasonal Cash Flow Patterns in Medical Practices

November 3, 2026 · 10 min read

A low-cash month does not automatically mean your medical practice has a seasonal slowdown. Payer deposits may have crossed a month-end boundary, provider and administrative payroll may have included an additional pay date, or an equipment purchase may have reduced cash without changing everyday operations.

Before using a seasonal assumption in a financial plan, establish what actually repeats—and why. The goal is to distinguish recurring changes in practice activity from collection timing, payment calendars, isolated transactions and changes in the business itself.

This article walks through that diagnosis. The output is a documented pattern with supporting evidence, not a reserve target or a forward cash-flow forecast.

What Counts as a Seasonal Cash Flow Pattern?

Seasonality is a recurring calendar-related pattern in receipts or disbursements. It is not simply a month that performed differently from the previous month.

For a staffed private practice, separate these explanations:

Explanation What you might observe Evidence to check
Recurring operating activity Service activity changes during a similar period across years Completed encounters, provider sessions and practice closure calendars
Collection timing Deposits fall while service activity remains relatively steady Receipt dates, aggregate payment batches and collection-lag reports
Calendar-driven disbursements Cash outflows rise because more payroll payments fall in a month Actual payroll funding dates and amounts
Scheduled annual payments A recurring payment creates a cash trough Vendor invoices, renewal schedules and bank transactions
One-time event A purchase or unusual receipt changes one period Purchase records, settlement details or transaction documentation
Structural change Results shift after a location, provider or payer arrangement changes Opening dates, staffing records and effective dates of changes

A recurring annual expense can create a genuine cash pattern without indicating weaker practice demand. Conversely, a repeating deposit delay may explain the bank balance without showing any reduction in services delivered.

Do not assume that another specialty—or another practice in the same specialty—shares your pattern.

1. Build a Comparable Historical Record

Start with complete, reconciled periods

Use several complete years when available. Two full years provide an initial comparison, but repetition across only two observations is still limited evidence. A newer practice can investigate possible patterns while clearly labeling them provisional.

Gather:

  • Reconciled bank activity for all accounts within the review scope.
  • Payer receipts and patient collections, separated where practical.
  • Payroll funding reports covering providers and administrative staff.
  • Operating payments, refunds, financing movements and owner transactions.
  • Aggregate service-activity reports and relevant operating calendars.
  • A dated list of location, staffing, billing-process and payer changes.

The IRS guide to starting a business and keeping records supports maintaining complete records, keeping business accounts separate and reconciling bank records. Those practices also create a more reliable foundation for this analysis.

Do not fill missing months with averages and then treat the resulting pattern as observed history. Resolve the gap or mark the period incomplete.

Define what each measure means

Use deposit or settlement dates consistently for cash receipts. Use actual withdrawal dates for disbursements. Service activity belongs in a separate series based on when services occurred.

Keep gross charges out of the cash-collections line. They are not interchangeable with deposits. Likewise, an accrual-basis P&L is useful context, but it does not substitute for a cash history.

If payment processors settle patient collections net of fees, document that treatment and use it consistently. Explain any reconciliation differences rather than forcing unlike reports to agree.

Keep the practice boundary stable

For a multi-location practice, compare like with like. A newly opened office can make total collections rise even when established locations are unchanged.

Review both the total practice and comparable locations that operated throughout the comparison period. Keep entity-level accounts distinct, and eliminate transfers between included accounts when viewing consolidated cash. A transfer is not a new payer receipt or an operating expense.

Shared costs need consistent treatment. If one entity pays a shared administrative bill, a later reimbursement can distort entity-level cash patterns. Keep that cash movement visible while using a consistent allocation method for location-level expense comparisons.

2. Separate Service Activity from Deposit Timing

A deposit received this month may relate to services delivered earlier. Comparing monthly deposits alone can therefore assign the apparent slowdown to the wrong period.

Build a companion view:

Measure What it helps explain Important limitation
Completed encounters or provider sessions Whether operating activity changed Counts alone do not capture service or payer mix
Payer receipts by deposit month When payer cash reached the practice Deposits may relate to earlier service periods
Patient collections by receipt month When patient payments were collected Payments may include older patient balances
Aggregate receivables aging Whether unpaid balances are accumulating Aging changes require investigation, not a seasonal label
Collection timing by service period, where available Whether the lag between service and receipt changed Reports must use consistent definitions

Investigate whether a weak month is followed by a compensating deposit increase. Examine weekly activity around month-end, bank holidays and practice closures. A payment batch moving into the next month can create an apparent trough and rebound without changing the combined cash received across those periods.

Use aggregate financial and operational reports wherever possible. Do not place patient-identifiable information into ordinary Slack messages or general-purpose AI tools. The HHS minimum necessary guidance is relevant to limiting information use where that standard applies; the appropriate workflow depends on the data and parties involved.

3. Explain Disbursements Before Calling a Month “Slow”

Payroll calendars can make otherwise similar months look very different. Compare the number and size of actual funding events, including separately funded payroll taxes and benefits where applicable. An additional biweekly payroll date is a calendar effect, but it does not necessarily recur in the same month each year.

Also identify annual renewals, equipment purchases, owner distributions, debt proceeds and principal repayments. Keep them in the actual cash record, but distinguish them from recurring operating payments.

Maintain two views:

  1. Actual cash movement: what entered and left the accounts.
  2. An explained comparison: how identifiable timing differences and unusual transactions affected the apparent pattern.

Never overwrite the first view with the second. An adjustment should have a named transaction, supporting record and explanation. If you cannot substantiate it, leave it unresolved.

5. Test the Pattern Against Competing Explanations

Compare consistent calendar periods

Start with the same months across years rather than only consecutive months. Then inspect weekly detail around the suspected pattern.

Check the number of open days and provider sessions. Collections per open day can add context, but they are not a direct productivity measure when deposits relate to earlier services. Keep both the raw cash totals and the contextual measure.

Separate growth from seasonality

A practice adding providers may collect more in every month. That is growth, not necessarily a seasonal change.

Compare established locations separately and mark changes in provider capacity, payer mix, payment terms or collection processes. A recurring monthly pattern may no longer be comparable after a major operational change.

Look for evidence that contradicts the explanation

Ask what you would expect to see if the proposed cause were true:

  • If fewer operating days explain lower activity, do the session and encounter reports agree?
  • If deposits were delayed, can the later receipts be traced to the affected payment batches?
  • If an annual payment explains the trough, does its amount explain the cash difference?
  • If service activity is stable but receivables are worsening, is this a collection issue rather than seasonality?

A pattern becomes more credible when it repeats, has a documented mechanism and survives these alternative explanations.

Finish with a Diagnosis, Not a Forecast

The owner and finance team should leave the review with a clear statement of:

  • Which receipt and payment patterns repeat.
  • Whether each relates to activity, timing, scheduled costs or structural change.
  • Which locations and entities the conclusion covers.
  • What evidence supports it and what remains uncertain.

Keep unsupported explanations out of the seasonal assumptions used later in planning. Reserve sizing, borrowing decisions and forward projections are separate steps that depend on this diagnosis.

FAQs

How much history does a medical practice need to identify seasonality?

Several complete years are useful when available because they allow repeated comparisons. Two years can reveal a candidate pattern, but neither two matching months nor a longer incomplete dataset proves seasonality. Document gaps, practice changes and the evidence behind each conclusion. Treat findings from limited history as provisional.

Can cash flow be seasonal even when service activity is steady?

Yes. Recurring annual payments or recurring collection-timing effects can create a cash pattern without a corresponding change in service activity. Verify the payment schedule or deposit evidence, and describe the cause accurately rather than calling it a demand slowdown.

Should unusual transactions be removed from the historical record?

No. Preserve actual cash movement and create a separate, clearly explained comparison when necessary. Identify equipment purchases, financing, owner transactions and other exceptions so they do not obscure recurring operations. Every adjustment should trace back to supporting records.

What if a suspected seasonal dip turns out to be a collection problem?

Classify it as a collection issue and investigate the underlying timing or process change with the appropriate practice team. Do not normalize an unexplained delay merely because it occurred in a familiar month. Reassess the seasonal conclusion after the discrepancy is explained.
