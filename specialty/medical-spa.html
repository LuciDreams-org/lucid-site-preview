Why bookkeeping for medical spas is different

A general bookkeeper can record your deposits and pay your bills. A med spa has three money problems that most small businesses don't have:

  1. Memberships, packages and gift cards: clients pay you before you do the work.
  2. Injectables, retail and inventory: you buy product in bulk and use it one treatment at a time.
  3. Provider pay and medical director fees: commissions, contractors versus employees, and a monthly fee for your medical director.

If your bookkeeper doesn't handle all three, your monthly profit numbers will be wrong, even when every deposit is recorded.

Memberships and packages

Say a client pays you $1,800 in March for a package of six laser sessions. If your bookkeeper counts the full $1,800 as March income, March looks like a great month. Then in April, May and June your staff does the sessions, you pay your provider and you use the room, and those months look like losses.

You didn't make $1,800 in March. You took $1,800 for work you still owe. Until you do each treatment, you owe that client either the session or a refund. Accountants call this deferred revenue. It means you count the income when you do the treatment, not when you charge the card.

The same rule applies to:

  • Monthly memberships where clients bank unused credits
  • Prepaid series of injectables, facials or laser treatments
  • Gift cards, which you owe until someone redeems them
  • Deposits for treatments booked for a later date

If you skip this step, you'll run into three problems:

  • You can't tell how much you really made last month, because a few big package sales can hide a slow month.
  • You may spend cash that you still owe in treatments.
  • If you ever sell the practice or bring in a partner, the buyer will ask how much you owe in unused sessions and gift cards. If you can't answer from your books, you'll lose time or money in the deal.

How deferred revenue affects your taxes depends on the accounting method you use, so ask your CPA which method fits your practice.

Injectables, retail and inventory

Neurotoxin, filler and skincare are some of your biggest costs. You buy them in bulk, often on wholesale accounts with the manufacturer or a distributor, and you use them one syringe or one unit at a time.

If your bookkeeper records every product order as an expense on the day you pay for it, your margins will swing with your ordering schedule. The month you stock up on filler looks bad. The next month looks great. Neither number tells you whether your filler treatments make money.

Product you buy on a wholesale account is inventory until you use it. Each time a provider treats a client, your bookkeeper should match the cost of the units or syringes used to that treatment. Then you can see the real margin on a lip filler appointment, a neurotoxin visit or a chemical peel, after product cost and provider pay.

Retail skincare adds a second problem. In many states, you have to charge sales tax on products you sell over the counter, while treatments often aren't taxed. Rules vary by state, so check your state's rules. Either way, you'll want retail sales recorded apart from treatment sales, so you charge and pay the right sales tax and can see how much you make on retail by itself.

Your books should let you answer questions like these:

  • How much did we spend on neurotoxin this quarter, and how many units did we bill?
  • What's our margin on filler after product cost and injector pay?
  • How much do we make on retail, and how much sales tax do we owe on it?

Provider pay and medical director fees

How you pay your injectors and your medical director affects both your profit and your tax risk.

Many med spas pay injectors a commission or a share of treatment revenue. Some pay a base wage plus commission. Some pay providers as 1099 contractors. Depending on your state, you may also need a medical director, who you'll usually pay a flat monthly fee.

Here's where owners get into trouble:

  • Contractor or employee. The IRS looks at who controls how, when and where the work is done. If you set the schedule, supply the room and the product, and set the prices, the IRS may treat that provider as an employee. Paying someone a commission doesn't make them a contractor. If you get this wrong, you can owe back payroll taxes and penalties.
  • Commissions in one lump. If your bookkeeper puts every commission in one payroll line, you can't tell which providers bring in more than they cost you.
  • Medical director fees mixed in with other costs. Keep this fee on its own line, so you know what supervision costs you each month and can plan for it when you add a location.

Ask a CPA to review how you classify each provider, especially before you hire your next one.
