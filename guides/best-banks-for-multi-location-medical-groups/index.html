Short answer

For a larger group that wants payer remittances matched to deposits, J.P. Morgan (Healthcare Link) and PNC (National Lockbox Network) have the deepest healthcare treasury tools, with Bank of America, Huntington and Webster Bank as strong regional or national options. Panacea Financial stands out for MSOs and DSOs, with free zero balance accounts with sweeps and insured cash sweep coverage up to $125 million per tax ID. Lemma and Mercury are fintech platforms, not banks, that give each entity its own accounts under one login at low published prices, so pick the option whose lockbox, sweep and permission setup matches how your entities and locations really move money.

What a group with several locations needs from a bank

A single practice can live with one checking account. A group with five clinics, two professional corporations and a management company cannot. These are the treasury features that matter.

  1. Accounts by entity and location. Each legal entity needs its own accounts. Many groups also want an account or sub-account per location so deposits show where revenue came from.
  2. One login, scoped permissions. Owners should see everything. An office manager should see one location. Approval rules for wires and ACH stop one person from moving money alone.
  3. Lockbox and payer deposit handling. Insurance checks and EOBs go to a lockbox address. Better lockboxes also match each deposit to its remittance (the 835 file), so billing staff post faster.
  4. Sweeps and zero balance accounts. A zero balance account (ZBA) moves each location's balance to a main account every day. A sweep moves excess cash into an insured or interest earning product.
  5. Positive pay. The bank only clears checks you have already issued. This blocks the check fraud that hits practices paying many vendors.
  6. Deposit insurance. FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each ownership category. Insured cash sweep programs spread deposits across many banks to raise that limit.
  7. Clean feeds. Your accounting team needs a feed or statement for every account. More accounts help only if every one is reconciled.

J.P. Morgan (Chase)

J.P. Morgan serves healthcare providers through its commercial bank and Chase Business Bank. Its InstaMed unit is part of J.P. Morgan Healthcare Payments, which it describes as an end to end suite of payments, treasury and banking for healthcare organizations of all sizes. Its Healthcare Link service gives access to incoming check and electronic payments and "matches dollars to remittances", with inbound EOBs and 835 files visible in one report area.

Best for: larger groups with high payer volume that want lockbox deposits matched to remittances before billing staff touch them. Limits: pricing is quoted, not published. Smaller groups may be served by Chase Business Banking rather than the commercial team, so ask which tools come with your relationship.

PNC

PNC's treasury page for healthcare providers lists a National Lockbox Network (retail and wholesale), patient accounting system integration, consolidated payment reconciliation for check, cash and electronic payments, remote deposit, Zelle patient refunds, commercial cards, ACH and wires, and check issuance with Positive Pay. Reporting runs through its PINACLE platform. PNC says this team serves companies over $10 million in annual revenue. Its Healthcare Business Banking team serves those under $10 million, with collections, disbursement, excess cash investing and online reporting tools.

Best for: groups that want one bank from a small business team up to corporate healthcare treasury as they add locations. Limits: no treasury fees are published.

Bank of America

Bank of America's Healthcare Payment Solutions gives providers one dashboard for patient payments and payment devices, and its platform "integrates into the largest Electronic Medical Record (EMR) solutions in the U.S." Its commercial site splits clients by size: Business Banking for companies with $1 million to $50 million in revenue and Global Commercial Banking for $50 million to $2 billion. Its practice lending and Preferred Rewards rate discounts are covered in our best banks for medical practices guide.

Best for: groups that want front desk payments and banking at one national bank, especially if they borrow there too. Limits: its healthcare page does not name lockbox, remittance matching or sweeps for practices, so ask for the treasury list in writing.

Huntington

Huntington runs a commercial healthcare banking team and says thousands of healthcare organizations have worked with it. Its healthcare pages cover practice finance for smaller practices and commercial banking for larger provider groups.

Best for: groups in Huntington's footprint that want a commercial healthcare banker. Limits: ask Huntington directly for its lockbox, sweep and positive pay terms.

Webster Bank

Webster Bank's healthcare page names medical groups and dental practices among the providers its medical industry specialists work with. It lists business checking, remote deposit capture, merchant services, savings and healthcare IT and EHR financing. Its business banking navigation also lists treasury management and lockbox services. Ask Webster for its commercial healthcare terms.

Best for: Northeast groups that want a regional bank with named healthcare specialists. Limits: no fees published, and lockbox detail for practices is not stated.

Panacea Financial

Panacea Financial is a division of Primis Bank, Member FDIC. Its MSO Deposit Services and DSO Deposit Services pages are built for groups across locations. Account management, zero balance accounts with sweeps, ACH, wires and remote deposit are listed as free. Merchant processing and an on-location lockbox for patient payments are offered for separate fees. Its high balance program uses Insured Cash Sweep (ICS) to cover balances up to $125 million per tax ID, free on checking balances over $250,000. Its business online banking also offers a Positive Pay module and a remote deposit scanner.

Best for: MSOs and DSOs that want ZBA sweeps, high insured balances and no treasury fees. Limits: the lockbox is for patient payments on location. Ask how it handles insurance remittances and 835 files.

Lemma

Lemma is not a bank. Banking services are provided by Core Bank, Member FDIC. It gives each entity its own bank accounts, lockbox address and cards, and reconciles insurance payments against the remittances that explain them. Its docs cover setting up an MSO and its PCs, MSO management fees and approval rules. Multi-Entity is $50 per entity per month, Single-Entity is $0, and Healthcare Systems is custom priced. Lockbox mail is $2.00 per envelope on Multi-Entity and $2.50 on Single-Entity, and wires are a flat $15. Its business banking page claims $100 million plus in FDIC coverage per entity through partner banks.

Best for: MSO and PC groups that want per entity accounts, a payer lockbox and remittance matching at a published price. Limits: newer and online only, with no branches. Confirm the sweep network terms behind its coverage claim.

Mercury

Mercury is a fintech company, not an FDIC insured bank. Banking services are provided through Choice Financial Group and Column N.A., Members FDIC. Plans are $0, $29.90 or $299 a month. ACH, domestic wires and real time payments are $0 on all plans. It lists up to $5 million in FDIC insurance through partner banks and sweep networks, plus preset or custom roles and permissions. Mercury Treasury opens at a $250,000 balance.

Best for: smaller groups that want low fees, free wires and simple user roles across several entities. Limits: no healthcare lockbox or remittance matching, and Mercury's help center says cash cannot be deposited.

How to choose

  1. Over $10 million in revenue with heavy payer mail: compare J.P. Morgan Healthcare Link and the PNC corporate healthcare team side by side. Ask each to show remittance matching on a sample week of your deposits.
  2. An MSO or DSO with many locations: ask Panacea for its free deposit service analysis, and price Lemma per entity.
  3. Already borrowing from a regional bank: ask Huntington, Webster or your current lender for their full treasury list before you move anything.
  4. Fewer than five entities and mostly electronic payments: Mercury or Lemma Single-Entity keeps cost low.
  5. More than $250,000 in any one entity: get the sweep terms in writing, including which banks hold the funds.

Whatever bank you pick, map accounts to entities and locations before you open them. A clean structure is easier than a cleanup.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Should each location have its own bank account?

Each legal entity needs its own accounts. Per location accounts or sub-accounts are optional but useful, because deposits then show which clinic earned them. Use a ZBA or sweep so cash still pools in one main account.

What is a healthcare lockbox?

It is a mailing address the bank or platform runs for payer and patient checks. The bank deposits the checks and scans the EOBs. Healthcare lockboxes such as J.P. Morgan Healthcare Link also match each payment to its 835 remittance.

How does a medical group get more than $250,000 of FDIC coverage?

Standard coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each ownership category. Insured cash sweep programs spread balances across many FDIC banks. Panacea lists up to $125 million per tax ID, and fintechs list multi-million coverage through partner banks.

Do separate entities get separate FDIC coverage?

Corporation and partnership accounts are their own ownership category, so each separate business entity is generally insured on its own. Ask the FDIC or your bank how your exact structure is treated.

What is positive pay and do we need it?

Positive pay only clears checks you have already listed with the bank. Groups that write many vendor checks should turn it on. PNC and Panacea name it on their pages.

Is a fintech safe for a medical group?

Lemma and Mercury are not banks, but deposits are held at FDIC member banks. Read how pass-through coverage and sweeps work before you rely on the higher limit.

Can MSO and PC money share an account?

It should not. Management company and professional corporation money should stay in separate accounts, with management fees paid between them on paper. Lemma builds this split into its setup.

Can Lucid open bank accounts for our group?

No. Lucid is not a bank. Lucid reconciles and reports on the accounts you open, at whichever bank you choose.
