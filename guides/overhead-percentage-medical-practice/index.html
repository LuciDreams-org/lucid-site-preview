What overhead percentage is normal for a medical practice?

Short answer

There is no single normal overhead percentage for a medical practice, because the answer depends on how overhead is defined and on the specialty. Published figures from named sources run from the high 50s to the low 60s as a percent of revenue. One medical group benchmark reports a median operating cost of 57.5 percent of revenue for allergy and immunology practices, and the American Dental Association asks dental owners whether overhead is 63 percent or less of total income. Use those figures as a starting point only. The number that matters is your own ratio, calculated the same way every month, tracked by location, and compared with your own trend.

What overhead percentage means

Overhead percentage, also called the overhead ratio, answers one question: out of every dollar that comes in, how much goes out to run the practice? The basic formula is:

Overhead percentage = operating expenses divided by revenue, times 100

If a practice collects $100 and spends $60 to operate, its overhead percentage is 60 percent and what is left is 40 percent. That example is hypothetical and only shows the arithmetic.

Two choices change the answer more than anything else:

  1. Which revenue you divide by. Most benchmarks use collected revenue, meaning cash actually received after payer adjustments. Some use gross charges or gross production, which makes overhead look much lower. Always check which one a benchmark uses before comparing.
  2. Whether owner and provider pay sits inside overhead. Some benchmarks count all operating cost, including provider compensation. Others treat provider pay as the profit pool and count only what it costs to run the practice around the providers. A ratio of 57 percent and a ratio of 30 percent can describe the same practice under two definitions.

This is why a search for "medical practice overhead calculation" returns answers that look contradictory. They are usually answering slightly different questions.

Three points about this table:

  1. The sources are narrow. The medical group figure covers one specialty, and the data is from 2016. It is still one of the few public numbers that states exactly what it divides by what. Treat it as an illustration of the order of magnitude, not as a benchmark for your specialty.
  2. The dental figure is a rule of thumb from a professional association. It is a target to test your practice against, not a statistical average.
  3. The trend matters as much as the level. In the 2025 Dental Economics and Levin Group survey, 58 percent of practices reported higher overhead than the year before and 55 percent named rising overhead as their biggest current challenge. The average increase among practices with higher overhead was 4.5 percent. Respondents were mostly owners and partners in independent private practices.

For specialty specific numbers, paid benchmark sets from MGMA are one commonly used source. If you need a defensible comparison for a lender, a partner buy in or a valuation, buy the data for your specialty and size and match the definitions line by line.

Why one number misleads

Even a well sourced benchmark can mislead when you apply it to your own practice. These are the usual reasons.

  1. Specialty. A practice with high cost supplies, devices or procedure rooms carries a different cost structure than a practice that mostly bills visits. Dental, orthopedics, dermatology and primary care all sit at different points. See our pages for primary care, dental and orthopedics.
  2. Provider pay treatment. As above, a benchmark that includes provider pay and one that excludes it are not comparable.
  3. Employed or owner providers. If owners take their pay as distributions, the profit and loss statement will show low expenses and a flattering ratio. Add a fair market salary for owner providers before comparing, or you will think overhead is lower than it is.
  4. Payer mix and collection performance. If collections slip, revenue falls while rent and staff do not, so the ratio rises with no change in spending. Overhead can look worse because of a revenue problem.
  5. Size and number of locations. Adding a location adds fixed cost before it adds revenue. A new site can raise the combined ratio for months while it ramps up.
  6. Timing. Annual prepayments for insurance, software or licenses can make one month look expensive and another cheap. Look at a rolling three month or twelve month figure, not one month.

How to calculate overhead for your practice, step by step

  1. Pick the revenue line. Use collected revenue for the period, net of contractual adjustments and refunds. Write down that choice and keep it.
  2. List operating expenses. Pull them from the profit and loss statement, grouped in the same categories every month (see the next section).
  3. Decide how provider pay is treated. Choose one definition, and compute both if you want to compare with outside sources: total operating cost including provider pay, and overhead excluding provider pay.
  4. Normalize owner pay. Replace owner draws with a fair salary figure so the ratio reflects real cost.
  5. Remove one time items. Large one off costs, such as a legal settlement or a build out, should be shown separately so they do not distort the trend.
  6. Divide and record. Calculate the percentage monthly, plus a trailing three month and trailing twelve month figure.
  7. Compare to yourself first. Your own history, by location, tells you more than any outside range.

Reliable results depend on clean books. If expenses are coded into a few broad accounts, or if the books are months behind, the ratio is a guess. Which expense lines make up overhead

For most practices, overhead lives in a handful of categories. Tracking each as a percent of collections shows where a change in the total comes from.

  1. Staff pay, benefits and payroll taxes for non provider staff. This is usually the largest line.
  2. Facilities: rent, utilities, cleaning, repairs.
  3. Clinical supplies and, where relevant, lab or procedure costs.
  4. Insurance, including malpractice and general liability.
  5. Technology: practice management software, electronic health records, phones, IT support.
  6. Billing and administrative services, whether in house or outsourced.
  7. Marketing and patient acquisition.
  8. Professional fees and licenses.

Reading the profit and loss statement this way turns "overhead is high" into a specific claim, such as "supplies went from one share of collections to a larger share over two quarters." A specific claim can be investigated.

Overhead allocation across locations and entities

The second most common overhead question is allocation: how to assign shared costs, such as a billing team, a central office lease or management salaries, to each location or provider.

  1. Separate direct cost from shared cost. Costs that belong to one location, such as that site's rent and front desk, are direct. Everything else is shared.
  2. Choose one allocation driver and stay with it. Common drivers are share of collections, share of provider hours, share of visits or procedures, and square footage for facility costs. Different cost types can use different drivers.
  3. Write the method down. Partners who dispute results usually dispute the method. A written method, applied the same way each month, ends most of those arguments.
  4. Keep intercompany items clean. If a management company charges the practice entities, the charges and the matching income need to cancel out in a consolidated view. Our post on intercompany account mapping for medical groups covers the mechanics.

Allocation does not change total profit. It changes which location or provider looks profitable, so choose a method partners consider fair and review it once a year.

What to do when overhead looks high

  1. Confirm the number is real. Check the definition, the revenue line, owner pay treatment and any one time items.
  2. Check the revenue side first. Falling collections raise the ratio without any change in cost. Look at days in accounts receivable and aging. See our month end receivables aging checklist.
  3. Find the category that moved. Compare each expense line as a percent of collections with the same line three and twelve months ago.
  4. Look at the unit view. Cost per visit, per provider or per location shows whether one site or one provider explains the change.
  5. Fix the biggest controllable item. Staffing levels and schedule fit are usually the biggest lever, then supply purchasing and vendor terms, then recurring subscriptions.
  6. Set a target and review it monthly. A target you do not review is a wish. See which monthly numbers a practice owner should review.

Do not cut cost blindly. Cutting front desk or clinical support staff can reduce visits and collections, and the ratio gets worse.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is a good overhead ratio for a private practice?

It depends on specialty and on whether provider pay is included. A published medical group benchmark reports a median operating cost of 57.5 percent of revenue for allergy and immunology, and the American Dental Association asks dental owners to test for 63 percent or less of total income. Treat those as reference points and judge your practice against your own trend by location.

How do I calculate overhead for my medical practice?

Divide operating expenses by collected revenue for the same period and multiply by 100. Decide in advance whether provider pay is inside the expenses, replace owner draws with a fair salary figure, and show one time costs separately. Calculate it monthly with trailing three and twelve month views.

Does overhead include provider pay?

It depends on the source. Some benchmarks count all operating cost including provider pay, and others exclude it. Never compare your number with a benchmark until you confirm both use the same definition.

Why did my overhead percentage go up when I did not spend more?

Overhead is a ratio, so lower collections raise it even when spending is flat. Slower payer payments, more denials or fewer visits can all do it. Check collections and receivables aging before you cut costs.

How should I allocate overhead across several locations?

Separate direct costs from shared costs, pick one driver such as share of collections or visits, write the method down and apply it every month. The method matters less than consistency and partner agreement. Review it once a year so it still matches how the practice works.

How often should I look at overhead?

Monthly, by location, with a trailing three month and twelve month view to smooth timing effects. A quarterly review of the allocation method is enough. Watch for a sudden change in any one line, because that is where a problem usually starts.
