How do I choose an M&A advisor for my practice?

Home / Guides / How do I choose an M&A advisor for my practice?

Short answer

Match the advisor to the size of your practice and the buyers you want to reach. Practice brokers usually list smaller practices and are paid a success fee that Practice Transitions Group puts at 6% to 12% of the price, while healthcare M&A advisors and investment banks run a competitive process for larger groups, usually for a monthly work fee plus a success fee. Consultants and exit planners can help you prepare, but most do not run the sale. Pick the one with recent deals in your specialty and size, a clear fee in writing, a senior person on your deal every day, and a plan to defend your numbers once a buyer starts testing them.

Size bands are rough. SovDoc places brokers under about $500,000 of EBITDA, specialized M&A advisors from about $500,000 to $10 million plus, and healthcare investment bankers from about $5 million to $50 million plus. Mertz Taggart puts brokers at up to $2 to $3 million of enterprise value and M&A firms usually at $5 million and above.

Healthcare investment bank

An investment bank runs a full sale or recapitalization for a larger group. It prepares an offering memorandum and data book, builds a buyer list of private equity sponsors, platforms and strategic buyers, and manages a timed process with several bids at once. SovDoc describes this tier as handling complex deals, structured capital raises and platform M&A.

Strengths: deep buyer reach among sponsors, experience with rollover equity and earnouts, and the staff to run a long diligence period.

Limits: most will not take a small practice, and the fees and paperwork assume a larger deal. Ask whether the firm or its broker dealer affiliate is registered for the work it will do, a question Auxo Capital Advisors suggests every owner raise.

Healthcare M&A advisor

This is the middle of the market and where many specialty groups land. A healthcare M&A advisor prepares the practice, rebuilds adjusted EBITDA, contacts a curated set of MSOs and private equity backed platforms, and creates competition for price and terms. Mertz Taggart contrasts this with a broker's listing approach: the advisor identifies a targeted group of buyers, manages confidentiality and runs one coordinated process.

Strengths: specialty knowledge, buyers who already acquire in your field, and help comparing offers that mix cash, rollover equity, seller notes and earnouts.

Limits: fees are higher than a pure listing, and quality varies a lot by the people assigned to you.

Practice broker

A broker lists and markets a practice, screens buyers and helps close. This fits a single location practice selling to another owner, a local group or an associate. Mertz Taggart describes the broker model as listing driven, with broad marketing and offers reviewed one at a time.

Strengths: lower cost on small deals, local buyer contacts and a simpler process.

Limits: less reach into private equity platforms, and less help modeling complex deal terms. FOCUS Investment Banking notes that brokers often work many transactions at once with lighter preparation.

Consultant or exit planner

Some owners hire a consultant, exit planner or valuation professional before they hire anyone to sell. They help set goals, fix gaps and get a range of value. Most do not run a buyer process, so you still need a broker or advisor to go to market. Fees are usually hourly or fixed. For the value side, see how a medical practice is valued.

Fee structures, as published

No regulator publishes advisory fees, and Kadenwood Capital notes the data is self reported by advisors in surveys. Use these as benchmarks for negotiation, not price lists.

  1. Broker success fee. Practice Transitions Group says broker fees typically run 6% to 12% of the final sale price, paid only at closing. Its breakdown: success only fees of 6% to 10%, hybrid models of $2,000 to $10,000 upfront plus 4% to 8% at closing, and 10% to 12% common for practices under $400,000 in revenue.
  2. Work fee or retainer. Kadenwood, citing the Firmex fee guide survey, says monthly fees are the most common work fee, most often $5,000 to $15,000 a month. Many advisors credit work fees against the success fee.
  3. Success fee by deal size. The same Firmex data shows average quoted success fees of about 5.5% on a $5 million deal, 3.7% at $20 million and 2.1% at $100 million.
  4. Lehman formula. A sliding scale: 5% of the first $1 million, 4% of the second, 3% of the third, 2% of the fourth and 1% above that. Many firms use doubled or adjusted versions.
  5. Accelerators. Some agreements raise the rate on value above an agreed target, so the advisor gains most when you do.

Read the fine print on what counts as "price". Ask whether the fee applies to rollover equity, seller notes and earnouts, and whether it is paid when those amounts are paid or at closing. Ask about the tail, meaning the period after the agreement ends when a deal with a buyer the advisor introduced still earns a fee, and about expenses.

Healthcare experience matters

General M&A experience does not carry over cleanly. Midcap Advisors points to healthcare rules such as Stark, Anti Kickback and state corporate practice of medicine laws, plus healthcare specific valuation work like fair market value pay and quality of earnings. An advisor who knows your specialty knows which buyers pay for Mohs surgery or ancillaries, how payer mix moves value, and what the buyer's quality of earnings team will challenge. See what a quality of earnings report is.

Ask for recent closed deals in your specialty and size band, and call two or three of those sellers.

Buyer network and process

A good process creates competition. Ask how the advisor will build and rank the buyer list, how many buyers they expect to contact, and why each one is there. Auxo lists a generic buyer list copied from a database as a warning sign.

A typical sell side process looks like this:

  1. Preparation: rebuild adjusted EBITDA and the add back file, and fix gaps.
  2. Materials: offering memorandum, data book and a clean data room.
  3. Outreach: confidential contact with selected buyers under NDA.
  4. Bids: indications of interest, then letters of intent.
  5. Diligence: the buyer's quality of earnings, legal and operational review.
  6. Closing: purchase paperwork, employment terms and funds.

Preparation starts long before step 1. See how to prepare a medical practice for sale.

Questions to ask before you sign

Adapted from Auxo Capital Advisors' list and other sell side guides:

  1. What deals have you closed in my specialty and size in the last two years?
  2. Who will run my deal day to day, and how many deals are they on now?
  3. How will you build the buyer list, and which buyers would you call first?
  4. How will you prepare for the buyer's quality of earnings and defend add backs?
  5. How will you compare cash, rollover equity, earnouts and seller notes?
  6. What is the full fee in writing, including work fees, tail, expenses and how deferred amounts are treated?
  7. Do you represent sellers only? What buyer relationships should I know about?
  8. What would make you tell me not to launch, or not to sign a letter of intent?

Red flags, in general

These are patterns sell side guides warn about, not claims about any firm:

  1. A precise valuation before anyone has reviewed your books.
  2. A pitch about introductions with little about preparation or diligence.
  3. Fees, tail or expenses that stay vague.
  4. Buyer relationships or conflicts brushed aside.
  5. A rush to go to market no matter how ready the practice is.
  6. No examples of defending value when a buyer pushed back on earnings.

How to choose by practice type

  1. Solo or small single location practice selling to a local buyer: a practice broker, with a fee in writing.
  2. Specialty group with several providers or locations: a healthcare M&A advisor with recent deals in your specialty.
  3. Larger groups or platforms with $5 million plus of EBITDA: a healthcare investment bank.
  4. Not sure yet whether to sell: a consultant or valuation first, then a sell side advisor. Read should I sell my practice to private equity and private equity vs MSO vs staying independent.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a practice broker and an M&A advisor?

A broker usually lists a smaller practice and markets it broadly to find one buyer. An M&A advisor prepares the practice and runs a competitive process among selected MSOs, private equity platforms and strategic buyers. Size is the usual dividing line.

How much does an M&A advisor charge to sell a practice?

Most charge a success fee at closing, and many add a monthly work fee. Practice Transitions Group says broker fees typically run 6% to 12% of the price. Survey data cited by Kadenwood shows average success fees near 5.5% on a $5 million deal, falling as deals get larger.

Do I need an investment bank to sell my practice?

Not usually for a small practice. Investment banks fit larger groups and platforms, often with $5 million or more of EBITDA. Many specialty groups below that use a healthcare M&A advisor.

Should my advisor have experience in my specialty?

Yes. Specialty experience shapes which buyers get called, how add backs are defended and how service lines are valued. Ask for recent closed deals in your specialty and call those sellers.

What is a tail clause?

It is the period after your agreement ends when the advisor still earns a fee if you close with a buyer they introduced. Agree on its length and the buyer list it covers before you sign.

When should I hire an M&A advisor?

Many sell side guides suggest six to 12 months before going to market, after your books are clean. Start the accounting work two to three years out so buyers see a clean history.
