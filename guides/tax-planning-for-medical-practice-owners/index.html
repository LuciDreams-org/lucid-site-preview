Tax planning for medical practice owners

Tax planning for a practice owner means making a few decisions during the year that lower what the practice owes: when you buy equipment, which retirement plan you open, how the IRS taxes your practice, and which state tax and credit rules you use. Some, like buying equipment, only count for this year if you act by December 31.

This guide is general information, not tax advice for your practice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and your numbers. Talk to a CPA before you act on it.

This guide covers the taxes your practice pays as a business. It doesn't cover deducting your own medical bills or treatments.

Key points

  • For 2026, you can generally deduct up to $2,560,000 of equipment in the year it's ready to use, under Section 179.
  • If you work alone, a Solo 401(k) lets you put away up to $72,000 for 2026, counting your own deferral and the practice's contribution.
  • In many states, your PLLC or S corp can pay state income tax itself, so you get a federal deduction beyond the personal cap.

What are the 10 ways practice owners lower their tax?

Most practice owners use only a few of the 10 ways in the table below. A solo owner with no staff will lean on retirement plans and equipment; a practice with staff and partners has more to gain from the S corp election and the state tax program.

# Way Who it suits
1 Deduct equipment the year it's ready to use Any practice buying devices, chairs, lasers or imaging
2 Put more into a retirement plan Owners with steady profit, with or without staff
3 Elect S corp tax treatment Owners whose profit is well above a fair salary
4 Pay state tax through the practice (PTET) PLLCs with more than one owner and S corps in states that offer it
5 Claim the QBI deduction while you still qualify Owners near or under the 2026 income limits
6 Claim the credits you qualify for Practices that started a retirement plan or hired in 2025
7 Deduct health insurance and fund an HSA Owners who buy their own coverage
8 Deduct a home office Owners who do admin work at home
9 Track every professional cost Every owner
10 Time purchases, bonuses and payments Practices on the cash method

What do the tax planning terms in this guide mean?

Section 179 is the rule that lets you deduct the full cost of equipment in the year it's ready to use, instead of over several years. Bonus depreciation is a second rule that does the same for most equipment, with no dollar cap. PTET, the pass-through entity tax, is a state program where your PLLC or S corp pays the state income tax on its profit. The QBI deduction is a federal deduction of up to 20% of the profit from a business you own. Cost of goods sold is what you paid for the product you sold or used, and you deduct it when you sell or use it.

How does each of the 10 ways work?

You'll find each way's rule, its 2026 limit where one applies, and an IRS source below. You'll get the most from the ways when you and your CPA decide before December 31, because you can't buy equipment for this year after December 31, and some retirement plan choices have year-end deadlines.

1. Deduct equipment the year it's ready to use

You can generally deduct the full cost of equipment in the year it's installed and ready to use. For 2026, Section 179 covers up to $2,560,000, and the amount you can take shrinks once you buy more than $4,090,000.

Bonus depreciation is back at 100% for equipment bought after January 19, 2025, and the 2025 tax law made it permanent (IRS, Publication 946). Many states don't follow bonus depreciation, so your state deduction may differ. Ask your CPA whether deducting it all now or spreading it out suits you better, since selling the equipment later can bring part of the deduction back as income.

Dental practice owners buying chairs or imaging can read bookkeeping for dental practices.

2. Put more into a retirement plan

A retirement plan is often the biggest deduction an owner controls. For 2026, you can defer up to $24,500 into a 401(k), and the total from you and the practice can reach $72,000, plus catch-up amounts if you're 50 or older. (IRS 401(k) limits)

  • No staff: a Solo 401(k) or a SEP IRA.
  • With staff: a safe harbor 401(k), which means you'll also contribute for your staff.
  • High earners close to retirement: ask about a cash balance plan, which needs an actuary.

3. Elect S corp tax treatment

If your profit is well above a fair salary for your role, the IRS can tax your PLLC as an S corp, and you'll pay Social Security and Medicare tax on your salary, not on all of the profit. You'll take on payroll and a separate business return. See setting a reasonable owner salary in a medical S corp.

4. Pay state tax through the practice (PTET)

If your state offers a PTET, your PLLC or S corp can pay the state income tax on its profit and deduct it on the federal return. That gets you around the personal cap on state and local tax, which is $40,400 for 2026 and shrinks once your income passes $505,000. (Pub. L. 119-21 sec. 70120; IRS Notice 2020-75)

5. Claim the QBI deduction while you still qualify

Medicine is a "specified service" under the QBI rules, so you lose the deduction as your income rises. For 2026, the phase-out starts at $201,750 of taxable income for a single filer and $403,500 for a joint return. (Rev. Proc. 2025-32; Instructions for Form 8995-A) If you're near the line, a retirement contribution can bring you back under it. As an S corp owner, your salary doesn't count toward QBI. (IRS, Qualified business income deduction)

6. Claim the credits you qualify for

A credit cuts your tax bill dollar for dollar, so it's worth more than a deduction of the same size. If you start a retirement plan and have 100 or fewer employees, you may claim up to $5,000 a year for three years toward the setup costs (IRS, retirement plan startup costs credit).

The Work Opportunity Tax Credit covers staff who started work by December 31, 2025, and Congress hadn't renewed it as of November 2026 (IRS, Work Opportunity Tax Credit). Ask your CPA which other credits fit your practice.

7. Deduct health insurance and fund an HSA

If you own the practice and buy your own health insurance, you can usually deduct the premiums on your personal return (Form 7206). If you own more than 2% of an S corp, the S corp adds the premiums to your W-2 first, then you take the deduction. With a high-deductible plan, you can put up to $4,400 (self-only) or $8,750 (family) into an HSA for 2026 (Publication 969).

8. Deduct a home office

If you do the practice's admin at home, in a space you use only for that, you may deduct a home office (Publication 587). The rules for an owner who also has a clinic are strict. See the simplified home office deduction, with examples.

9. Track every professional cost

You can deduct the ordinary costs of running a practice, and owners lose money when nobody records them (Publication 334). The common ones:

  • State license and DEA registration fees
  • Continuing medical education, courses and travel to them
  • Malpractice insurance
  • Board certification and professional dues
  • Practice software and subscriptions

10. Time purchases, bonuses and payments

If your practice uses the cash method, you deduct an expense in the year you pay it (Publication 538). Buying equipment counts only once it's installed and ready to use by December 31. You'll also want your last estimated payment set before year end, so your plan and your payments match. For payment amounts, see estimated tax safe harbors for medical practice owners.

How much could these ways save? Two examples

How much you save depends on your income, your state and how the IRS taxes your practice. In the first example below, a solo owner lowers federal income tax by about $29,575 with two of the ways. These examples use round numbers to show the arithmetic. They're illustrations, not typical results.

Example 1: a solo owner's year-end plan. A physical therapy practice owner runs a single-owner PLLC with no staff, files as a single taxpayer and expects $400,000 of profit in 2026. In November, the owner buys and installs a $60,000 piece of equipment and defers $24,500 into a Solo 401(k). The example assumes a 35% federal income tax rate on the last dollars of income.

Way Amount deducted Federal income tax lower by (35%)
1. Equipment, Section 179 $60,000 $21,000
2. Solo 401(k) deferral $24,500 $8,575
Total $84,500 $29,575

The 401(k) amount is taxed when you take it out in retirement.

Tax planning for med spa owners

Med spa owners have the same 10 ways as any practice, plus three issues of their own: product, providers and ownership. You'll want all three recorded correctly in your books before you plan.

  • Product: you deduct injectables and retail skincare when you use or sell them, not when you buy them (Example 2).
  • Providers: whether an injector or aesthetician is a contractor or an employee depends on the IRS rules on control, not on the contract you sign (IRS, independent contractor or employee).
  • Ownership: if you aren't a licensed provider, your state may require a provider to own the medical side, with your company managing the business.

Questions, answered

Questions practice owners ask about tax planning

This guide is general information, not tax advice for your practice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and your numbers. Talk to a CPA before you act on it.

What can a medical practice owner write off?

You can write off the ordinary costs of running the practice: staff pay, rent, equipment, supplies, malpractice insurance, licenses, continuing education and software. You'll also deduct your own retirement contributions and, if you buy your own coverage, your health insurance. You can't deduct your personal medical bills as a practice expense.

Can I write off a laser or other medical equipment in the year I buy it?

Usually, yes, if it's ready to use that year. For 2026, Section 179 covers up to $2,560,000 of equipment, and bonus depreciation covers most equipment at 100%. If the laser arrives in December but isn't installed and ready for patients until January, you'll deduct it in the new year. Your state may not follow the federal rule.

What retirement plan saves the most tax for a practice owner?

For a solo owner, a Solo 401(k) often lets you put away the most, up to $72,000 for 2026 before catch-up amounts. With staff, a safe harbor 401(k) works but means contributing for your team too. Owners close to retirement with high profit may save more with a cash balance plan.

Are injectables and skincare a med spa buys deductible right away?

Usually not. Injectables and retail skincare are inventory, so you deduct them as cost of goods sold when you use them in a treatment or sell them. Product still on the shelf at December 31 carries into next year. Some small businesses can use simpler inventory rules, so ask your CPA which method you're on.

What tax credits can a medical practice claim?

If you have 100 or fewer employees and start a retirement plan, you may claim up to $5,000 a year for three years toward the setup costs. The Work Opportunity Tax Credit covers staff who started by December 31, 2025, unless Congress renews it. Your CPA can check which others fit, including state credits.
