Short answer

Most medical practices are valued by applying a multiple to adjusted earnings, then adjusting for size, specialty, growth and risk. Published multiples range widely: small practices commonly sell for a few times owner earnings, while large platform deals can price at ten times or more of EBITDA. Buyers test those earnings hard, so accurate monthly books, documented add backs and clean receivables lift the number a buyer will pay. This guide is general information, not a valuation. A certified valuation professional performs formal valuations, and a healthcare transaction adviser helps with a sale.

What "valuation" means for a practice

A valuation is an opinion of what a practice would sell for, or is worth for a stated purpose such as a sale, a partner buyout, a divorce, estate planning or a loan. The purpose changes the method and the number. A formal valuation for a legal or tax purpose is prepared by a certified valuation professional following professional standards. A broker or banker's price opinion for a sale is a different thing. A quick calculator is a rough guide at best.

Three standard approaches exist.

  1. Income approach. Value the earnings the practice produces, either with a multiple of earnings or by discounting expected future cash flow.
  2. Market approach. Compare with prices of similar practices that sold.
  3. Asset approach. Add up what the practice owns, such as equipment and cash, less what it owes. This is used mostly when earnings are low or negative.

For practices that are sold as going concerns, the earnings multiple is the center of most conversations, so the rest of this guide focuses on it.

The two earnings measures you will hear

  1. Seller's discretionary earnings, or SDE. This is operating profit with the owner's pay, owner benefits and one time or personal costs added back. It is used mostly for smaller practices, where the buyer will also work in the practice.
  2. EBITDA, or earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, adjusted for add backs and for a market rate of pay for the owner. It is used for larger practices and groups, where the buyer will hire a clinician to replace the owner's clinical work.

The earnings figure matters as much as the multiple. A multiple of five applied to profit that a buyer will not accept is worth nothing. This is where bookkeeping quality turns into dollars.

Read these carefully.

  1. Size moves the multiple more than anything. Signal Hill's compilation shows large platforms pricing several turns above smaller practices in the same specialty.
  2. Deal type matters. A platform deal, where a buyer builds a new group around you, prices above an add on to an existing group.
  3. Specialty matters too. Many top ranges depend on being a fast growing platform or passing an EBITDA threshold.
  4. Old data misleads. HealthFMV notes that some high urgent care platform figures date from the mid 2010s boom, so newer deals may price lower.
  5. These are ranges that firms publish and compile, not guarantees. They are not an offer for your practice.

What buyers look at

Buyers and their advisers run a review of the practice's earnings, often called a quality of earnings review. Based on GATP Solutions' description, they look at:

  1. Revenue timing, meaning whether collections match the month of care.
  2. Accounts receivable aging, write offs and collection rates.
  3. Payer mix and how concentrated it is.
  4. Provider productivity against what the providers are paid.
  5. Support for every add back to earnings.

Why clean books raise the price

Earnings that cannot be proven get removed. GATP describes how buyers treat the numbers:

  1. Add backs with an invoice or other support are accepted. Examples include a related party lease reset to market rent, or a documented one time cost.
  2. Add backs without support are rejected. So are "one time" costs that recur every year.
  3. Each item a buyer cannot verify comes out of adjusted EBITDA, and the multiple applies to the lower number.
  4. Buyers also set a working capital target. A shortfall at closing reduces the price, and bad receivables such as voided invoices left in the ledger can cut it further.

A simple example, with made up numbers. A practice shows $400,000 of adjusted EBITDA and applies a five times multiple, for $2,000,000. A buyer rejects $60,000 of unsupported add backs. Earnings drop to $340,000, and at the same five times the price falls to $1,700,000. The practice lost $300,000 of value because of missing paperwork. This is an illustration, not a client result.

Clean books help in five specific ways.

  1. Monthly statements that tie to tax returns remove doubt about the earnings.
  2. Accrual numbers show revenue in the month of care. See cash versus accrual for a medical practice. GATP says buyers price on accrual numbers even when the practice keeps cash books.
  3. Receivables with aging reports and honest write offs show real collections.
  4. Profit by provider and by location shows where earnings come from and how dependent they are on one person.
  5. Documented owner pay and personal costs make add backs easy to prove.

How to prepare, starting two or three years before a sale

  1. Get books current and reconciled every month. If they are behind, catch up first. See bookkeeping costs for medical practices.
  2. Ask your CPA about accrual reporting and the timing of any tax method change. One adviser recommends switching at least two years before a sale. That is an adviser's view, so ask for advice on your facts.
  3. Separate personal costs from practice costs, and keep invoices for anything you will want to add back.
  4. Track receivables and clean up old or voided items.
  5. Review payer mix and provider dependence, and make a plan to reduce concentration where it is high.
  6. Produce a monthly package: profit and loss, balance sheet, cash flow and the key practice numbers. Ask for a version a lender or buyer can read.
  7. Model what a sale would net you after taxes with your CPA, and consider a healthcare transaction adviser and a valuation professional.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How is a medical practice valued?

Most are valued by applying a multiple to adjusted earnings, either seller's discretionary earnings for smaller practices or EBITDA for larger ones. The multiple reflects size, specialty, growth and risk. A certified valuation professional performs formal valuations.

What multiple do medical practices sell for?

It varies widely. Across all industries, not only medical, IBBA and M&A Source median multiples were 2.0x to 3.1x SDE for deals under $2 million and 4.0x to 5.8x EBITDA for deals from $2 million to $50 million in Q2 2026. Large platform deals in some specialties are published at 8x to 20x EBITDA. Treat these as ranges from different samples, not a price for your practice.

What do buyers look at when they buy a private practice?

They examine earnings quality: revenue timing, receivables, payer mix, provider productivity and support for each add back. This is often called a quality of earnings review. Buyers remove any earnings they cannot verify.

Why do clean books matter for a sale?

Buyers value only the earnings they can prove. Each item a buyer cannot verify comes out of adjusted EBITDA, and the multiple applies to the lower number. Books with monthly statements that tie to tax returns, documented add backs and clean receivables lower the risk they see, which can mean fewer price cuts late in the deal.

Can I use an online calculator to value my practice?

A calculator gives a rough range at best, because it cannot see your payer mix, add backs or owner dependence. Two practices with the same revenue can be worth very different amounts. Use it for a first look, then ask a valuation professional or transaction adviser for a real opinion.
