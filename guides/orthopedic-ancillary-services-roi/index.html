Which ancillary services pay off for an orthopedic practice?

Short answer

For most orthopedic groups, in-house X-ray and DME pay back first because they cost little to start, and physical therapy follows once you have the volume to keep therapists busy. MRI can be a strong service line, but a scanner costs from about $300,000 refurbished to $2 million or more new, so it needs steady scan volume to pay back. Orthopedic urgent care and an ASC bring the largest income per surgeon in published data, and the largest capital and risk. Every option sits inside Stark law and state rules, so model the money and get a healthcare attorney before you sign.

The same article cites a 2023 GMA and AAOE benchmarking report: ancillary revenue makes up to 60% of surgeon pay in large groups that own an ASC, and up to 30% in groups that do not.

In-house X-ray

Most orthopedic offices already have X-ray, and it is the entry point for every other ancillary. Medical Outfitters lists a fixed digital radiography room at $60,000 to $275,000 delivered and installed, with the refurbished room most practices buy at $60,000 to $135,000.

Strengths: low cost, used in almost every visit, and same-day reads keep patients moving. Limits: low payment per study, a technologist on payroll and state radiation safety rules. In the Journal of Orthopaedic Business group, 79,046 radiographs in 2024 produced about $23,429 per partner.

In-office MRI

MRI is the ancillary most groups debate. In the Journal of Orthopaedic Business group, 11,905 MRIs in 2024 produced about $27,030 per partner. The authors note that Medicare MRI payments fell by more than 60% between 2005 and 2020 after inflation, yet surgeon-owned MRI still pays when it is set up well.

Costs, from Medical Outfitters' 2026 price guide:

  1. Open or low-field MRI, the class orthopedic practices often buy: $300,000 to $525,000 refurbished, $600,000 to $950,000 new.
  2. 1.5T MRI: $475,000 to $975,000 refurbished, $1,025,000 to $2,000,000 new.

The journal authors report $1 million to $2.5 million for full-body scanners, and note that siting can need shielded rooms or even structural work. Extremity-only scanners cost less but image only arms and legs.

Two rules change the math. Billing Medicare for MRI generally needs an accredited site (ACR, IAC or The Joint Commission), and some states require a certificate of need before you buy, according to CASRAI's MRI cost guide. Under Stark, MRI also carries a disclosure rule, covered below. Read our guide to medical equipment financing before you choose between lease and loan.

Physical therapy

Physical therapy brings patients back two or three times a week, so it builds steady revenue and keeps post-surgery care close to the surgeon. The Journal of Orthopaedic Business group logged 77,813 PT visits across 5 sites in 2024, about $32,971 per surgeon. The authors say surgeon-owned PT is legal in most states, but patients must be free to choose another provider.

Benchmarks from Red Bike Advisors, a PT accounting firm: $90 to $130 net revenue per visit for most outpatient orthopedic PT, and a net margin of 15% to 25% for owner-run practices. Workers' compensation and cash pay tend to pay best; Medicare and Medicaid pay less. Some states restrict referral-linked PT ownership, so check your state with counsel. For claims work, see our guide to physical therapy billing companies.

DME and bracing

DME and bracing are often the fastest payback, because braces, boots and slings are dispensed at the visit and the start-up cost is small. The journal authors call DME one of the most profitable and underused ancillaries, with margins of up to 60% when managed well. Their group dispensed 20,058 items in 2024, about $31,908 per surgeon FTE. Breg, a bracing maker, says its in-house program generates about $83,000 in revenue per surgeon a year and $40,000 to $50,000 in profit. That is the vendor's own figure.

You can run the program in-house, with your own stock, staff and billing, or with a vendor that supplies and sometimes bills on a consignment basis. In-house keeps more of the margin; a vendor model takes less staff time and less cash. Billing Medicare for these items usually means DMEPOS supplier enrollment, accreditation and supplier standards set by CMS.

Orthopedic urgent care

Orthopedic urgent care brings in injured patients who would otherwise go to an emergency room, and it feeds every other ancillary and the operating schedule. In the Journal of Orthopaedic Business group, 21,693 urgent care visits across 2 sites produced about $75,433 per partner, and the authors found an average ED visit cost $8,000 against $287 for a dedicated orthopedic urgent care visit.

It also costs the most of the clinic-based options. A business plan firm puts a general urgent care center at $500,000 to $1,000,000 or more to open, including build-out, X-ray, staff and 3 to 6 months of working capital. An orthopedic version needs surgeon or advanced practice staffing for evening and weekend hours.

ASC

The ASC produces the largest income per surgeon in the published data: about $493,109 per surgeon for 7,309 cases in the Journal of Orthopaedic Business group. It also carries the largest capital, capital calls and its own federal safe harbor rules. We cover buy-in, building and office suite options in our guide to ASC investment vs an office surgery suite, so we do not repeat it here.

Stark law and the in-office ancillary services exception

Legal note: this is a general summary, not legal advice. Before you add or restructure any ancillary, work with a healthcare attorney.

The federal Stark law bars a referring clinician from referring Medicare patients for certain services, including imaging, physical therapy and DME, to an entity the clinician has a financial interest in, unless an exception applies. For services inside your own group, the usual exception is the in-office ancillary services exception at 42 CFR 411.355(b). In general terms:

  1. The service is performed or supervised by members of the group or people they supervise.
  2. It is provided in the same building where the group sees patients, or in a centralized building the group uses.
  3. It is billed by the group or an entity the group owns.
  4. For MRI, CT and PET, the patient must get a written notice at the time of referral listing at least 5 other suppliers within 25 miles.
  5. For DME, the exception only covers a short list of items: canes, crutches, walkers, folding manual wheelchairs and blood glucose monitors that meet set conditions. Braces and boots raise separate questions for your counsel.

How you pay partners matters as much. Profit shares cannot track how much each partner refers into the ancillary. The Journal of Orthopaedic Business authors describe equal pooling as common, with productivity or hybrid models tied to neutral measures such as RVUs or total visits. State self-referral laws, certificate of need rules and the anti-kickback statute can add further limits.

Payback examples

Illustrative example only. These are round assumptions, not quotes from any vendor, payer or practice. Replace each one with your own numbers.

AncillaryAssumptionsPayback
Digital X-ray room$100,000 cost. 20 studies a day, 250 days, $45 net each. Tech and service $85,000 a yearAbout 9 months
Low-field MRI$700,000 with siting. 10 scans a day, 22 days a month, $300 net each. Staff and service $20,000 a monthAbout 15 months
PT clinic$250,000 build-out and ramp. 3 therapists, 12 visits a day each, $110 a visit, 20% marginAbout 15 months at full volume
DME program$25,000 starting stock. $83,000 revenue per surgeon, 40% margin, 4 surgeonsAbout 2 months

How the numbers work:

  1. X-ray: 5,000 studies at $45 is $225,000 a year. Less $85,000 for tech and service leaves $140,000, so the $100,000 room pays back in about 9 months.
  2. MRI: 220 scans a month at $300 is $66,000. Less $20,000 leaves $46,000 a month, so $700,000 pays back in about 15 months. Break-even with a loan payment of about $14,000 a month is roughly 115 scans a month, or 5 to 6 a day.
  3. PT: 9,000 visits at $110 is $990,000 a year, and a 20% margin leaves about $198,000, so $250,000 pays back in about 15 months once the clinic is full. Add the months it takes to ramp up referrals.
  4. DME: 4 surgeons at $83,000 is $332,000 of revenue, and a 40% margin leaves about $133,000 a year, so $25,000 of starting stock pays back in about 2 months.

Payer mix, denials and collection lag move all of these. Tax changes the result too: equipment can often be deducted faster under Section 179 or bonus depreciation, which your CPA plans against year-end income.

How to choose

  1. Small group, 2 to 5 surgeons: start with X-ray and DME, then PT once referrals fill a therapist's schedule.
  2. Mid-size group with steady imaging orders: price a low-field or extremity MRI and compare against your current imaging referrals.
  3. Large or multi-site group: add urgent care to feed the system, and look at an ASC with the help of our ASC guide.
  4. Planning a sale in the next few years: ancillaries change what a buyer pays. See who is buying orthopedic practices.
  5. Changing systems at the same time: check that your EHR can report each service line. See our guide to the best EHR for orthopedics.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Which orthopedic ancillary pays back fastest?

DME and X-ray usually pay back first, because start-up cost is low and every clinic day uses them. In the illustrative examples, a DME program pays back in about 2 months and an X-ray room in about 9. MRI, urgent care and an ASC take longer and need more capital.

Is in-office MRI worth it for an orthopedic practice?

It can be, if you have steady volume. A refurbished low-field scanner starts around $300,000, and a new 1.5T runs $1 million to $2 million. In the illustrative example, a $700,000 scanner breaks even at about 5 to 6 scans a day.

How much does a PT clinic add for an orthopedic group?

One large group reported about $33,000 per surgeon a year from PT. Benchmarks put outpatient PT at $90 to $130 per visit and 15% to 25% net margin, so profit depends on keeping therapists busy and on payer mix.

Can my practice own physical therapy under Stark?

Often yes, through the in-office ancillary services exception, if the service, location and billing tests are met. Patients must be free to go elsewhere, and some states add their own limits. A healthcare attorney should review your setup.

Do I need accreditation for in-house DME?

Billing Medicare for DME and bracing generally needs DMEPOS supplier enrollment, which includes accreditation and supplier standards. A vendor or consignment program can carry some of that work. Ask counsel how Stark applies to braces and boots.

How should partners split ancillary income?

Splits cannot track how much each partner refers into the ancillary. Many groups pool ancillary income equally, and some use neutral measures such as RVUs or total visits. Have your healthcare attorney approve the formula.

Should I lease or buy an MRI?

Leasing lowers the upfront cash; buying can cost less over the machine's life and may allow faster depreciation. Compare total payments, service contracts and your tax position, and read our equipment financing guide before you sign.
