Should my dental practice mill crowns in house?

Home / Guides / Should my dental practice mill crowns in house?

Short answer

In-house milling pays off when you place enough single-unit crowns each month to cover the system's monthly cost, and when you have chair time and a trained assistant to run it. Chairside systems include Dentsply Sirona CEREC, Planmeca PlanMill, Glidewell's fastmill.io and, for printed crowns, SprintRay Midas. The alternative is an outside lab such as Glidewell, where a model-free digital BruxZir zirconia crown lists at $89 per unit (Glidewell price list, checked November 26, 2026). Many practices land on a hybrid: mill simple single units, and send bridges, implants and esthetic cases to the lab.

What you are really comparing

The lab bill is visible. The cost of milling is spread across several lines. Put both sides on one page before you decide.

Outside lab, per crown: the lab fee, shipping, a second appointment to seat the crown, and a temporary crown.

In house, per month: the equipment payment or lease, software and service plans, blocks or resin, burs and other consumables, and the assistant and chair time it takes to design, mill and finish each crown.

In house turns a variable cost (a fee per crown) into a mostly fixed cost (a monthly payment). That is good when volume is steady and bad when it drops. A slow month at the lab costs you less. A slow month with a mill costs you the same payment.

Chairside milling systems

Dentsply Sirona CEREC

CEREC pairs the Primescan intraoral scanner, CEREC software and the Primemill milling unit. Dentsply Sirona's Primemill page says a zirconia crown can be milled in around 5 minutes in Super-Fast mode, with a fast grinding option of around 7 minutes for glass ceramics, and that the unit works with around 50 materials from validated partners. Its own materials include CEREC Tessera, Celtra Duo, CEREC MTL Zirconia, CEREC Zirconia+ and CEREC Blocs C.

Dentsply Sirona does not publish a system price. Dealers sell it, and promotions change through the year, so get the scanner, mill, software, training and service priced line by line.

Best for: practices that want one maker's connected scan, design and mill workflow and plan to place same day crowns often.

Planmeca PlanMill

Planmeca's comparison page lists the PlanMill 35 for dental clinics, with 4 axis milling of blocks, wet and dry processing, an automated tool changer for 5 tools and an average milling time of 11 minutes for an IPS e.max molar crown. Planmeca says it is designed for inlays, onlays, veneers and up to 3-unit bridges. The PlanMill 30 S is a wet-only clinic mill, and the 5 axis PlanMill 60 S is built for dental laboratories. Prices: see the vendor's site.

Best for: practices that already use Planmeca imaging or software, or want both wet and dry milling in one unit.

Glidewell fastmill.io

Glidewell sells an in-office workflow called glidewell.io, made up of the fastscan.io scanner (Medit hardware, with training and zero monthly fees), the fastmill.io mill and the fastprint.io 3D printer. Glidewell's page says the mill handles BruxZir NOW and other materials for everyday single-unit cases in under 40 minutes, with no oven needed, and that its Unlimited Lab Design service and on-demand technician support shorten the learning curve. The mill is described as an open system that works with your preferred scanner.

Best for: owners who want lab design help while they learn, and who already send work to Glidewell for the cases they will not mill.

SprintRay Midas and 3D printing

SprintRay's Midas is a chairside 3D printer for definitive crowns. SprintRay says it prints up to 3 definitive crowns in 10 minutes and that design, printing and post cure can take under 30 minutes. Printing is a different material path from milling: it uses highly filled composite resins rather than ceramic blocks.

SprintRay's own cost article for its Pro 2 printer puts a complete system at $20,000 and lists Ceramic Crown resin at $490 per 1 kg bottle, which it works out to about $4 to $6 of resin per crown (SprintRay, January 5, 2026). Those are the maker's own estimates.

Best for: practices that want in-house crowns, guides and night guards at a lower start cost, and are comfortable with composite crowns for the right cases.

Scanners: the first step either way

You do not need a mill to go digital. A scanner alone cuts impression material, remakes from distorted impressions and shipping, and many labs charge less for model-free digital cases. Glidewell's price list, for example, shows a model-free digital BruxZir crown at $89 against $109 for the standard case, and a $20 per unit deduction for digital monolithic cases without model work.

3Shape publishes US prices: TRIOS 5 from $25,900, or from $531 a month, excluding tax and shipping. Align Technology (iTero) and Medit pricing: see the vendor's site. Glidewell bundles a Medit scanner in its fastscan.io package.

If your crown volume is modest, a scanner plus a lab that rewards digital cases may capture much of the saving without the mill payment.

The outside lab

Glidewell

Glidewell publishes a flat per unit price list for its lab. On November 26, 2026 it listed BruxZir Full-Strength Zirconia at $109 per unit ($89 model-free digital), BruxZir Esthetic at $119 ($99 model-free digital), IPS e.max posterior at $129 ($109 model-free digital) and IPS e.max anterior at $149. Its in-lab times are listed at 3 to 5 working days depending on the product.

Best for: practices that want a known per unit fee and a large menu of materials and implant options.

Your local lab

A local lab often wins on shade matching, anterior esthetics, complex implant work and the ability to talk to the technician. Fees vary by lab and region, so ask for a written fee schedule, then compare it line by line with your last 12 months of invoices.

Before buying a mill, negotiate with your current lab first. Consolidating volume, sending digital scans and agreeing on remake terms can lower your per unit cost without any equipment.

Payback math (illustrative only)

The numbers below are made up to show the method. They are not quotes or benchmarks. Use your own lab invoices, your own equipment quote and your own collections.

  1. Monthly fixed cost. Equipment payment, software and service plan. Say $3,000 a month.
  2. Lab fee saved per crown. Say $129 per unit.
  3. Variable cost per milled crown. Block, burs and other consumables. Say $30.
  4. Saving per crown. $129 minus $30 is $99.
  5. Break even. $3,000 divided by $99 is about 30 single-unit crowns a month.

Then adjust for what the formula leaves out:

  1. Chair and assistant time. A same day crown keeps the patient in the chair longer. If that time would otherwise be used for other production, count it as a cost.
  2. Saved appointments. No second seat appointment and no temporary can free chair time, which works in milling's favour.
  3. Cases you will still send out. Bridges, implants and esthetic anterior work may stay at the lab, so count only the crowns you would really mill.
  4. Remakes and learning curve. Expect lower volume in the first months.

If your realistic monthly single-unit volume sits well above the break even, milling likely pays for itself during the term. If it sits near or below it, a scanner plus a lab, or a printer, may be the better buy.

Tax timing

Section 179 and bonus depreciation can let you deduct much of the cost in the year the equipment is placed in service, depending on your income and current rules. A deduction lowers your tax bill. It does not make an unused mill pay for itself. See our guide to tax deductions for medical practice owners. This is general information, not tax advice.

How to choose

  1. Fewer single-unit crowns than your break even: keep the lab, add a scanner, and ask for model-free digital pricing.
  2. Steady single-unit volume and a trained assistant: get CEREC, Planmeca and Glidewell fastmill.io quotes on the same terms.
  3. You want lab design help while you learn: look at Glidewell fastmill.io.
  4. Lower start cost and a mix of crowns, guides and night guards: look at SprintRay printing.
  5. Esthetic or complex implant cases: keep a strong local lab whatever you buy.
  6. Several offices: model each office's volume separately before putting a mill in every one.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is CEREC worth it for a general dental practice?

It can be, if you place enough single-unit crowns each month to cover the monthly cost of the system after materials. Get a full written quote, then divide the monthly cost by what you save per crown. If your schedule clears that number with room to spare, it is worth a closer look.

How many crowns a month do I need to justify a mill?

It depends on your equipment cost and your lab fees. Divide the monthly payment, software and service by the lab fee you save per crown minus the block and consumables. Our illustrative example came out at about 30 crowns a month, but your number will differ.

What does a dental lab charge per crown?

Fees vary by lab, material and region. Glidewell's published list on November 26, 2026 showed BruxZir zirconia at $109 per unit, $89 for model-free digital cases, and IPS e.max posterior at $129. Ask your own lab for a written fee schedule.

Is a 3D printed crown a real alternative to milling?

For some cases. SprintRay's Midas prints definitive composite crowns, and SprintRay says up to 3 crowns print in 10 minutes. Material and indications differ from milled ceramic, so check the cases you plan to treat with the maker's indications.

Should I buy a scanner before a mill?

Usually yes. A scanner lowers impression and remake costs and can lower lab fees for model-free digital cases. It also tells you how many of your cases are simple single units before you commit to a mill.

Does milling in house save chair time?

It removes the second seat appointment and the temporary crown, but the first visit gets longer while you design and mill. Whether that is a net gain depends on how full your schedule is.

How do I book an in-house mill?

Record the system as equipment and depreciate it, record the loan or lease separately, and book blocks and consumables as supplies. Track lab fees and milling costs side by side so you can see the real saving. See best bookkeeping for dental practices.
