Should I sell my practice to private equity?

Short answer

Selling to private equity tends to make sense if you are within about five to ten years of retiring, your specialty still draws platform level prices, and you can accept lower pay and less control for a large payment now. It tends to make less sense if you plan to work for decades, because you carry the pay cut longest. The headline multiple is only the start: cash at closing, rollover equity, earnouts, the employment agreement and taxes decide what you actually keep. Compare any offer with selling to a hospital or partner, joining an MSO, and staying independent, using a model of your own numbers.

How a PE practice deal works

Most states bar investors from owning a medical practice outright. So the investor buys the business side through a management services organization (MSO), a licensed owner keeps the clinical entity, and the two sign a long term management agreement. Foley and Lardner (January 2025) describes the price as cash plus rollover equity in the new structure.

Here is the usual sequence:

  1. The buyer measures your EBITDA, after adjusting owner pay down to a market level. That pay reduction is called the scrape.
  2. The buyer offers a multiple of that adjusted EBITDA.
  3. You receive part of the price in cash and part as rollover equity in the buyer's holding company. Some deals add an earnout, escrow or holdback.
  4. You sign an employment agreement, often three to five years, with a non compete.
  5. The investor plans to sell the platform in three to seven years, according to Medical Economics. If it sells for more, your rollover pays a second time. Owners call this the second bite of the apple.

Platform or add-on: the first question to ask

A platform is the first practice an investor buys in a specialty or region, and everything else is added to it. An add-on joins an existing platform. Sikich, citing PitchBook (October 2026), says platforms command a four to eight turn premium over add-ons.

The market has cooled. PitchBook data reported by STAT show practice management deals fell from 851 in 2021 to 105 in the first half of 2026. Sikich counted 105 practice deals in the third quarter of 2026, down from 137 a year earlier. MedMoneyGuide links the drop to new state laws, including California SB 351 and AB 1415 and Oregon SB 951, and reports that buyers are asking for larger rollover stakes and more earnouts. For how value is set in general, read how a medical practice is valued.

Rollover equity

Rollover equity is the part of your price paid as ownership in the buyer's holding company instead of cash. Stout, cited by MedMoneyGuide, puts it typically at 20% to 40% of total consideration. Foley says it can reach 40% of value in larger deals.

It can pay off, and it can also lose value. Nixon Peabody (May 2026) says its real value depends on governance rights, dilution protections, the capital structure and the timing of any future exit. Ask:

  1. Which entity issues my units, and what class are they?
  2. How much debt sits above my equity?
  3. Can new investors dilute me, and do I get information rights?
  4. What happens to my units if I leave, retire or am terminated?
  5. Can I be forced to sell, or blocked from selling?

Earnouts, escrow and holdbacks

An earnout pays part of the price later, only if the practice hits targets such as revenue or EBITDA, usually over one to three years. SRS Acquiom data, as reported by Lartico Capital, show earnouts appeared in 37% of 2025 healthcare services deals, and that across deals outside life sciences only 21% of the maximum earnout dollars were paid. Value an earnout at well below its face amount.

Escrow and holdbacks keep part of the cash back for a period, often 12 to 24 months, to cover claims under the purchase agreement. Ask what triggers a claim and when the money is released.

Pay and employment terms

The scrape is how the buyer creates the earnings it pays for. A Qubera Wealth Management guide (September 2026) puts the typical cut at 20% to 30% of pay. Medical Economics advises owners to ask any partner for proof of "income repair", the plan to bring pay back up through better payer rates, ancillaries or growth.

A made up example. Two owners take $1,500,000 a year combined. A 25% scrape turns $375,000 of pay into EBITDA. At 9x, that adds $3,375,000 to the price: $2,362,500 in cash at 70% and $1,012,500 in rollover at 30%. Pay drops to $1,125,000 a year. This is an illustration, not a client result or a typical offer.

Read the employment agreement as closely as the price. Look at the term, the pay formula and productivity targets, call coverage, who controls your schedule and staff, termination for cause or without cause, and the non compete. Medical Economics notes that a non compete inside a rollover agreement can last longer than the one in your employment agreement, and several states now limit non competes.

Tax treatment, in general terms

Taxes can move your net proceeds more than a turn of multiple. The points below are general. Review your own deal with your CPA and tax attorney before you sign the letter of intent.

  1. Asset or equity sale. Medical Economics (September 2025) says most practice sales are asset sales, which buyers prefer.
  2. Purchase price allocation. Goodwill is generally taxed at long term capital gains rates. Payments for a non compete, collected receivables and recaptured depreciation on equipment are generally ordinary income.
  3. Personal goodwill. For a practice taxed as a C corporation, goodwill that belongs to you personally may be sold separately and avoid a second layer of tax, if it is documented properly.
  4. Rollover equity. It can be tax deferred if it is structured correctly. If not, you may owe tax on equity you cannot sell. Foley notes S corporation practices need special care in how the rollover is held.
  5. Earnouts. They may be taxed as sale price or reclassified as pay, depending on how they are written. The installment method may defer tax until you are paid, but it is not always available.
  6. Post sale pay. Your salary under the employment agreement is ordinary income.
  7. Entity history. A C corporation that converted to an S corporation may owe corporate level tax on built in gains for up to five years after converting.
  8. State taxes. Where you live and where the practice sits both matter.

Flagged for CPA review: every item above depends on your entity type, state and the final paperwork.

Questions to ask any buyer

  1. Am I a platform or an add-on?
  2. What adjusted EBITDA are you pricing, and which add backs did you accept?
  3. What is the split between cash, rollover, earnout and escrow?
  4. What will my pay be in year one, and what is your record on income repair?
  5. When do you expect to sell the platform, and what did past rollover holders receive?
  6. Can I talk to owners who sold to you two or more years ago?
  7. Who decides on staffing, scheduling, payer contracts and capital spending after closing?
  8. How do current state notice and ownership laws affect this structure and timeline?

Warning signs

  1. Pressure to sign a letter of intent with long exclusivity before you understand the terms.
  2. A high headline price that rests mostly on an earnout tied to EBITDA the buyer will control.
  3. Vague answers about the rollover units, debt load or dilution.
  4. A non compete that runs well past your employment term.
  5. No references from earlier sellers.
  6. A pay plan that does not show how income repair would happen.
  7. Your own books cannot show your EBITDA before the buyer does. That puts you on their numbers.

How to choose by specialty

  1. Dermatology. Buyers value medical, Mohs, pathology, cosmetic and retail revenue differently, so report them separately. See dermatology.
  2. Orthopedics. Surgery center, imaging and therapy ownership can add turns. Know which ancillaries are in the deal. See orthopedics.
  3. Ophthalmology. Retina, surgery centers and optical drive value; be clear about how each is included. See ophthalmology.
  4. Plastic surgery. A thinner buyer pool and dependence on one surgeon's brand can mean lower multiples and more earnout. See plastic surgery.
  5. Medical groups with several locations or owners. Each owner's age and goals differ, so model the deal owner by owner. See medical groups.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Should I sell my practice to private equity?

It often fits owners within five to ten years of retiring, in specialties where buyers still pay platform prices, who accept lower pay and less control. Younger owners carry the pay cut longest. Model the offer after tax against staying independent before you decide.

What is a typical EBITDA multiple for a practice sale in 2026?

Published ranges vary by specialty and size, from about 3x to 10x for plastic surgery to 12x to 15x for large dermatology platforms. The median healthcare M&A multiple fell from 14.5x in 2024 to 11.6x in 2026 to date, per PitchBook data cited by Sikich.

What is rollover equity?

It is part of your price paid as ownership in the buyer's company instead of cash, typically 20% to 40%. It can pay a second time when the platform sells, or lose value. Its worth depends on the terms, debt and exit timing.

How much will my pay drop after a PE sale?

Sources put the scrape at roughly 20% to 30% of owner pay. The buyer should show how pay recovers over time.

Are earnouts worth it?

Treat them as uncertain. SRS Acquiom data show only about a fifth of maximum earnout dollars were paid outside life sciences. Prefer cash at closing and metrics you can see.

How is a practice sale taxed?

It depends on the deal structure and price allocation. Goodwill is generally capital gain, while non compete payments, receivables and post sale pay are generally ordinary income. Rollover can be tax deferred if structured correctly. Review with your CPA.
