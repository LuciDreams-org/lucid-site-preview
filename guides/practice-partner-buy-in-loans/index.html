How do I finance a practice partner buy-in?

Short answer

Most new partners finance a buy-in with a bank or specialist buy-in loan, by paying through the group with reduced pay over several years, or both. Panacea Financial, Doc2Doc Lending, U.S. Bank, PNC and Live Oak Bank all list buy-ins or partner buyouts among their loan uses, and SBA 7(a) can fund a partial change of ownership. The price is usually your share of the group's tangible assets, receivables and goodwill, so ask for the valuation and three years of financials, then test whether your added income as a partner covers the payment with room to spare.

How buy-ins are priced

There is no single formula. ACP's practice management guide on partner buy-ins names three parts of practice value: tangible assets (cash, furniture, equipment), accounts receivable (money owed for care already given) and goodwill (expected future earning power). In theory, your price is the sum of the three times the share you are buying.

Groups handle the hard part, goodwill, in different ways. ACP lists:

  1. A full outside valuation each time a partner buys in.
  2. Rules of thumb or comparable sales data for goodwill.
  3. A preset amount paid through salary reductions over several years.
  4. Receivables only, with goodwill and tangible assets left out.
  5. Tangible assets and receivables, with goodwill left out.

ACP's own example: a group of four values the practice at $2 million, including goodwill, and adds a fifth owner, who pays $400,000, one fifth of the value.

Two things to check in any offer. First, how receivables are counted. A practice that keeps cash basis books has receivables that never appear on its balance sheet, so ask for an AR aging report (see cash vs accrual accounting for medical practices). Second, what the buy-in actually buys. Real estate, a surgery center or imaging may sit in separate entities with their own buy-ins. For valuation methods, see how is a medical practice valued and the best medical practice valuation firms.

Bank buy-in loans

Several practice lenders make buy-in loans as a separate product.

  1. Panacea Financial, a division of Primis Bank, offers a partner buy-in loan that it says "does not require a lien on the existing practice, real estate, or equipment," and advertises same day approvals.
  2. U.S. Bank lists buying into a partnership among the uses of its medical practice loans, which run up to 15 years with up to 100 percent financing options.
  3. PNC lists buy-ins among practice loan uses. It does not publish amounts or terms.
  4. Live Oak Bank lends for acquisitions and partner buyouts, and its healthcare team profiles describe buy-in and buy-out experience.
  5. Heritage Bank NA, a community bank, runs a partnership buy-in program that can finance up to 100 percent of a new partner's buy-in with no down payment or collateral from the new partner. Its listed requirements show what lenders often ask for: a business valuation, a corporate guarantee from the practice, a documented buy-sell agreement where the deal needs one, life insurance equal to the loan assigned to the bank, a 10 year maximum term and at least 3 years in business.

Where bank loans are strong: you own your full share from day one and pay a fixed schedule. Limits: the group often guarantees the loan, so your partners have to agree to it. More lender detail is in best medical practice loan lenders.

Specialist buy-in lenders

Doc2Doc Lending offers practice buy-in loans from $50,000 to $1 million in all 50 states, with up to 100 percent financing and no prepayment penalty. Its FAQ says buy-in loans run 5 years with 20 quarterly payments of principal plus accrued interest. For surgery center buy-ins, the center sends ownership distributions directly to the lender to pay the loan. It lists surgery centers, primary care, specialty groups and dental groups among the buy-ins it supports.

Where they are strong: underwriting built around the group's distributions. Limits: shorter terms mean bigger payments, so the distributions have to be real and steady.

SBA 7(a) for a partial change of ownership

SBA allows 7(a) loans for changes of ownership, complete or partial. Under SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026, a buy-in is an Owner Buyout with a partial change of ownership. The rules that matter:

  1. The practice and every new owner must be co-borrowers on the loan, and at least one original owner stays and guarantees it.
  2. A person not already employed by the practice can buy less than 50 percent and cannot become the largest owner.
  3. Equity is 10 percent of the purchase price, which the lender may reduce if the practice has enough liquidity.
  4. Debt service coverage must be at least 1.25 times.
  5. The selling partner may stay as an owner or employee.

Bank of America's SBA page lists terms up to 10 years for a business acquisition or partner buyout. SBA suits larger stakes when a conventional loan falls short, but it puts the practice on the loan, so the whole group is involved. Compare lenders in best SBA lenders for healthcare practices.

Paying through the group

Many groups skip the loan. ACP describes a phased buy-in where the new owner takes a reduced share of owner income for several years, usually three to five, for example 60 percent of a full share in year one, rising to 90 percent by year four. Some groups set a price and let you pay it over three to five years, with or without interest.

Where it is strong: no bank, no personal loan, and the payment flexes with the group's profit. Limits: the terms are set by the people you are buying from, and a weak year can stretch your path to a full share.

Note to the partners and cash

The existing partners can carry a note for part of the price, which closes a gap between the price and what a bank will lend. Cash from savings avoids interest but drains the reserve you may need for a home, taxes or the next buy-in. Put the rate, schedule and what happens if you leave early in the paperwork.

Is the buy-in a good deal?

Run the payback before you sign. Here is an illustrative example, not a quote.

  1. Price: $300,000, financed over 5 years at an assumed 8 percent.
  2. Payment: about $6,083 a month, or about $73,000 a year. Total interest about $65,000.
  3. Test: compare the payment with the extra income partnership brings, after tax. If partnership raises your pay by $150,000 before tax and an assumed 40 percent goes to tax, about $90,000 is left, which covers the payment with about $17,000 to spare.

Then stress it. What if distributions fall 20 percent, a payer cuts rates, or a senior partner retires? Ask for three years of financial statements, the distribution history by partner, the operating or shareholder agreement, the buy-sell terms and how a partner who leaves is paid out. The checklist lenders use is in what lenders look for in a practice loan.

Tax and book treatment (flag for CPA review)

These are general points. Your entity type and paperwork decide the answer, so a CPA should review every buy-in.

  1. Your side. ACP notes that the new owner gets no tax deduction for buying shares. The price becomes your basis in the shares or partnership interest.
  2. Loan interest. IRS Schedule E instructions say interest on a loan used to buy into a partnership or S corporation is allocated among the entity's assets by a reasonable method. The part tied to business assets is reported as business interest.
  3. The selling partners. ACP notes that selling owners report any gain on the shares they sell as a capital gain. In a partnership, IRS Publication 541 has special rules for payments tied to unrealized receivables, which matter for practices with large unbilled or uncollected AR.
  4. Paying through reduced pay. A salary reduction buy-in is effectively paid with pre-tax dollars, which changes the math against a loan paid from after-tax income.
  5. The group's books. A new owner changes capital accounts in a partnership or stock records in a corporation, profit allocations, K-1s or W-2 pay, and the buy-sell funding, including any life insurance a lender requires.

How to choose

  1. A fair price and steady distributions: a bank buy-in loan, ideally one that needs no lien on the practice.
  2. A surgery center or ambulatory group stake: a specialist buy-in lender that is repaid from distributions.
  3. A large stake where conventional loans fall short: SBA 7(a), if your partners accept co-borrowing.
  4. Partners who prefer no outside debt: paying through reduced pay, with the formula in writing.
  5. A price gap: a note to the partners on top of a bank loan.
  6. Multi location groups or groups with several entities: map which entities the buy-in covers first. See our medical group page.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How much does a partner buy-in usually cost?

It depends on the group's value and your share. ACP's example is a practice valued at $2 million adding a fifth owner, who pays $400,000. Ask how tangible assets, receivables and goodwill were each valued.

Can I get 100 percent financing for a buy-in?

Some lenders advertise it. Doc2Doc Lending and Heritage Bank NA both publish up to 100 percent financing for buy-ins as of November 26, 2026. Approval still depends on the group's numbers and your credit.

Do my partners have to guarantee my buy-in loan?

Often, yes. Some bank programs require a corporate guarantee from the practice, and SBA loans make the practice a co-borrower. Panacea markets a buy-in loan that needs no lien on the practice.

Can I use an SBA loan for a buy-in?

Yes, as a partial change of ownership. The practice and the new owner are co-borrowers, an outside buyer must stay under 50 percent, and coverage must be at least 1.25 times.

Is a practice buy-in tax deductible?

The purchase price generally is not. It becomes your basis in the shares or interest. Interest on the loan may be deductible depending on how it is allocated, so ask your CPA.

Should I pay through reduced pay or take a loan?

Reduced pay avoids outside debt and is paid with pre-tax dollars, but the group sets the terms. A loan gives you a full share sooner. Compare the after-tax cost of both.

What should I ask for before agreeing to a buy-in?

Three years of financial statements and tax returns, distributions by partner, an AR aging report, the valuation, the operating or shareholder agreement and the buy-out terms if you leave.
