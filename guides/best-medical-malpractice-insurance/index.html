Which malpractice insurance is best for a group practice?

Short answer

There is no single best malpractice carrier: the right one depends on your state, specialty and group size. The largest national options are MedPro Group (Berkshire Hathaway), TDC (which completed its purchase of ProAssurance in June 2026), CNA, MagMutual, Coverys, Curi and Chubb, with strong regional and specialty carriers such as MLMIC in New York and TDIC for dental. Most groups choose claims-made coverage with a plan for tail, compare entity coverage and shared or separate limits, and get quotes through a broker who writes several carriers. Premiums vary more by county and specialty than by carrier, so compare like for like quotes.

Occurrence vs claims-made

This is the first decision, and it shapes cost for years.

  1. Occurrence. Covers incidents that happen while the policy is in force, no matter when the claim is filed, per the Texas Department of Insurance. You never need tail. Premiums start higher.
  2. Claims-made. Covers claims reported during the policy term for incidents after the retroactive date. TDC notes that claims-made policies are initially less expensive than occurrence policies. Premiums usually step up over the first several years as the policy matures.
  3. The catch. When a claims-made policy ends, you need tail coverage or prior acts (nose) coverage from the next carrier, or incidents from your covered years are left exposed.

Most private practices buy claims-made because the early years cost less. Groups that hire and lose clinicians often should, because the tail question comes up every time someone leaves.

Tail coverage: the cost owners forget

Tail, formally an extended reporting period endorsement, lets you report claims after a claims-made policy ends, for incidents that happened while it was active.

What it costs, as published:

  1. The Texas Department of Insurance says tail may cost one and a half to three times an annual premium.
  2. AMA guidance on renewals puts it at two to three times the premium.
  3. AMA Insurance says it can cost as much as two times the annual premium, usually as a one-time charge.

Ways to avoid or reduce it:

  1. Free tail at retirement. Many insurers offer it after continuous coverage for a stated period, usually five or more years, per the Texas Department of Insurance. TDC says it offers free tail to qualified members at retirement, disability or death.
  2. Prior acts coverage. Your new carrier sets a retroactive date that reaches back to your first policy, so no tail is needed from the old one.
  3. Paperwork terms. Who pays tail when a clinician leaves depends on the employment agreement. AMA Insurance lists employer pays, employee pays, or a split based on years of service.

Tail is a real liability at a sale, a merger or a partner exit. Buyers ask who pays it. See how to prepare a medical practice for sale.

The carriers, one by one

MedPro Group

MedPro calls itself a Berkshire Hathaway company and says it has written malpractice insurance since 1899. It lists coverage across the spectrum of healthcare, from solo practitioners to large groups, and cites an A++ rating from AM Best and a pure consent provision that lets you refuse to settle a claim. Best for: groups that want the largest carrier and national reach.

TDC

TDC describes itself as the largest malpractice insurer in the country owned by its member clinicians and says it is rated A+ by AM Best and A by Fitch. It completed its acquisition of ProAssurance on June 26, 2026, so ProAssurance and NORCAL policyholders now sit in the same group. Its Tribute Plan pays a loyalty award to members who retire at 55 or older after at least five years of continuous coverage. Best for: practices that value consent to settle and a retirement benefit.

CNA

CNA says it is a leading provider of professional liability for allied healthcare professionals, small businesses and practices, and it also writes for larger healthcare organizations. Best for: practices with nurse practitioners, therapists and other allied staff who want one carrier across roles.

MagMutual

MagMutual says it has provided medical liability insurance for more than four decades, with risk management, defense and learning tools for members. It ranked fourth in 2025 by premium. Best for: groups in its core states that want a mutual with strong risk education.

Coverys

Coverys says it has more than 45 years of experience protecting healthcare and is rated A (Excellent) by AM Best. Best for: groups and facilities that want a healthcare-only carrier with risk analytics.

Curi

Curi runs two businesses: Curi Insurance for malpractice and general liability, and Curi Advisory for business and clinical consulting. It also lists alternative insurance options for larger groups. Best for: practices that want insurance and practice advisory from one relationship.

Chubb

Chubb writes healthcare liability for hospitals, health systems and providers alongside property, cyber and other commercial lines. Best for: larger groups that want malpractice and business coverage from one global insurer.

Regional and specialty carriers

  1. MLMIC. Part of the Berkshire Hathaway family and, by its own description, the largest malpractice insurer in New York, covering medical practices, dentists and hospitals.
  2. TDIC. Founded by dentists, insures only dentists, with more than 24,000 dentist policyholders. A natural first quote for dental groups. See our dental page.
  3. State and specialty mutuals. Many states have a local mutual with deep courtroom history in that state. A broker can tell you which ones write your specialty.

Risk retention groups and group options

Risk retention groups (RRGs)

An RRG is a liability insurer owned by its members, formed under the federal Liability Risk Retention Act of 1986, per the NAIC. It is licensed in one state and can write in others.

Two things to know:

  1. No guaranty fund. The NAIC says the law specifically precludes RRGs from state guaranty funds. In Texas, for example, a licensed insurer's claims are backed by the guaranty association for up to $300,000 per claim if the carrier fails; an RRG's are not.
  2. Less rate regulation. The Texas Department of Insurance notes that RRG rates and policy forms are not regulated there.

RRGs can be a good fit for a specialty that the standard market prices high. Check the RRG's financial statements, rating and reinsurance before you buy.

Group policies and entity coverage

A group can insure each clinician separately or buy one group policy. Points to compare in every quote:

  1. Entity coverage. The practice itself can be named in a suit. Make sure the corporation or LLC is covered, not only the clinicians.
  2. Shared or separate limits. With shared limits, one large claim can use up the limit for everyone. Separate limits cost more but protect each clinician.
  3. Defense costs. The AMA notes that some carriers put defense costs inside the $1 million and $3 million limits. Outside is better.
  4. Consent to settle. Both the AMA and TDC advise looking for it.
  5. Financial strength. The AMA suggests at least an A minus rating from AM Best.
  6. Captives. Large groups sometimes retain part of the risk through a captive or a self-insured retention. That needs actuarial and legal help.

How to choose

  1. Small group in one state. Get three quotes through a broker: one national carrier, one regional mutual, and one specialty option.
  2. Group that hires often. Claims-made with prior acts for new hires, and clear tail terms in every employment agreement.
  3. Surgical and procedural groups. Look at higher limits and defense outside limits. See our orthopedics page.
  4. Dental groups. Start with TDIC and a dental program from a national carrier.
  5. Several locations or states. Check that the carrier writes every state you work in, and that the entity is covered at each site. See our medical group page.
  6. Primary care. Premiums are lower than surgical lines, so service, consent and tail terms often matter more than price. See our primary care page.
  7. Planning a sale or merger. Price tail for every owner before you sign a letter of intent.

Start the process early. The AMA suggests three to four months before renewal; the Texas Department of Insurance says about six.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is claims-made or occurrence better for a group practice?

Claims-made is cheaper in the early years and is what most groups buy. Occurrence costs more but never needs tail. If your group has frequent turnover, claims-made plus clear tail rules in your paperwork is the common answer.

How much does tail coverage cost?

Published guidance ranges from about one and a half to three times the annual premium, paid once. Free tail at retirement is common after five or more years with one carrier.

Who pays tail when a clinician leaves?

It depends on the employment agreement. Common terms are the employer pays, the clinician pays, or the cost is split based on years of service or the reason for leaving.

What limits does a practice need?

$1 million per claim and $3 million aggregate is the most common limit and a typical starting point. Surgeons and proceduralists, and some hospital credentialing rules, may call for more.

Is a risk retention group safe?

Many are well run, but RRGs are not covered by state guaranty funds. Review the group's financial statements, rating and reinsurance, and compare with a licensed carrier quote.

Why did my premium go up when I did not have a claim?

Premiums follow state and specialty trends, and the AMA reports seven straight years of rising premiums through 2025. A claims-made policy also steps up as it matures over its early years.

Should the practice entity have its own coverage?

Yes, in most cases. A suit can name the practice as well as the clinician, so confirm the entity is covered and whether it shares limits with the clinicians.

Do I need a broker?

A broker who writes several malpractice carriers can compare quotes, entity terms and tail options. Some carriers also sell directly.
