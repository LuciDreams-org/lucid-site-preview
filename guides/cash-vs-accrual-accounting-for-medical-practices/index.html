Cash or accrual: which should a medical practice use?

Short answer

Cash accounting records income when money arrives and expenses when you pay them. Accrual accounting records income when you earn it and expenses when you incur them, whether or not cash has moved. Many small practices are allowed to file taxes on the cash method, and many still choose accrual for their monthly management reports because it shows what the practice actually earned and owes. The method used on the tax return is a decision for the practice's CPA, based on entity type, size and IRS rules in IRS Publication 538. This guide is general information, not tax advice.

How each method works

The IRS describes the two methods in plain terms. Under the cash method, you generally report income in the tax year you receive it. Under the accrual method, you generally report income in the tax year you earn it. On the expense side, cash generally deducts expenses in the year you pay them, and accrual in the year you incur them. A third option, a hybrid or combination method, mixes elements of both and has its own rules.

A medical practice shows the difference clearly because it is paid late. Here is an illustration with made up numbers, not client data.

  1. In December a practice provides care and bills payers $50,000. Payers pay in late January.
  2. In December it also pays $30,000 in staff wages and receives a $5,000 supplier invoice due in January.
Cash viewAccrual view
December revenue$0$50,000
December expenses$30,000$35,000
December resultA loss of $30,000A profit of $15,000
January resultA large profit when payers payShows the January costs only

Neither view is wrong. They answer different questions. The cash view says how much money moved. The accrual view says how the practice performed in the month it did the work. For a practice where payers pay weeks after the visit, the gap between the two can swing monthly profit by tens of thousands of dollars.

What the IRS says about who can use which method

Publication 538 covers accounting periods and methods. Key points from the IRS text, based on the January 2022 revision on irs.gov:

  1. You choose an accounting method when you file your first tax return, and you must generally use the same method from year to year.
  2. Corporations other than S corporations, and partnerships that have a corporation (other than an S corporation) as a partner, generally cannot use the cash method unless they meet a gross receipts test. The test is an average of annual gross receipts for the three prior tax years, at or under an amount indexed for inflation. The January 2022 revision shows $26 million. Confirm the current year amount with your CPA.
  3. Tax shelters cannot use the cash method.
  4. There is an exception for qualified personal service corporations. Publication 538 says a corporation that meets its function and ownership tests can use the cash method, and the function test covers services performed in the fields of health. Ask your CPA whether your practice meets the ownership test.
  5. If you do not meet the rules, you must change to an accrual method.
  6. To change a method later, you must generally get IRS approval, requested on Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method.

The gross receipts test applies to the corporations and partnerships described above. Sole proprietors, S corporations and partnerships with only individual partners are not subject to that test, though other limits can apply, such as the tax shelter rule. Rules differ by entity and by facts, so do not rely on a general guide. Your CPA decides which method applies to your practice and signs off on any change.

Why practices choose cash for tax

There are good reasons that many smaller practices file on the cash method.

  1. It is simple. Income is what hit the bank.
  2. It can defer tax, because income you have billed but not collected at year end is not taxed until you are paid.
  3. It matches how most owners think about the business: what is in the account.

The cost of cash accounting shows up in management, not on the return. A practice can look weak in a month when payers are slow and strong in a month with a catch up. Cash books do not show how much patients and payers owe the practice, or what the practice owes vendors, unless someone tracks it separately.

Why accrual is worth keeping even when you file on cash

A practice that files on the cash method can still ask for accrual management reports. IRS Publication 334 says you must use the same accounting method to figure your taxable income and to keep your books, so ask your CPA how supplemental accrual reports should sit alongside the books behind your return. Several concrete benefits follow.

  1. Accounts receivable become visible. Accrual books carry what payers and patients owe, so an aging report can show slow payers and write off trends. Lucid reports AR aging as part of its reporting and review work, using the data from your billing system.
  2. Revenue lands in the month of care. Month to month profit stops swinging with payer timing.
  3. Unpaid bills and payroll are counted. Expenses you owe but have not paid appear in the right month, so you do not mistake a payment delay for profit.
  4. Prepaid revenue is handled properly. Membership and package revenue is recognized over the period it covers instead of when the money arrives. This matters for med spas, direct care and concierge models. See the med spa and primary care pages.
  5. Locations and providers can be compared. Profit by location or provider only means something when revenue and costs are matched to the same period.
  6. Outsiders ask for it. Lenders, partners and buyers usually want accrual financials.

That last point deserves a closer look.

Buyers, lenders and partners prefer accrual

When a practice borrows, brings in a partner or sells, outsiders judge it on accrual numbers. A buyer wants to see what the practice earned in each month, not when cash happened to land. Public valuation guidance says the same: GATP Solutions, an accounting firm, writes that many practices keep cash basis books but buyers price on accrual numbers, and it recommends making the switch for tax at least two years before a sale. That is an advisor's recommendation, not an IRS rule, so ask your CPA about timing for your facts.

A practice that has kept accrual management reports all along can hand a buyer or lender monthly statements that tie to the tax return. A practice that has not may spend months rebuilding them under deadline pressure. See how a medical practice is valued for what buyers check, and part time CFO costs for the planning work that depends on accrual numbers.

How to decide, step by step

  1. Confirm your entity type and your three year average gross receipts. A CPA can tell you in minutes whether the cash method is open to you.
  2. Ask your CPA which method your return uses today and whether a change makes sense. Do not switch on your own, since a change generally needs IRS approval on Form 3115.
  3. Decide what your monthly reports should show. If you want profit by location, AR aging or a forecast, ask your CPA about accrual management reports even if the return stays on cash, and how they fit with the books behind the return.
  4. Check what your lender, partners or likely buyers expect.
  5. Make sure your bookkeeper or bookkeeping service can produce the method you pick, month after month.

What changing from cash to accrual involves

A switch is mostly a bookkeeping project and a tax filing. The bookkeeping side means setting up receivables, payables, accrued payroll and deferred revenue, and counting what the practice was owed and owed at the switch date. The tax side generally means a Form 3115 filing and often a one time adjustment for income that was not yet taxed. The size and timing of that adjustment depend on the practice, so a CPA should model it before you commit.

One option to discuss with your CPA is to keep the tax method as is and add accrual management reports first. That can give you the benefits in the list above without a tax method change, but confirm it against the IRS rule that books and taxable income use the same method.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Should a medical practice use cash or accrual accounting?

It depends on the entity, the size and the goal. Many small practices file on the cash method, while accrual gives a truer picture of monthly performance. The practice's CPA decides the tax method under IRS Publication 538. Many practices keep accrual style management reports either way.

Can a medical practice file taxes on the cash method?

Often yes. Sole proprietors, S corporations and partnerships with only individual partners are generally not subject to the gross receipts test. Some corporations must use accrual unless they meet that test or an exception such as the qualified personal service corporation rule. Your CPA confirms what applies to your entity.

What is the main difference for a practice with slow insurance payers?

Cash records revenue when payers pay, accrual when you provide the care. With accrual, the money owed to you shows up as accounts receivable. A slow payer then appears on an aging report instead of looking like a quiet month.

How do I switch from cash to accrual?

A change in the tax method generally needs IRS approval, requested on Form 3115. The practice's CPA should plan the timing and model the tax effect first. You can also start accrual management books without changing the tax method.

Do buyers and lenders want accrual books?

Usually, yes. Outsiders judge a practice on accrual numbers because they show earnings in the month of care. One accounting firm recommends making the switch at least two years before a sale. Ask your CPA what fits your plans.
