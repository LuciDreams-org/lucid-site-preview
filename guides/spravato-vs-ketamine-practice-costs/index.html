Short answer

Spravato usually costs a practice more to run, because the FDA's REMS program requires a certified site and at least two hours of monitoring per dose, and buying the drug yourself ties up cash before payers pay. IV ketamine is simpler to bill because most patients pay cash, often about $450 a session, but the practice carries the staff, room and recovery time and sells against at-home ketamine services. A Spravato program can earn more per session when the drug is bought and billed and claims are paid, while getting the drug through a specialty pharmacy cuts the cash risk and the margin. Model both on your own payer mix, staffing and room count before you commit.

What Spravato requires before the first dose

Spravato (esketamine nasal spray) is only available through the FDA's Spravato REMS, a safety program the FDA last updated on June 24, 2026. The REMS objective is that patients are monitored for at least two hours and until sedation, dissociation and breathing effects resolve.

For an outpatient practice, the enrollment form asks the setting to agree, among other points, to:

  1. Have a prescriber onsite during administration and monitoring.
  2. Have staff and a pulse oximeter onsite to monitor patients.
  3. Complete REMS paperwork for each patient and each session, including a patient monitoring form for outpatient use.

The business meaning is simple. Every Spravato session holds a chair and a monitoring staff member for two hours or more, plus check-in and dosing time. A prescriber has to be in the building. That time is the main cost of the service, whatever the payer pays.

Buy and bill or specialty pharmacy

The maker's site for treatment centers describes two ways to get the drug.

Buy and bill. The practice buys Spravato from an authorized specialty distributor and bills the payer for the drug and the visit, usually under the medical benefit. The maker notes that this pathway requires a benefits check and prior authorization before you buy and store the product. You control timing and supply, but you pay for each dose before payment arrives.

Specialty pharmacy. A REMS-certified specialty pharmacy ships the drug for a specific patient, usually under the pharmacy benefit. The practice does not buy the drug, but has to coordinate each shipment before the session. You bill for the visit and monitoring time only.

Many practices use both, because the patient's benefit design decides which path a plan allows. One Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan document, for example, says only buy and bill can be used for its Medicare Advantage members.

Reimbursement, in general terms

Medicare pays for Spravato with two codes that bundle the drug, the visit and the two hours of observation into one payment when the practice supplies the drug: G2082 for doses up to 56 mg and G2083 for doses above 56 mg. JotPsych, working from CMS 2026 fee schedule files, reports national 2026 rates of $952.59 for G2082 and $1,356.08 for G2083, with nurse practitioners paid 85% of those amounts ($809.70 and $1,152.67). When the practice monitors but does not supply the drug, Medicare billing moves to an office visit plus prolonged service codes.

The drug itself is expensive. Signal Hill Strategies, using Medicaid's national average drug acquisition cost (NADAC) data, reports that one 84 mg dose cost retail pharmacies $1,288 under the October 21, 2026 survey, up 15.7% from December 2024. It also reports that in 29 of 109 Medicare payment localities, the 2026 payment for an 84 mg Spravato visit falls below that drug cost alone.

What this means for cash: on Medicare, a buy-and-bill 84 mg session can leave little room after the drug, before you pay the staff who watch the patient. Commercial plans pay on their own schedules, usually with prior authorization. Codes also change. Iowa Medicaid, for example, moved Spravato claims to HCPCS code J0013 on January 1, 2026. Your biller handles codes and claims. See our guide to mental health billing services.

Ketamine: the cash-pay side

Ketamine for depression is mostly a cash business. One clinic directory, Healing Maps, says insurance covers it for fewer than 5% of patients.

Published prices, checked in late 2026:

  1. KetamineHQ checked 412 U.S. ketamine practices and found only 120 publish a price. The median IV infusion was $450 (middle half $397 to $550), a six-infusion series $2,730, and an IM injection $425.
  2. Signal Hill Strategies found a median posted IV price of $475 across 29 clinics in 18 metros in October 2026, with six-infusion series at a median of $2,925.
  3. At-home competition is cheaper per session. Signal Hill reports new Mindbloom clients paying $165 to $215 per at-home session in October 2026.

Tovani Health, which runs an at-home ketamine practice, lists going rates of $400 to $800 per IV session, $300 to $500 for an initial consultation and $150 to $250 for follow-ups. It also says a patient spends 45 to 90 minutes reclined in the treatment room, and that most practices overestimate how many sessions a clinic day supports by 30% to 50% in year one.

The cash-pay model has real advantages: no prior authorization, payment at the time of service and no drug float. The costs sit elsewhere. You need IV-trained staff, a vitals monitor, a quiet treatment room and time for recovery. Prepaid series also create a liability. Money collected for sessions not yet delivered is not yet earned, and refunds come out of the same bank account.

Staffing and room time

Room time decides capacity for both services. Use these as planning assumptions, not rules:

  1. Spravato: about two and a half hours of chair time per session, counting check-in, dosing and at least two hours of monitoring.
  2. IV ketamine: about one and a half to two hours of chair time, counting setup, infusion and recovery.
  3. Staff: a monitoring nurse or medical assistant can watch more than one patient at a time in many practices, but your clinicians and counsel set the ratio, and state rules vary.
  4. Prescriber time: Spravato needs a prescriber onsite for every session. Ketamine supervision rules depend on your state and protocol.

One eight-hour chair therefore holds about three Spravato sessions or four to five ketamine sessions a day. If you already treat TMS patients, those chairs and staff may compete for the same rooms. See our guide to choosing a TMS machine for the device side.

How the numbers work. Buy and bill earns the most per session, but one denied dose loses the $1,300 drug plus the staff time, which wipes out the margin of almost three paid sessions. The specialty pharmacy path earns less but puts no drug cash at risk.

The cash float matters more than the margin. If 20 patients average 6 buy-and-bill doses a month, you buy about $156,000 of drug a month. If payers take 30 to 45 days to pay, $156,000 to $234,000 sits in receivables at any time. That is cash you need in the bank before the program pays you back.

The ketamine side works differently. At 4 infusions a day on 2 chairs, 20 days a month, 160 sessions earn about $48,000 a month before rent and overhead in this example. Prepaid series collected upfront show as cash, but they are only earned as each session is delivered.

How to choose

  1. Mostly commercially insured patients and strong cash reserves: Spravato with buy and bill can pay the most per session, if your authorization and billing work is tight.
  2. Thin cash or a high Medicare share: lean on the specialty pharmacy path for Spravato, or start with fewer chairs.
  3. A cash-pay market with room capacity: IV or IM ketamine avoids prior authorization and drug float, but prices compete with at-home services.
  4. Little admin capacity: a partner-run program trades margin for less work and less setup.
  5. Several clinics: open one site first, measure real chair time and denial rates for six months, then expand.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to add Spravato to a practice?

The setup cost is mostly REMS certification work, a monitoring room with chairs, a pulse oximeter and staff time. The larger cost is ongoing: two hours or more of monitoring per session and, under buy and bill, the drug bought before payment arrives. Model the drug float before your first order.

Is Spravato buy and bill worth it?

It can be, because the payer pays for the drug and the visit. But the practice buys each dose first, and a denied claim after the dose is a direct loss. Signal Hill reports that in 29 of 109 Medicare localities, the 2026 payment for an 84 mg visit falls below the drug's NADAC cost.

What does a ketamine infusion cost patients?

Published medians in late 2026 sit around $450 to $475 per IV infusion, with six-session series around $2,730 to $2,925, according to KetamineHQ and Signal Hill Strategies. Most clinics do not publish prices at all.

Does insurance cover IV ketamine?

Rarely. Healing Maps says insurance covers it for fewer than 5% of patients, so most practices run it as cash pay. Spravato, by contrast, is billed to Medicare, Medicaid and commercial plans under the codes above.

How many Spravato sessions do I need to break even?

Divide your monthly fixed costs (room, staff hours set aside, REMS admin) by your margin per paid session after drug cost and direct cost. In the illustrative example, a buy-and-bill session leaves about $490 and a specialty pharmacy session about $190, so the same fixed costs need two to three times as many specialty pharmacy sessions.

Should prepaid ketamine packages count as revenue?

Not when collected. Money for sessions not yet delivered is held as a deposit, often called deferred revenue, and becomes revenue as each session happens. Ask your CPA how refunds and unused sessions are handled.

Who decides whether my practice should offer these treatments?

Your clinicians make the clinical decision and your healthcare counsel covers REMS, controlled substance and supervision rules. The money side, including cash float, margin and break-even volume, is where your accountant and CFO help.
