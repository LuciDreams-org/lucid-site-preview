Sell to PE, join an MSO, or stay independent?

Short answer

Selling to a private equity platform pays you a large sum now, usually part cash and part rollover equity, in exchange for lower pay, a multi year employment agreement and less control. Joining a management services organization (MSO) without selling keeps clinical ownership with you, but you pay a management fee and give up some say over the business side. Staying independent keeps full control and full profit, and you carry all the workload, risk and investment yourself. The right choice depends on how many working years you have left, how much you want to run the business, and what each path is worth after tax, which only a model of your own numbers can show.

Path 1: Sell to a private equity platform

How it works. In most states, investors cannot own a medical practice outright because of corporate practice of medicine rules. So the investor buys the business side through an MSO, and a licensed owner keeps the clinical entity. Medical Economics explains that the MSO then signs a long term management agreement with the practice, and the management fee typically draws out all practice revenue other than clinical pay. Foley and Lardner describes the price as cash plus rollover equity in the new structure.

What you get:

  1. A large payment at closing. Medical practice deals in 2026 still price at a multiple of EBITDA, with scaled dermatology platforms at 12x to 15x according to FOCUS Investment Banking (May 2026). See the sale guide for multiples by specialty.
  2. A second payment later, if the platform sells again. Foley notes rollover equity can be up to 40% of value in larger deals.
  3. Less admin. The platform takes over billing, staffing, payer contracts and technology.

What you give up:

  1. Pay. Buyers create the EBITDA they pay for by reducing owner pay after closing, called the scrape. Medical Economics authors call confidence in later "income repair" a key test of any partner.
  2. Control over business decisions, hiring and growth plans.
  3. Freedom to leave. Expect an employment agreement, often three to five years, and a non compete.

Timing matters. PitchBook data reported by STAT (August 17, 2026) show practice management deals fell from 851 in 2021 to 105 in the first half of 2026. MedMoneyGuide reports that new state laws, including California SB 351 and AB 1415 and Oregon SB 951, are slowing deals and pushing buyers toward larger rollover stakes and more earnouts.

Path 2: Join an MSO without selling

This is the path owners often confuse with a sale. Some MSOs buy your practice, which is Path 1. Others sell services to practices that stay owned by their clinicians.

How it works. The MSO runs agreed business functions, such as billing, credentialing, HR, purchasing, payer contracting and technology. You pay a fee. Forvis Mazars (April 2025) lists three common fee structures: cost plus a margin, a fixed annual fee per service, or a percentage of net patient revenue. The fee should be consistent with fair market value. Medwave (October 2026) adds that state law often decides which fee types are allowed.

What you get:

  1. Scale without selling: group purchasing, payer leverage and back office staff.
  2. Clinical ownership and the practice's value stay yours.
  3. In some models, a stake in the MSO itself.

What you give up:

  1. A permanent cost. A percentage of revenue fee grows as you grow.
  2. Some business control, set by the management agreement.
  3. Exit freedom. Read the term, termination and buyout clauses closely. Owners polled by Sermo (June 2026) named vesting and exit terms as the top item to scrutinize in an MSO agreement.

Owners can also build their own MSO. A group with several locations or several practices can form a separate company that serves all of them, which keeps the upside inside the group. That route needs careful legal structure and a second set of books.

Path 3: Stay independent

How it works. You keep ownership, profit and every decision, and you buy the support you need: billing, accounting, CFO planning, HR. Medical Economics lists remaining independent as a real option but says it needs strong management and access to capital.

What you get:

  1. Full control and all profit.
  2. The option to sell later, possibly at a higher price if you grow EBITDA first.
  3. No scrape, no non compete with a buyer.

What you give up:

  1. Scale. You negotiate payer rates and buy supplies alone.
  2. Time. Owners carry admin on top of clinical work.
  3. Concentration of risk. Your practice is most of your net worth.

Independence is shrinking but not gone. The PAI and Avalere report found 82% of practicing clinicians in its data were employed by hospitals or corporate owners as of January 2026. In dermatology, a Health Affairs Scholar study (July 2026) found the independent share fell from 62% to 44% between 2012 and 2022, while PE backed chains grew from 1% to 16%.

Other paths worth a look

  1. Merge with peer practices into a larger independent group. You trade some control for scale and keep ownership among clinicians.
  2. Join a hospital or health system. Pay is stable and set within fair market value rules. See the practice sale guide for how hospital and partner buyers differ.
  3. Sell to a partner or associate over time. Usually a lower price, more continuity.

How to choose by specialty

  1. Dermatology. Buyer interest is high. A Practicelayer survey of 156 dermatology owners (July 2026) found 86% had been approached by PE or a consolidator, and the top reason owners gave for considering it was operational support (34%), ahead of an exit (30%). If support is the real need, price an MSO or outsourced back office before you sell. Separate medical, cosmetic and retail revenue, because buyers value them differently. See dermatology.
  2. Orthopedics. Surgery centers, imaging and therapy drive value, so the sale and MSO terms turn on who owns those ancillaries afterward. See orthopedics.
  3. Ophthalmology. Retina, surgery centers and optical lift value. Groups with several subspecialties often draw platform offers. See ophthalmology.
  4. Plastic surgery. CT Acquisitions (June 2026) says the market is thinner than dermatology or ophthalmology because many practices depend on one surgeon's personal brand. A med spa line with recurring revenue can widen the buyer pool. See plastic surgery.
  5. Medical groups with several locations or entities. Forming your own MSO or merging with peers may capture scale without a sale. You will need consolidated and location level reporting either way. See medical groups.

A simple rule of thumb from the sources: owners close to retirement tend to gain most from a sale, because they collect the price and the rollover without many years of reduced pay. Younger owners carry the scrape longest, so the MSO and independent paths deserve a careful look.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between selling to private equity and joining an MSO?

In a PE sale, an investor owned MSO buys the business side of your practice and you become an employee with rollover equity. In a services MSO, you keep ownership and pay a fee for business support. Read the paperwork to see which one you are being offered.

Will my pay go down if I sell to private equity?

Usually, yes. Buyers reduce owner pay after closing to create the earnings they pay for. Sources put the cut at roughly 20% to 30%, and the buyer should show how pay recovers over time.

How much does an MSO cost?

Fees are usually cost plus a margin, a fixed fee per service, or a percentage of net patient revenue, and they should match fair market value. Ask for the fee in dollars on your last 12 months of revenue so you can compare offers.

Can I stay independent and still compete?

Yes, if you run the practice on numbers. That means monthly books, payer and provider level reporting, and a forecast, plus outsourced help where scale matters. Independents trade scale for control.

How long does each path take?

A PE sale often takes six months to a year from first meeting to closing, and longer under new state notice rules. An MSO services agreement can start within a few months. Staying independent has no deadline, but cleanup work takes time to show up in your books.

Do I need clean books for all three paths?

Yes. A buyer prices your EBITDA, an MSO prices its fee on your revenue, and an independent owner needs numbers to make decisions. Accrual books with documented add backs make every comparison possible.
