Which numbers should a practice owner check every month?

Short answer

A practice owner should review about a dozen numbers every month, in four groups: cash and profit, revenue, collections, and cost. The core list is cash on hand, profit and loss against the prior months, collected revenue, days in accounts receivable, adjusted collection rate, denial rate, overhead as a percent of collections, and staffing cost as a percent of collections. If you have several locations or providers, view each number by location and provider, because a healthy total can hide one weak site. Review the list on a fixed schedule, from the same report, with the books closed first.

Why a dozen and not fifty

Practice management systems and billing software can produce hundreds of reports. Owners who try to read them all end up reading none. A short list, reviewed on time, beats a long dashboard nobody opens.

The Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) lists many practice measures in its 2023 article on foundational benchmarks. Among them are net revenue, collection ratios, denial rates, bad debt, payer mix, days in accounts receivable and aging, charge posting lag, copay and patient balance collections, and provider productivity. The same article says revenue cycle reports should be run at least monthly for administrative leaders and more often for billers. That is a useful split. Billing staff watch daily and weekly numbers. The owner watches the monthly view and asks why anything moved.

Your list should answer five owner questions:

  1. Do we have cash, and how many weeks does it last?
  2. Did we make money this month, and is that better or worse than the trend?
  3. Is revenue growing, and from which services, locations or providers?
  4. Are we getting paid for the work we do, and how fast?
  5. Is the cost of running the practice staying in proportion to what comes in?

The rest of the list (cash, profit, revenue, overhead and staffing cost) has no single published benchmark that fits all specialties, so compare each to your own trend. The sections below explain each number.

Group 1: cash and profit

  1. Cash balance and cash change. Write down the operating account balance at month end, then the change from last month. Add one planning line: months of fixed cost covered by cash. A profitable practice can still run short, because tax payments, equipment, owner distributions and slow payers all consume cash.
  2. Net profit after fair owner pay. Read the profit and loss statement for the month and the trailing twelve months. Put a fair salary for owner providers inside expenses. Otherwise profit looks higher than it is. Compare the month with the same month last year, because many practices are seasonal.
  3. Profit by location, provider or service line. The total profit number hides who is carrying whom. Where books are set up properly, you can see revenue, direct cost and contribution by location, by provider and by service line. This is the single most useful extra cut for owners with more than one site. Lucid reports revenue and cost by location or provider for practices with several locations. See the medical group page.

Group 2: revenue

  1. Collected revenue. Cash actually received for patient care in the month, after payer adjustments and refunds. Gross charges are a billing measure. Collected revenue is what pays the bills. Compare to prior months and the same month last year.
  2. Revenue mix. For practices that bill insurance, payer mix shows which payers produce the revenue. For cash practices, such as many med spas and dermatology aesthetics lines, it is the split among services, packages and memberships. A mix shift can change profit even if total revenue is flat. See dermatology and med spa.

Group 3: getting paid

This group matters most for practices that bill insurance. Your biller or billing software produces the raw data, and the books should tie those figures to the bank.

  1. Days in accounts receivable. It is total accounts receivable divided by average daily charges, and it shows roughly how many days pass between the date of service and payment. The AAFP, the family medicine professional association, says days in accounts receivable should stay below 50 days at a minimum, with 30 to 40 days preferable. AAFP also advises tracking claims older than 120 days separately, because a decent average can hide old balances. In MGMA's July 2026 poll of 203 medical group leaders, 43 percent said days in accounts receivable were about the same as a year earlier and 32 percent said they were higher.
  2. Accounts receivable aging. Split the balance into buckets: 0 to 30, 31 to 60, 61 to 90, 91 to 120 and over 120 days. A growing share in the old buckets is an early warning that collections will fall later. Our month end receivables aging checklist walks through the review. Lucid's team prepares accounts receivable aging reports as part of its reporting.
  3. Adjusted collection rate. AAFP says the adjusted collection rate should be 95 percent at minimum, with average performance between 95 and 99 percent and top performers at 99 percent or more, calculated over twelve months. The dental equivalent on the American Dental Association (ADA) practice management page asks whether the practice collects at least 98 percent of billable or adjusted production.
  4. Denial rate. AAFP calls a 5 to 10 percent denial rate the industry average and says staying below 5 percent is better. MGMA benchmarking has put first submission denials at 7 to 8 percent over the past four years, and its report suggests practices can get below 5 percent with targeted process fixes. Denial work is done by your billing team. The owner's job is to see the number move.
  5. Patient balance collections. MGMA reports that in 2024 practices collected 72 percent of copayments at the time of service but only about 27 percent of other patient due balances. If your patient balances grow, it is a process problem at the front desk and in statements, and it shows up as bad debt later. See our guide to RCM software if your billing tools are the bottleneck.

Lucid does not file claims or run billing. It reports these measures next to the books so the owner sees collections, aging and cash in one place, alongside the biller.

Group 4: cost

  1. Overhead as a percent of collections. Operating expenses divided by collected revenue. Track the total and each major line: staff, rent, supplies, insurance, technology and marketing. Our guide on what overhead percentage is normal for a medical practice covers definitions and the few benchmarks with named sources.
  2. Staffing cost as a percent of collections. Pay, benefits and payroll taxes for each group of staff, as a share of collections. Staffing is the largest controllable line for most practices. Split provider pay from support staff, because they behave differently.
  3. Cost per provider, per visit or per procedure. Where the practice has the volume data, divide cost by units. For procedure heavy specialties, cost and margin by surgeon or provider shows which work makes money.

Items 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11 and 12 form the core list. The others are the second look when a core number moves.

Operational numbers that belong next to the financial list

Financial numbers explain results. Operating numbers explain causes. Add a few from your practice management system, based on specialty:

  1. Visits or encounters per provider.
  2. Cancellation and no show rate. The ADA page asks dental owners whether the rate is 5 percent or less.
  3. New patient count. The ADA page says dental new patient numbers should increase 10 to 15 percent annually.
  4. For dental, case acceptance. The ADA page asks whether at least 75 to 80 percent of case presentations are accepted.

These come from your scheduling and practice management software, not from the books. They are best read on the same page as the financial list.

What a good monthly report looks like

A practical owner report fits on two pages and has the same layout every month.

  1. Page one: cash balance and change, profit and loss for the month, the prior month and the same month last year, and profit by location.
  2. Page two: collected revenue, days in accounts receivable, aging buckets, adjusted collection rate, denial rate, overhead percent and staffing percent, each with the prior three months.
  3. A short notes block: what moved, why, and what you will do about it.

Two conditions make the report trustworthy. First, the books must be closed before the review, meaning every bank and card account is reconciled. Second, accounts must be coded consistently so lines mean the same thing each month, which is a chart of accounts question. See how to set up a chart of accounts for a medical practice.

How often to review

  1. Daily or weekly: your biller and front desk review claims, denials, cash posting and schedule fill.
  2. Monthly: the owner reviews the list above, by location, with the books closed. Many practices hold a short monthly meeting with the practice manager and, if there is one, the outside CPA.
  3. Quarterly: review the trend lines, estimated tax payments, hiring plans and any equipment purchase.
  4. Yearly: review pay models, payer contracts, pricing and the budget for next year.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Which financial numbers should a medical practice owner review every month?

Review cash, profit after fair owner pay, collected revenue, days in accounts receivable, adjusted collection rate, denial rate, overhead as a percent of collections and staffing cost as a percent of collections. View each by location if you have more than one. Review it after the books are closed.

What is a good days in accounts receivable number for a medical practice?

AAFP says days in accounts receivable should stay below 50 days at a minimum, with 30 to 40 days preferable. Track claims older than 120 days separately. Specialty and payer mix change what is realistic, so also compare with your own trend.

What is a good adjusted collection rate?

AAFP says it should be at least 95 percent, with average performance between 95 and 99 percent and top performers at 99 percent or higher, measured over twelve months. The American Dental Association asks dental owners whether they collect at least 98 percent of billable or adjusted production. Compare your own figure with the same measure each month, because a falling trend matters more than one reading.

Do I need a dashboard or is a monthly report enough?

A two page monthly report is enough for most practices, as long as it has the same layout each month and is built from reconciled books. A dashboard helps when you have several locations, but it only helps if the underlying accounts are coded consistently. Start with the report and add a dashboard later.

How do I see which location or provider makes money?

Code revenue and direct cost by location and provider in the books, then report profit for each with shared costs allocated by a written method. Without that coding, no report can separate them. Review the result with the books closed so the numbers are final.

Who should prepare the monthly numbers?

Your billing system produces collections data, and your bookkeeper or accounting team produces the financial statements. Ask for both on the same schedule and reconcile them. A CPA led team can then explain what moved and what to do about it.
