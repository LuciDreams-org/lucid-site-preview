Short answer

For most practices with staff, the best card depends on where the money goes. Flat-rate cash back cards such as Capital One Spark Cash Plus (2%), American Express Blue Business Cash (2% on the first $50,000 a year) and Chase Ink Business Unlimited (1.5%) pay the most on medical supplies, which rarely fall into bonus categories. Chase Ink Business Preferred and the American Express Business Gold Card pay more on software, phone, internet and advertising. Ramp, Brex and the BILL Divvy Card are pay-in-full corporate cards built around employee cards, spending limits and receipt capture, which matters more than rewards once several people spend for the practice.

What a practice card should do

A practice card has three jobs, and rewards are only one of them.

  1. Pay for the right spend. Supplies, software, phones, marketing, continuing education and travel. Large distributor orders and rent usually go by ACH, so ask your supplier whether it accepts cards and whether it adds a surcharge before you plan rewards around it.
  2. Control who spends. Front desk, office managers and clinicians need cards with limits, so one person is not lending the practice a personal card.
  3. Land cleanly in the books. Every charge needs a receipt, a category and, for groups, a location. A card that exports clean data saves hours at month end.

Supplies are the biggest card category for many practices, and they rarely earn bonus rewards. Medical and dental distributors are not "office supply stores", so a 5% office supply category will not cover them. That is why a flat-rate card often beats a category card for clinical supply spend.

The cards

Capital One Spark Cash Plus

Capital One lists unlimited 2% cash back on every purchase, with no category limits. The annual fee is $150, and Capital One refunds it every year you spend at least $150,000. The card is designed to be paid in full, with an option to carry part of the balance with interest. Capital One lists free employee and virtual cards.

Best for: practices that put large, mixed supply spend on one card. Limits: it is a pay-in-full card, so it is not a way to finance a cash gap.

American Express Blue Business Cash

American Express lists 2% cash back on the first $50,000 of eligible purchases per calendar year, then 1%, with a $0 annual fee. Cash back is credited to the statement automatically. Employee cards earn cash back the same way. For the purchase APR, see the rates and fees on the Amex page.

Best for: smaller practices with under $50,000 a year of card spend. Limits: the 2% rate drops to 1% after $50,000, so larger groups outgrow it.

Chase Ink Business Unlimited

Chase lists unlimited 1.5% cash back on every purchase, a $0 annual fee and a 0% intro APR for 12 months on purchases, then 16.99% to 24.99% variable. Chase lists employee cards at no additional cost, with individual spending limits.

Best for: practices that want a simple no-fee card and may carry a balance briefly after a large purchase. Limits: a lower flat rate than the 2% cards above.

Chase Ink Business Cash

Chase lists 5% cash back on the first $25,000 a year in combined purchases at office supply stores and on internet, cable and phone services, 2% on the first $25,000 at gas stations and restaurants, and 1% on everything else. The annual fee is $0, with a 0% intro APR for 12 months on purchases, then 16.99% to 24.99% variable.

Best for: front office spend, phone and internet bills. Limits: the 5% cap is low, and clinical supply orders will usually earn 1%.

Chase Ink Business Preferred

Chase lists 3 points per $1 on the first $150,000 a year in combined purchases on shipping, advertising with social media sites and search engines, internet, cable and phone, and travel, and 1 point per $1 on everything else. The annual fee is $95 and the APR is 17.99% to 28.74% variable. Employee cards come at no additional cost.

Best for: practices with steady online ad spend, such as a med spa or a dental office that markets for new patients. Limits: points pay best when redeemed for travel, and supplies earn only 1 point.

American Express Business Gold Card

American Express lists 4X Membership Rewards points on your top 2 of 6 categories each billing cycle, on the first $150,000 a year in combined purchases, then 1X. The categories include U.S. advertising in select media, U.S. electronic goods retailers and software and cloud system providers, restaurants, gas stations, transit and wireless phone service. Flights and prepaid hotels booked on AmexTravel.com earn 3X. The annual fee is $375 and Amex lists $95 for each Employee Business Gold Card. It has no preset spending limit, and its Pay Over Time feature lets you carry eligible charges with interest.

Best for: practices with large software, EHR and ad bills that will use the points. Limits: the highest fee here, plus fees for employee cards.

Ramp

Ramp is a corporate card and spend platform. Its card is a corporate liability charge card, and Ramp says there are no personal credit checks or personal guarantee and no annual fee. Ramp Free costs $0 per user per month, and Ramp Plus costs $15 per user per month plus a platform fee. Plans include unlimited physical and virtual cards, category and vendor controls, spend limits with preset accounting codes and automatic receipt matching.

Best for: groups with many staff spenders or several locations that want controls first. Limits: it is paid in full, it is not a credit line, and advanced features sit in the paid plan.

Brex

Brex lists a $0 per user per month Essentials plan, global card acceptance, expense management and card rewards that you can redeem for cash back or credits. Brex sets card limits from business financial factors.

Best for: larger or fast-growing groups that want one spend platform across entities. Limits: check the Brex site for eligibility; it is built around company financials, not owner credit.

BILL Divvy Card

BILL lists the BILL Divvy Card with no annual fee and as many virtual cards as the company needs at no extra cost. It is a charge card with the balance due in full. Credit lines run from $1,000 to $5 million. Rewards rise with how often you pay: BILL says you can earn up to 7x points on restaurants and 5x on hotels on a weekly payment schedule.

Best for: practices that already pay bills through BILL and want cards and budgets in one place. Limits: the best reward rates need weekly payments, which ties up cash sooner.

How to choose

  1. Most spend is clinical supplies. A flat 2% card: Spark Cash Plus if you spend over $150,000 a year, Blue Business Cash if you spend under $50,000.
  2. Most spend is software, phones and ads. Ink Business Preferred or Amex Business Gold, if you will redeem points well.
  3. Many staff spenders or several locations. Ramp, Brex or the BILL Divvy Card for limits, receipts and coding by location. See our medical group page.
  4. You want no fee and one simple card. Ink Business Unlimited.
  5. You need to finance a gap. A card is the wrong tool. Compare a line of credit for a medical practice.
  6. Dental offices with lab, supply and marketing spend split across vendors. Pair a flat-rate card for supplies with a category card for ads. See our dental page.

A card is not the same as accepting cards from patients. For that, see our guide to credit card processing for medical practices. For supply pricing itself, a group purchasing organization can save more than any reward rate.

How card spend shows in the books

This is where most practices lose time, so set it up before staff get cards.

  1. The card is a liability account. Each charge is recorded as an expense in the right category, such as medical supplies, software or marketing, and the balance owed sits on the card account. See a sample chart of accounts for a medical practice.
  2. Paying the card is not an expense. The payment moves money from checking to the card account. Recording it as an expense counts the same spend twice.
  3. Reconcile every month. Match the card statement balance to the books, the same way you reconcile the bank.
  4. Code by location. In a group, each charge should carry the location or practice it belongs to, so profit by location is accurate.
  5. Keep personal spend off the card. If it happens, record it as an owner draw or distribution, not a practice expense.
  6. Annual fees and interest. The annual fee is a business expense. Interest on a carried balance is interest expense, not supplies.
  7. Rewards. Ask your CPA how to treat cash back. It is often recorded as a reduction of the cost of what you bought.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is the best business credit card for medical supplies?

A flat-rate cash back card usually wins, because medical and dental distributors rarely fall into bonus categories. Capital One Spark Cash Plus lists unlimited 2%, and Amex Blue Business Cash lists 2% on the first $50,000 a year. Check that your distributor takes cards without a surcharge.

Should my practice use Ramp or an Amex card?

Ramp is a pay-in-full corporate card that Ramp says needs no personal guarantee, with controls and receipt matching built in. Amex cards earn more on certain categories but carry owner credit and, for Business Gold, a $375 annual fee. Many groups use a corporate card for staff and a rewards card for the owner's own purchases.

Do business credit cards need a personal guarantee?

Most small business cards from banks hold the owner personally responsible, so read the cardmember agreement. Ramp says its corporate card has no personal guarantee. Corporate cards usually look at the practice's cash and revenue instead.

Can I give employee cards with spending limits?

Yes. Chase lists employee cards at no additional cost with individual limits, Capital One lists free employee and virtual cards, and Ramp and BILL offer unlimited virtual cards. Amex lists $95 for each Employee Business Gold Card.

Is it OK to carry a balance on a practice card?

It is an expensive way to borrow. Chase lists variable APRs from 16.99% to 28.74% on its Ink cards after any intro period. For timing gaps, a bank line of credit usually costs less.

How do I record card payments in the books?

The payment moves money from checking to the card account and is not an expense. The expenses are the individual charges, each coded to its category and location. Reconcile the card every month.

Do card rewards count as income?

Ask your CPA. Cash back on business purchases is often treated as a reduction in the cost of those purchases rather than income. Keep it consistent from year to year.
