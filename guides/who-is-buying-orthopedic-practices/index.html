Who is buying orthopedic practices?

Short answer

Orthopedic practices are being bought by private equity backed management services organizations (MSOs) such as HOPCo, United Musculoskeletal Partners, Orthopedic Care Partners, Evolve and OSM, and by health systems, which made 8 of the 14 announced orthopedic deals through September 2026 in Bayshore Growth Partners' tracker. Surgery center operators such as SCA Health (Optum) and USPI (Tenet), an academic and investor pairing (HSS with General Atlantic), and surgeon-owned platforms such as PELTO Health Partners are also active. Buyers pay most for surgery center ownership, ancillaries such as imaging, therapy and bracing, a strong commercial payer mix and surgeons who will stay. Compare offers on cash at closing per partner, rollover terms and pay after closing, not on the headline multiple.

How active is the market?

Bayshore Growth Partners tracks 222 announced US orthopedic and musculoskeletal deals from 2018 through September 30, 2026. Its numbers show a clear shift:

  1. PE-backed deals fell from 28 in 2022 to 7 in 2024, then rose to 9 in 2025.
  2. Sponsors made four platform investments in 2025, the most since 2021: Evolve (New York), OrthoNOW (Florida), Precision Orthopedics (Michigan) and a new sponsor for OSM (Florida).
  3. Health systems went from 1 deal in 2022 to 19 of 39 deals in 2025.
  4. Through September 2026, 8 of 14 announced deals were by health systems and 3 were PE-backed.

At the AAOS 2026 Annual Meeting, Dana Jacoby of Vector Medical Group said more orthopedic groups are "at the table" looking for a partner than a year earlier, largely because of reimbursement cuts (AAOS Now, October 2026).

Buyer type 1: private equity backed MSOs

In most states an investor cannot own a medical practice outright. So the sponsor buys into an MSO that runs the business side, the clinical entity stays with its licensed owners, and the two sign a long term management agreement.

Active platforms, with their sponsor and a recent dated event:

  1. HOPCo (The CORE Institute). Linden Capital Partners and Audax Private Equity since 2019. Partnered with Orthopaedic Specialty Group and OrthoConnecticut on May 13, 2026. Footprint includes Arizona, Nevada, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Florida and Connecticut.
  2. United Musculoskeletal Partners (Resurgens, Panorama). Welsh Carson since December 2021, with a 2022 merger with Novum. About 190 partners in Colorado, Georgia and Texas.
  3. Orthopedic Care Partners. Varsity Healthcare Partners, with a growth recapitalization from Brookfield in December 2024. 42 locations and six ASCs in five states at that time.
  4. Spire Orthopedic Partners. Kohlberg and Company since 2019. Connecticut, Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island; partnered with Ortho Rhode Island in June 2024.
  5. Growth Orthopedics. Trivest Partners since 2021. Added Orthopaedic Associates of Maine and its affiliated ASC in early 2025.
  6. Evolve Orthopedic Partners. Zenyth Partners formed it with OrthoNY in August 2025 and added Syracuse Orthopedic Specialists in January 2026.
  7. Orthopaedic Solutions Management (OSM). Vesey Street invested in July 2025. Added Florida Orthopaedic Associates (October 2025) and Orlando Orthopaedic Center with its ASC (August 2026).
  8. Sequel Ortho. InTandem Capital, built on OrthoNebraska. Added Fox Valley Orthopedics in November 2025.
  9. Newer platforms: Precision Orthopedics (Shore Capital, September 2025) and OrthoNOW (Redwood Growth, September 2025).

Bayshore notes that most PE add-ons since 2024 went to platforms with sponsor investment since 2023, and that a smaller group usually needs a platform already in its state. Other established platforms include US Orthopaedic Partners (FFL Partners and The Thurston Group), US Orthopedic Alliance (Castleford Capital), American Orthopedic Partners (Stone Point), Health Plus Management (Investcorp) and Unity MSK (Trinity Hunt).

How the deal usually works: partners accept lower pay (the scrape), that difference becomes MSO earnings, and the sponsor buys those earnings at a multiple. Stout (January 2026) says rollover equity typically runs 20% to 40% of the price, and 30% to 35% in recent orthopedic deals it worked on. A December 2025 article in the Bulletin of the Hospital for Joint Diseases models a 30% pay cut.

Buyer type 2: health systems

Health systems are now the most frequent orthopedic buyers in Bayshore's count. They usually employ the group, form a joint venture, or sign a professional services agreement in which the group keeps employing its own clinicians.

Stout (January 2026) explains the key difference. In its example, about one third of the value moves at closing and two thirds arrives as pay over the first employment term. Pay must stay within fair market value, so a group whose partners already earn at the top of the range may need more cash up front instead. Stout also sees health systems use joint venture or affiliation models with large groups to avoid a full purchase.

Buyer type 3: surgery center operators and payers

  1. SCA Health, part of Optum and UnitedHealth Group, completed its OrthoAlliance deal on November 1, 2024, for an estimated $1.4 billion (Orthopedics This Week, via CT Acquisitions). OrthoAlliance covers Ohio, Indiana and Kentucky.
  2. USPI, Tenet Healthcare's surgery center arm, bought a surgery center operator with centers in 17 states from KKR in 2024 and partnered with Florida Orthopedic Institute (Stout, January 2026). Becker's ASC Review reported a USPI deal with San Diego based Synergy Orthopedics on October 29, 2024.
  3. Ascension agreed in June 2025 to acquire AMSURG, a surgery center operator with orthopedics among its main specialties (Stout).

Bayshore notes that ASC and strategic buyers often pay cash for an ASC or ancillary stake while the practice stays independent.

Buyer type 4: academic hospital plus investor

On October 15, 2025, Hospital for Special Surgery and General Atlantic agreed to acquire Legent Health, a surgery center platform, with equity offered to surgeons (HSS, via CT Acquisitions). Terms were not disclosed. AAOS Now (October 2026) also lists three-way partnerships among a health system, a PE investor and a practice as an option.

Buyer type 5: surgeon-owned platforms and mergers

  1. PELTO Health Partners launched as a platform of EmergeOrtho, OrthoIndy and Proliance Surgeons with more than 400 surgeons and no outside capital (Proliance, via CT Acquisitions).
  2. Regional mergers: Pacific Crest Orthopedics formed in San Francisco in 2024, and Atlantis Orthopedics joined operations with South Florida Orthopaedics and Sports Medicine (Stout).
  3. Rothman Orthopedics stays independent and works with hospital partners across four states (Stout).

Gary Herschman of Baker Donaldson noted at AAOS 2026 that owners in these models must bring growth capital themselves or through debt.

Buyer type 6: ESOPs and family offices

Stout describes ESOP deals in orthopedics, financed with seller notes and bank debt. There is no rollover equity, but sellers can keep some upside through warrants. Herschman (AAOS Now, October 2026) says family office backed platforms tend to invest in the existing team and may hold 10 years or longer, and that ESOPs suit larger groups with a seasoned management team.

What orthopedic buyers look for

  1. Surgery center ownership. Stout reports some orthopedic ASCs run EBITDA margins above 50%, and commercial total joint payments of $10,000 to $20,000 plus the implant.
  2. Ancillaries. Imaging, physical therapy, bracing and equipment, and urgent care add earnings and give partners a path to income repair after the scrape.
  3. Site of care. CMS removed 285 mostly musculoskeletal procedures from the Medicare inpatient only list for 2026 and added 289 to the ASC list. Buyers check how many Medicare joint cases still run through a hospital outpatient department.
  4. Payer mix. Medicare share of joint volume versus commercial contracts.
  5. Surgeon age, retention and recruiting. Buyers ask who keeps operating through the hold period, and AAOS speakers stress a partner's plan to recruit new surgeons.
  6. Technology and data. Stout lists a high functioning EMR, online booking, referral management and patient engagement among the factors behind the multiple.
  7. Clean, separable numbers. Professional fees, ASC distributions and each ancillary need their own lines, because buyers may price them differently.

How to compare offers

Put every offer in the same table, partner by partner, and ask for each figure in writing.

  1. Cash at closing per partner, after escrow, holdbacks and fees.
  2. Rollover: the share of price, which entity issues the units, their class, and what happens if you leave early.
  3. Pay after closing: the scrape percentage, the year one formula, and whether it resets.
  4. The ASC: is it bought at the same multiple, a different one, or left with the partners?
  5. Ancillaries: which are in the deal and how each is valued.
  6. Employment term and non compete: length, radius and which state law governs.
  7. Earnouts: the metric, the period and who controls the books that measure it.
  8. Platform plans: how long the sponsor has held it and when it expects to sell, since rollover pays at that sale.
  9. References: owners who joined two or more years ago.

Then compare each offer with your stay independent baseline over the same years. A partner retiring in three years and one with fifteen years left can reach opposite answers on the same offer.

How to choose by situation

  1. Most partners retire within about seven years: weight cash at closing over rollover.
  2. Most partners have ten or more years left: push for a lighter scrape, or look at a health system or surgeon-owned model.
  3. You own an ASC: ask every bidder to price ASC distributions separately from clinic earnings.
  4. Medicare joints still run through a hospital department: expect buyers to ask about an ASC plan.
  5. Several entities or owners: model the deal owner by owner. See medical groups.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Who is buying orthopedic practices in 2026?

PE-backed MSOs such as HOPCo, United Musculoskeletal Partners, Orthopedic Care Partners, Evolve and OSM, plus health systems, which made 8 of 14 announced deals through September 2026 per Bayshore. SCA Health, USPI, HSS with General Atlantic and PELTO Health Partners are also active.

What multiple do orthopedic practices sell for?

Stout (January 2026) reports mid-single digits for smaller tuck-ins, high single to low double digits for midsized practices and the mid-teens for larger platforms. The multiple applies to earnings after the pay cut, so compare cash per partner too.

How does an ASC change the offer?

Surgery centers keep the facility fee and can carry high margins, so buyers often price them separately. Ask each bidder how ASC distributions are valued and who owns the center after closing.

What is the scrape in an orthopedic deal?

It is the cut in partner pay that creates the earnings a PE buyer pays for. A December 2025 journal model uses 30%. Ask how and when pay recovers.

How is a health system offer different?

Stout's example moves about one third of value at closing and two thirds through pay over the employment term. Pay must stay within fair market value.

Can my group stay independent and still grow?

Yes. Surgeon-owned platforms such as PELTO and regional mergers are options, as are ASC joint ventures. Owners usually fund growth themselves or with debt.
