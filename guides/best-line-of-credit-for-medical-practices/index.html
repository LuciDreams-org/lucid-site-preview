Short answer

For most practices with staff, the best line of credit comes from a bank you already use or plan to borrow from: Bank of America Practice Solutions, Chase, PNC, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo Practice Finance, Panacea Financial or Huntington. Chase publishes the clearest bank line terms ($10,000 to $500,000, a 5 year draw period and a capped annual fee). Bluevine and American Express are faster online lines for smaller gaps, but they cost more per dollar drawn and repay in weeks or months. An SBA Express or CAPLines line is the option when a bank will not lend on its own terms.

What a line of credit should do for a practice

A line of credit covers timing gaps. Insurance pays 30 to 90 days after the visit, payroll runs every two weeks, and a new provider takes months to ramp. The line fills the hole, and the next payer deposits pay it back.

A loan buys something that lasts, such as a practice or equipment. A line should go up and down. If your balance never comes down, the line is funding a loss or an asset, and it should be turned into a term loan or the underlying problem fixed. Our guide to the best medical practice loans covers the loan side.

Five terms decide whether a line is good:

  1. Limit. Big enough to cover your worst month of payer lag, not your best guess.
  2. Collateral. Bank lines usually take a lien on business assets, often with a personal guarantee. Online lines may skip collateral and charge more instead.
  3. Covenants. Rules you must keep, such as sending annual financial statements, keeping a minimum debt service coverage ratio, or keeping your operating account at the bank.
  4. Renewal. Many bank lines are reviewed every year. Some require the balance to be paid to zero for a period each year. Ask for the renewal terms in writing.
  5. Draw cost. A variable rate on the balance (bank lines) or a fee per draw (many online lines). Compare the cost of the same draw held for the same time.

The lenders

Bank of America Practice Solutions

Bank of America Practice Solutions lends to dental, medical and optometry practices to start, expand or acquire, and offers a business line of credit beside its term loans. Line sizes and rates are not published. Its site says Practice Solutions line of credit and term loan rate discounts go to applicants enrolled in Preferred Rewards for Business: 0.25% at Gold, 0.30% at Platinum and 0.35% at Platinum Honors. Specialty lending products with custom pricing are excluded.

Best for: owners who already keep deposits at Bank of America or plan to borrow there. Limits: no published line size, rate or fee. Ask whether the line expects your operating account.

Chase

Chase publishes full terms for its Business Line of Credit. Lines run from $10,000 to $500,000 for existing Chase for Business customers. You can draw for 5 years with potential to renew, then you have another 5 years to repay any balance. The rate is variable and indexed to Prime, and the minimum monthly payment is 1% of principal plus interest. There is no application or origination fee. The annual fee is 0.25% of the approved line, at least $200 and at most $750, and from year two it is waived if you use at least 40% of the line on average over 12 months.

To qualify, Chase lists at least $100,000 in annual revenue, a FICO score of at least 660, and no change in majority ownership or management in the past two years.

Best for: established practices that bank with Chase and want clear, published line terms. Limits: only for existing Chase business clients. Chase's business pages do not show a separate practice lending unit.

PNC

PNC's medical practice loans page groups lending by stage, and its growing stage includes support for gaps in the collections process. It does not publish line sizes, rates or terms, and says limits depend on the loan's use and creditworthiness.

Best for: practices that want a working capital line from a bank with a healthcare team. Limits: no published terms.

U.S. Bank

U.S. Bank's medical practice loans page says funding can support working capital for payroll and reimbursement delays, and that working capital is available for early operating needs. Business lines of credit are listed in its business lending products. Line sizes and rates are not published.

Best for: practices that want a line and a long-term practice loan from the same healthcare bankers. Limits: line terms not published.

Wells Fargo Practice Finance

Wells Fargo Practice Finance lends to dentists, optometrists, veterinarians and medical owners. Its medical page says owners who expand or relocate can "explore our working capital and line of credit options". No line terms are published.

Best for: dental and optometry owners who already bank with Wells Fargo. Limits: ask for line size, rate and renewal terms.

Panacea Financial

Panacea Financial is a division of Primis Bank with a Practice Solutions team for medical, dental and veterinary practices. Its practice page lists a line of credit to "help bridge the gap of operating expenses, inventory purchases, or capital needs", alongside working capital loans, startup loans and real estate loans.

Best for: owners who want a lender that only works with practices. Limits: no published line terms.

Huntington

Huntington runs a Healthcare Practice Finance group for medical, dental and optometry practices. Huntington does not list line terms in one place, so ask the lender.

Best for: practices in Huntington's markets that want a local practice banker.

Bluevine

Bluevine is a financial technology company, not a bank, and its line of credit is issued by Celtic Bank. Lines go up to $250,000, funds can arrive within hours of a draw, and repayment is a weekly or monthly plan, debited automatically. To qualify, Bluevine lists at least $10,000 in monthly revenue, a 625 or higher FICO score, 12 or more months in business and an LLC or corporation.

Best for: smaller practices that need a fast line for short gaps. Limits: weekly or monthly repayment is short for a 90 day payer lag. Bluevine says rates and terms vary and additional fees apply.

American Express Business Line of Credit

American Express offers lines from $2,000 to $1,000,000, but lines at approval are capped at $250,000, and increases come after the account is open. Each draw becomes its own installment loan of 6, 12, 18 or 24 months. Instead of interest, you pay a monthly loan fee of 0.55% to 1.55% of the original draw, with no origination, annual or prepayment fees. Requirements: a business open at least a year, a FICO score of at least 660 and recent average monthly revenue of at least $3,000.

Best for: a known, one-off cost on a short draw. Limits: the fee is charged on the original draw every month, even as you pay it down. On a 12 month draw, that is 6.6% to 18.6% of the amount drawn, and the effective annual rate is higher because the balance falls.

SBA Express and CAPLines

The SBA does not lend directly. Banks lend and the SBA guarantees part of the line. Under SBA rules, revolving lines are allowed only under SBA Express, Export Express or CAPLines. SBA Express goes up to $500,000 with a 50% guarantee and allows revolving lines of up to 10 years. The Working CAPLine is an asset-based revolving line for businesses that cannot meet standard credit terms, drawn against existing assets such as receivables. See our guide to the best SBA lenders for healthcare practices.

Best for: younger practices or practices a bank will not lend to on its own. Limits: more paperwork, a guarantee fee, and the lender sets the final terms within SBA rules.

How to choose

  1. You bank with a healthcare lender already. Ask that bank first. A line from your current bank is usually cheaper and simpler. Our guide to the best banks for medical practices covers who has a healthcare team.
  2. You want published terms before you call. Chase gives size, draw period, fee and eligibility on its page.
  3. You need $50,000 for a few weeks. Bluevine or American Express can fund fast. Compare the full cost of the draw against a bank line.
  4. You run several locations or a medical group. Ask for one line across entities, with covenants tested on combined statements. See the medical group page.
  5. A bank says no. Ask an SBA lender about SBA Express or a Working CAPLine.
  6. The balance never comes down. Stop drawing. Find the cause first: payer denials, a slow billing team or a loss-making location. Our RCM software guide covers the billing side.

Before you apply, gather what lenders ask for: two to three years of tax returns, current financial statements, an AR aging report and a debt schedule. Our guide on what lenders look for in a practice loan has the full list.

If the bank says no or the gap comes from slow insurance payments, compare AR factoring, claims advances and AR lines in Should my practice use medical receivables financing?

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How big should my practice's line of credit be?

Size it to your worst cash month, not your average one. Add your payroll and fixed costs for the length of your slowest payer lag, then subtract the cash you keep in reserve. A cash forecast by week shows the real low point.

Can I use a line of credit to make payroll?

Yes, that is a common use when insurance payments run late. The line should be repaid from the deposits that follow. If you draw for payroll every pay period and never pay it down, the problem is profit or collections, not timing.

Do I need collateral for a practice line of credit?

Bank lines usually take a lien on business assets and often a personal guarantee. Some online lines skip collateral and price the risk into the fee. Ask each lender what it files and whether it blocks other borrowing.

What covenants come with a bank line?

Common ones are annual financial statements and tax returns, a minimum debt service coverage ratio, a limit on other debt, and keeping your operating account at the bank. Some lines also require the balance to be paid to zero for a period each year. Get every covenant in writing before you sign.

What happens when a line comes up for renewal?

The bank reviews your latest financial statements and tax returns and decides whether to renew, resize or reprice the line. Clean, current books make renewal routine. Late or messy statements are a common reason for a smaller line.

Is a line of credit better than a term loan?

For timing gaps, yes, because you pay only on what you draw. For a buildout, equipment or a practice purchase, a term loan is the better fit, because the payment matches the life of what you buy.
