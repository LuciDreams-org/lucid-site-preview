What chart of accounts should a medical practice use?

Short answer

A medical practice should use a chart of accounts with the five standard sections (assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses), numbered in blocks, with extra detail where owners make decisions: revenue by service line, payer or location, expenses by the categories that make up overhead, and a few healthcare specific accounts such as patient receivables, merchant processor clearing and refunds. Keep it short enough that staff code transactions the same way every time, usually somewhere around 60 to 120 accounts for a single location practice (a rule of thumb, not a published standard). Add location, provider or class tags for detail instead of multiplying accounts. The Medical Group Management Association (MGMA) publishes a chart of accounts for medical groups, and it follows the same five sections.

What a chart of accounts is, and why it matters

A chart of accounts is the list of every account your books use to sort money coming in and going out. Every transaction lands in one account. The profit and loss statement and the balance sheet are built from those accounts, so the chart decides which questions your reports can answer.

MGMA describes a chart of accounts as the basis for all accounting information recorded in the financial records of any business, including a medical practice. In its sixth edition (2014), MGMA organizes the accounts into five sections: assets, liabilities, equity, revenues and expenses. Those five sections are standard in accounting, not specific to medicine.

A weak chart causes three common problems:

  1. One big revenue account and one big supplies account. The statements balance, but they cannot tell you which service, payer or location is profitable.
  2. Too many accounts. Fifty similar expense accounts invite inconsistent coding, and no one can read the report.
  3. Different charts in different locations or entities. Consolidation becomes a monthly manual project.

A good chart is detailed where decisions are made and silent everywhere else.

Design rules before you list accounts

  1. Build the chart from the reports you want. Start with the monthly numbers an owner reviews, such as profit by location, overhead by category and staffing cost as a percent of collections. Then create the accounts those reports need. See which numbers a practice owner should review every month.
  2. Use numbering in blocks. A common convention is 1000s for assets, 2000s for liabilities, 3000s for equity, 4000s for revenue, 5000s for direct costs and 6000s for operating expenses. Leave gaps between numbers so you can add accounts later without renumbering.
  3. Use subaccounts for roll up. Group related accounts, such as several staff pay lines, under one parent so the summary report stays short and the detail is one click away.
  4. Use tags for the second dimension. Most accounting software lets you tag each transaction with a location, provider or class. Use tags for "where" and "who," and accounts for "what." That avoids creating a separate rent account for every site.
  5. One chart for every location and entity. Use the same account numbers and names across all locations and related entities so reports add up. Our post on intercompany account mapping for medical groups covers entities that bill each other.
  6. Match the tax return. Your CPA maps accounts to lines on the tax return. If an expense category has a different tax treatment, such as meals or owner related costs, give it its own account. The IRS says a business expense must be both ordinary and necessary to be deductible, and that businesses must keep records available for inspection. A clean chart makes both easier.

Revenue is where a medical practice chart should be most specific. Revenue should answer two questions the moment the profit and loss statement prints: which service lines produce it, and which payers or patient types produce it.

Two accounting choices need a decision with your CPA.

  1. Gross or net revenue. Insurance practices can record gross charges and show contractual adjustments as a contra account, or record only the allowed amount. Gross reporting needs more work but shows how much of each charge the practice actually keeps. Net reporting is simpler. Pick one and keep it.
  2. Cash or accrual. A cash basis chart has no patient receivables account on the books. The practice management system carries the receivables detail. Accrual books record revenue when earned. Many practices keep cash basis books for tax and use management reports for receivables. See medical practice bookkeeping cost for how this choice affects the work.

If you also want payer mix, do not create one revenue account per insurance company. Track payer mix from the practice management system and the remittance data, or use a limited set of payer groups (commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, self pay) as subaccounts or tags.

A few notes:

  1. Keep provider pay separate from support staff pay. Without that split you cannot calculate overhead excluding provider compensation. See what overhead percentage is normal for a medical practice.
  2. Keep merchant fees out of revenue. Record the full sale as revenue and the fee as an expense, so revenue ties to the schedule.
  3. Do not create an account called "miscellaneous." It becomes a place where problems hide. Use a short "uncategorized" account for items awaiting a decision, and review it at every month end.
  4. Keep owner personal items out of the practice accounts. Use an owner distributions or draw account.

Setting up a chart of accounts for several locations or entities

Practices with several sites or several entities need extra care.

  1. One chart, many tags. All locations share account numbers. Use location tags to see each site's revenue and cost.
  2. Provider tags for provider level reporting. If you want profit by provider or surgeon, tag revenue and direct costs.
  3. Shared cost accounts and allocation. Central costs, such as a billing team or management salaries, live in their own accounts and are allocated by a written method. The allocation entry should be a monthly routine, not a one off.
  4. Intercompany accounts. If an operating company pays a management company, set up a matching pair of accounts so the balance eliminates in a combined report.
  5. Closing the books. Reconcile every bank, card and processor account before the review so the location reports are reliable.

Setup steps

  1. List the reports you want, and decide cash or accrual and gross or net revenue with your CPA.
  2. Start from the MGMA chart or a specialty template and cut it down to what you need.
  3. Add detail in revenue and overhead categories, number in blocks with gaps, and set up location, provider and class tags.
  4. Map accounts to the tax return with your CPA.
  5. If you change a chart mid year, keep a map from old accounts to new ones.
  6. Review at year end. Merge duplicates, remove unused accounts, and add one only when a report needs it.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What chart of accounts should a medical practice use?

Use the five standard sections: assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses. Add detail where you make decisions, mainly revenue by service line and expenses by overhead category. MGMA publishes a chart of accounts for medical groups that you can start from.

How many accounts should a practice have?

For one location, roughly 60 to 120 accounts is a common rule of thumb, not a published standard. More accounts do not give better information if staff code them inconsistently. Use tags for location and provider instead of separate accounts.

Should I have separate revenue accounts for each insurance company?

Usually not. Use service line accounts for revenue, and track payer mix from your practice management system or with a small set of payer groups. One account per insurer makes the chart long and the report hard to read.

Can I use a generic small business chart of accounts?

You can start with one, but it will not show service lines, patient receivables, memberships or refunds. Add those accounts and split provider pay from support staff pay, and a generic chart becomes a usable medical one. Have your CPA confirm the final list.

How do I set up a chart of accounts for several locations?

Use one chart for all of them with identical numbers and names. Tag each transaction with a location, put shared costs in separate accounts, and allocate them by a written method. For entities that bill each other, add matching intercompany accounts.

Can I change my chart of accounts mid year?

Yes, but do it with a mapping document so old and new accounts compare cleanly. Many practices make changes at the start of a fiscal year or at a clean month end after reconciling every account. Keep the mapping with your records.
