Which medical practice brokers sell small practices?

Short answer

Several brokers list small medical practices, including Tinsley Medical Practice Brokers, Strategic Medical Brokers and Cynergy CSI, while Nechay Advisors focuses on California practices. Firms such as Practice Transitions Group, Raincatcher and Hello Exit work more like M&A advisors and fit larger practices. Practice Transitions Group publishes a typical broker fee of 6% to 12% of the sale price, paid at closing, with smaller practices at the higher end. Pick a broker with recent sales in your specialty and size, a fee in writing, and a clear plan to keep the sale confidential.

Tinsley Medical Practice Brokers

Tinsley lists primary care practices, specialty practices and med spas for sale, and also offers practice appraisals, buy in and partnership work, and transition consulting. The firm says it has over 40 years of experience, is rooted in Texas, and that up to 40% of its deals happen across the country. Its current listings include primary care and family medicine practices with annual revenue under $1 million and above it.

Good fit: a primary care or specialty practice that wants a broker who lists smaller practices publicly, with a Texas base and national reach.

Strategic Medical Brokers

Strategic Medical Brokers says it specializes in medical practice brokerage and connects sellers with qualified buyers nationwide. Its services include practice valuation, confidential marketing, buyer representation and exit planning. The team lists certified transaction credentials (MCBI, M&AMI, CBI and CBB), and client stories on its site include solo, pediatric and direct primary care practices. It reports that 85% of the businesses it represents sell within 12 months, which is the firm's own figure.

Good fit: a solo or small group practice that wants a medical-only broker and expects to sell to another owner or a small group.

Cynergy CSI

Cynergy CSI says it has over 40 years of experience and more than 2,000 medical practice transitions. Its brokerage process runs from consultation and valuation through marketing, negotiation and transition support. It lists offices in Buffalo, New York and Naples, Florida, and serves practices across the United States. Client stories on its site include internal medicine and primary care owners, and a sale to a regional health system.

Good fit: an owner who wants valuation and brokerage from one firm, including a possible sale to a hospital or health system.

Nechay Advisors

Nechay Advisors works only on medical practices and surgery centers in California, from a Newport Beach base. It is led by a Certified Valuation Analyst and offers formal valuations as well as confidential brokerage, so one advisor sets the price and runs the sale. The firm says it has completed more than 500 valuations across 45 or more specialties.

Good fit: a California practice that wants a formal, defensible valuation and brokerage under one roof.

Practice Transitions Group

Practice Transitions Group (PTG) sells medical, dental, behavioral health, dermatology, med spa and veterinary practices. For medical sellers, it says it works with practices in the $2 million to $20 million revenue range and creates competition among MSOs, private equity groups and strategic buyers. PTG is one of the few firms that publishes fee guidance: 6% to 12% of the sale price, success based, with practices under about $400,000 of annual production often at 10% to 12%. It also lists a hybrid option of a $2,000 to $10,000 retainer plus 4% to 8% at closing.

Good fit: a larger single location practice or small group that may draw MSO or private equity interest.

Raincatcher

Raincatcher is a Denver based sell side advisor for lower middle market companies, with a medical practice page and named advisors for practice sales. Its site points owners with a valuation under $5 million to a separate business brokerage service. It reports more than 100 completed sales across industries.

Good fit: a practice that wants a generalist M&A firm with healthcare experience, or an owner with other businesses to sell as well.

Hello Exit

Hello Exit is a sell side M&A advisor for businesses valued from $1 million to $400 million. Its medical practice page is clear about fit: below roughly $1 million, it says a medical practice broker or transition specialist is usually the better route. It focuses on putting a practice in front of several buyer types at once, including private equity platforms, health systems and larger groups.

Good fit: a group worth more than about $1 million that wants a structured process with several buyers.

Broker, M&A advisor or selling direct

The right path depends on size and on who will buy.

  1. Practice broker. Lists and markets the practice, screens buyers and helps close. Best for a single location practice selling to another owner, an associate or a local group. PTG describes brokers as handling smaller, straightforward deals with individual buyers.
  2. M&A advisor. Runs a competitive process for larger practices, often with private equity, MSO or health system buyers. PTG says advisors usually handle more complex deals, often above $1 million, and may also charge a retainer.
  3. Selling direct. Works best when you already have a buyer, such as a partner or associate. You save the commission, but you take on valuation, paperwork, buyer screening and the lease. PTG estimates a sale takes 200 to 400 hours of work from listing to close. A healthcare attorney is still needed for the purchase agreement.

If an outside buyer has already approached you, an advisor can help you test that offer against the market before you answer.

How the listing process works

Most broker sales follow the same steps.

  1. Consultation and valuation. The broker reviews your financials and gives a price range. PTG lists a formal valuation at $1,500 to $4,000, often waived if you list.
  2. Listing agreement. You sign an engagement that sets the fee, the length of the exclusive period and what happens if you sell to someone the broker introduced after it ends.
  3. Confidential marketing. Buyers see a profile without your name and sign an NDA before they get details.
  4. Offers and letter of intent. The broker helps you compare price, cash at closing, your role after the sale and any noncompete.
  5. Due diligence and closing. The buyer reviews your books, payer contracts, billing and lease, and attorneys finish the purchase documents.

PTG puts most sales at 6 to 12 months from engagement to close. Read how to prepare a medical practice for sale before step 1.

Confidentiality

A sale that leaks can cost you staff and patients before you close. Ask each broker how it markets without naming you, when buyers sign an NDA, and who approves each buyer before your name is shared. PTG and Hello Exit both describe blind profiles and NDAs before any practice details are released. Plan with your broker when and how staff and patients will hear the news.

Fees, in plain numbers

  1. Success fee. PTG puts the typical range at 6% to 12% of the sale price, paid at closing.
  2. Retainer. Some brokers charge a retainer of $2,000 to $10,000, which may be credited at closing, per PTG.
  3. Minimum fee. Small practices may face a minimum fee when a percentage would not cover the work.
  4. Valuation. A formal written valuation may be separate.
  5. Other costs. PTG lists attorney fees of $7,000 to $10,000 for the purchase agreement, plus real estate commission if the building is part of the deal.

Most firms on this list do not publish a fee, so get it in writing and compare at least two quotes.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. How many practices in my specialty and size have you sold in the last 24 months?
  2. Who will run my sale day to day?
  3. What is the fee, when is it earned, and is there a retainer or minimum?
  4. How long is the exclusive period, and what does the tail clause cover?
  5. Do you represent only me, or the buyer as well?
  6. How will you keep the sale confidential, and when do buyers sign an NDA?
  7. How did you value my practice, and which add backs will a buyer accept?
  8. Which buyers do you expect: other owners, groups, health systems or private equity?

How to choose by practice type

  1. Solo primary care, pediatric or NP practice. Start with a medical-only broker that lists small practices, such as Tinsley, Strategic Medical Brokers or Cynergy CSI.
  2. California practice. Add Nechay Advisors for a formal valuation and a statewide buyer list.
  3. Group with $2 million or more in revenue. Talk to an M&A style firm such as PTG, and compare it with a broker.
  4. Practice worth more than about $1 million with private equity interest. Hello Exit or Raincatcher run a multi-buyer process.
  5. Selling to a partner or associate. Consider selling direct with a valuation, an attorney and clean books.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How much does a medical practice broker charge?

Practice Transitions Group publishes a typical range of 6% to 12% of the sale price, paid at closing. Smaller practices often pay toward the top of that range. Most other brokers do not publish a fee, so ask for it in writing.

Is my practice too small for a broker?

Usually not. Brokers such as Tinsley and Strategic Medical Brokers show solo and smaller practice sales on their sites. A very small practice may face a minimum fee instead of a percentage.

What is the difference between a broker and an M&A advisor?

A broker lists the practice and works with individual buyers on simpler deals. An M&A advisor runs a competitive process for larger practices, often with private equity or health system buyers, and may charge a retainer as well as a success fee.

Can I sell my medical practice without a broker?

Yes, if you already have a buyer such as a partner or associate. You will still need a valuation, a healthcare attorney and clean financial records. Without a broker you handle buyer screening, negotiation and the lease yourself.

How long does it take to sell a small medical practice?

Practice Transitions Group puts most sales at 6 to 12 months from engagement to closing. Clean books, a current lease and steady patient numbers tend to shorten it.

How do I keep the sale confidential?

Ask the broker to market a blind profile and to require an NDA before buyers see any details. Approve each buyer before your name is shared, and plan when staff and patients will hear the news.

What records will a buyer ask for?

Expect several years of financial statements and tax returns, collections by provider and service, the add backs that show true earnings, payer contracts and your lease. Numbers that tie from one report to the next build buyer confidence. See how is a medical practice valued.
