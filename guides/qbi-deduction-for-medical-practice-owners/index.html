Do medical practice owners get the QBI deduction?

Updated November 26, 2026

Home / Guides / Do medical practice owners get the QBI deduction?

Short answer

Yes, many medical practice owners get the QBI deduction, but their income limits are tighter than for most businesses. Owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships and S corporations can deduct up to 20 percent of qualified business income. A practice that provides clinical care is generally a specified service business (SSTB), so the regular deduction is full below a taxable income threshold, shrinks across a phase-in range, and ends above it. For tax year 2026 the threshold is $201,750 for most filers and $403,500 for joint filers. Employees, including owners paid only as employees of a C corporation, do not qualify, so confirm your case with your CPA.

What the QBI deduction is

The qualified business income deduction comes from Internal Revenue Code section 199A. The IRS says owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships, S corporations and some trusts and estates may qualify. Eligible taxpayers can deduct up to 20 percent of qualified business income, plus 20 percent of qualified REIT dividends and publicly traded partnership income. The deduction is available whether or not you itemize.

Qualified business income, or QBI, is the net profit from a qualifying business after the usual business deductions. The IRS notes that QBI reflects adjustments such as the deductible part of self-employment tax, the self-employed health insurance deduction and qualified retirement plan contributions. The deduction is also capped at 20 percent of your taxable income minus net capital gain.

Income earned as an employee is not QBI. Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5 says the trade or business of performing services as an employee is not a trade or business for section 199A. The IRS also says income earned through a C corporation is not eligible. If your practice is taxed as a C corporation, owner wages and corporate profit do not produce a QBI deduction.

Is the deduction still in effect for 2026?

Yes. The original law set an end date for tax years after 2025. Public Law 119-21 (the 2025 tax act) replaced that sunset language in section 199A(i) with a minimum deduction rule, per the current text of the statute. Ask your CPA about any change after the date of this guide.

Two 2026 changes matter to practice owners:

  1. A wider phase-in range. For tax years after 2025, the phase-in range is $75,000 above the threshold, or $150,000 for joint returns. It was $50,000 and $100,000 before.
  2. A minimum deduction. A taxpayer with at least $1,000 of QBI from active businesses gets a deduction of at least $400. Active means the owner materially participates in the business, under section 469(h). The statute adjusts these amounts for inflation after 2026.

Why a medical practice faces extra limits

Section 199A treats some fields as specified service trades or businesses, called SSTBs. Health is one of them. Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5(b)(2)(ii) describes the field of health as the provision of medical services by individuals such as pharmacists, nurses, dentists, veterinarians, physical therapists, psychologists and other similar healthcare professionals performing services in that capacity. Clinical practices generally fall in that group. Services not directly related to a medical services field, such as operating health clubs or health spas, payment processing, or the research, testing, manufacture or sale of pharmaceuticals or medical devices, are outside it.

The rule has a de minimis exception. If a business has gross receipts of $25 million or less and less than 10 percent of its receipts come from an SSTB service, it is not treated as an SSTB (Treasury Regulation 1.199A-5(c)(1)). A practice that is mostly clinical care will not meet that exception.

The practical result for an SSTB owner depends on taxable income, which is your income after deductions but before the QBI deduction.

Where wages and property come in

For an owner above the threshold, section 199A adds a wage and property limit. The deduction cannot exceed the greater of 50 percent of the W-2 wages the business pays, or 25 percent of those wages plus 2.5 percent of the unadjusted basis of qualified property. Inside the phase-in range, an SSTB owner uses a reduced version of the QBI, W-2 wages and property amounts. This is why a practice with many employees and expensive equipment is treated differently from a one person practice.

The wage limit matters mostly when an owner is inside the phase-in range, because above the range an SSTB owner gets no deduction. Below the threshold, neither the SSTB rule nor the wage limit applies. The IRS has two forms for the calculation, Form 8995 and Form 8995-A, and your CPA will use the one that fits your return.

S corporation owners: how salary affects the deduction

For S corporation owners, there is a link between pay and the deduction that is easy to miss. Under section 199A(c)(4), reasonable compensation paid to the owner for services is not QBI. Your W-2 wage from the practice is outside QBI, and the remaining profit that flows through is inside it.

That means a higher salary lowers QBI, and a lower salary raises QBI. It also means that cutting salary to raise QBI is not a plan. The IRS expects reasonable compensation, and a very low wage invites a payroll tax problem, as explained in our guide on S corp salary for practice owners. The wages the practice pays count toward the wage limit above, so salary can help in one place and hurt in another.

Salary and the deduction should be modeled together, using your real numbers and filing status. Lucid also has a post on how medical S corps set reasonable owner salaries.

Other things that change the answer

  1. Filing status and spouse income. The threshold uses taxable income on the return, so a spouse's income can push a joint return above it.
  2. Retirement plan contributions and other deductions. Deductions that lower taxable income can pull a filer under the threshold, which may restore a larger QBI deduction. The IRS says QBI itself is reduced for the deductible retirement and health insurance items. See tax deductions for practice owners.
  3. Several practices. Treasury Regulation 1.199A-4 sets rules that let some owners aggregate trades or businesses and treat them as a single business for certain limits. The rules have conditions, so ask your CPA whether they apply to you.
  4. Hospital employment plus a side practice. The W-2 job pays no QBI. Only the owned business can produce it.
  5. Rental income. If you own the building and rent it to the practice, the rental income can raise separate questions under section 199A. Ask your CPA.
  6. Partners. A partner reports a share of QBI from the partnership. Guaranteed payments to partners for services are not QBI under section 199A(c)(4)(B).
  7. State taxes. The deduction is a federal one. Ask your CPA how your state treats it.

A short example

Illustration only: these numbers are invented, rounded and not a prediction. A joint filing couple has $350,000 of taxable income before the QBI deduction. One spouse's S corporation practice shows $250,000 of QBI after her salary. Because $350,000 is under the 2026 joint threshold of $403,500, the practice is not limited by the SSTB rule or the wage limit. The QBI component is 20 percent of $250,000, or $50,000, and the deduction is then limited to 20 percent of taxable income minus net capital gain. Now assume the same couple has $450,000 of taxable income. They are inside the phase-in range, and only part of the deduction is allowed. At $600,000 they are above the range, and the regular calculation allows no deduction for the practice income. The point is the cliff: planning that moves taxable income down, such as retirement contributions, can matter more than usual.

What practice owners can do before year end

  1. Ask your CPA to project 2026 taxable income and see which column of the table you are in.
  2. If you are near the threshold, model retirement plan contributions, equipment purchases and the timing of income. Check the cash effect too.
  3. If you are an S corporation, review salary against both payroll tax and the deduction.
  4. Confirm your structure. The deduction is not available through a C corporation.
  5. Keep clean books. QBI is calculated from reported profit, so mistakes in categorization flow into the deduction. Bank and card records that match the return make the calculation faster.
  6. Track the 2027 limits. The IRS adjusts thresholds yearly, so do not reuse 2026 numbers next year.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Do medical practice owners qualify for the QBI deduction?

Many do, if they own a sole proprietorship, partnership or S corporation and have qualified business income. A practice that provides clinical care is generally a specified service business, so the regular deduction phases out as taxable income rises. Above the range the regular calculation allows no deduction for that income.

What is the income limit for the QBI deduction in 2026?

For 2026 the threshold is $201,750 for most filers and $403,500 for joint filers, per Rev. Proc. 2025-32. The phase-in range is $75,000 above the threshold, or $150,000 for joint returns. Confirm the figures for your year with your CPA.

Does my S corp salary reduce the QBI deduction?

Yes, it can. Reasonable compensation paid to the owner is not qualified business income under section 199A(c)(4). You still must pay a reasonable salary, so the two should be planned together.

Can I take QBI if I am an employee of a hospital or group?

Not on W-2 pay as an employee. The regulations say performing services as an employee is not a trade or business for section 199A. QBI can come only from a business you own, such as a separate practice.

Is the QBI deduction permanent?

Under the 2025 tax act (Public Law 119-21), the old end date for tax years after 2025 was replaced, and section 199A now includes a minimum deduction rule, per the current statute text. The thresholds still change each year with inflation. Tax law can change, so confirm with your CPA each year.

What is the minimum QBI deduction?

For tax years after 2025, a taxpayer with at least $1,000 of QBI from active businesses gets a deduction of at least $400. The owner must materially participate in the business. The amounts are adjusted for inflation after 2026.
