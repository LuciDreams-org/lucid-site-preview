What salary should an S corp owner take from a medical practice?

Home / Guides / What salary should an S corp owner take from a medical practice?

Short answer

An S corp owner who works in the practice must be paid reasonable compensation as a W-2 wage before taking any distributions. Reasonable means about what the practice would pay someone else to do the same work, based on training, duties, time worked and what comparable practices pay. There is no single percentage the IRS sets in the sources reviewed for this guide, so the number comes from a documented, defensible comparison, not a rule of thumb. This is general information for tax year 2026, not tax advice, so confirm your number with your CPA.

Why the salary question matters at all

An S corporation passes its profit through to its owners, who report it on their personal returns. Wages paid to an owner who works in the business carry payroll tax. Distributions of profit do not. That gap is the reason owners ask about S corporations, and it is the reason the IRS watches how owner pay is split.

The IRS states that S corporations must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee in return for services before non-wage distributions are made. The IRS also says officers who perform services are employees for employment tax purposes, which follows Treasury Regulation 31.3121(d)-1(b). In plain terms, you cannot be the practice's main clinician, pay yourself nothing, and take everything as a distribution.

For a medical practice this point is sharper than for many businesses. The owner's own patient work often produces most of the revenue. The IRS says wage treatment applies to the extent gross receipts come from the shareholder's personal services. A practice that bills for the owner's clinical time is therefore a clear case where a real wage is expected.

What the IRS says "reasonable" means

The IRS lists the factors it considers when it decides whether compensation is reasonable:

  1. Training and experience.
  2. Duties and responsibilities.
  3. Time and effort devoted to the business.
  4. Dividend (distribution) history.
  5. Payments to non-shareholder employees.
  6. Timing and manner of paying bonuses to key people.
  7. What comparable businesses pay for similar services.
  8. Compensation agreements.
  9. Use of a formula to determine compensation.

Read the list as a file you build, not a formula you run. For a practice owner, the comparison most often comes down to a few concrete questions.

  1. What would it cost to hire a clinician with your license, training and schedule to do your patient work?
  2. If you also manage the practice, what would it cost to hire a practice manager for those duties?
  3. How many hours a week do you actually work in each role?
  4. What do your associate clinicians earn, and how does your pay compare?

Salary survey data for your specialty and region, your associates' pay and a written note of your duties and hours are the kind of support that makes a salary defensible. Keep it with your tax records.

Is there a 60/40 rule or a percentage?

Owners often hear a fixed split, such as 60 percent salary and 40 percent distributions. The IRS page reviewed for this guide lists the factors above and does not give a percentage. Treat any fixed split you read online as a rough opening point for a conversation with your CPA, not as a safe harbor.

A fixed split can also mislead in both directions. A practice with a high-earning owner and several associates may owe a salary that is a small share of total profit, because associates generate part of that profit. A practice where the owner treats nearly every patient may owe a salary that is a large share of profit, because the profit is the owner's own labor.

What the courts have said

Two points come up in court decisions on S corporation pay.

First, the label on the payment does not control. In Rev. Rul. 74-44, 1974-1 C.B. 287, as described in the Watson opinion, an S corporation paid dividends to its two shareholder-employees and no wages, and the IRS treated those payments as made in lieu of reasonable compensation for employment tax purposes. Second, courts look at the facts. In David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States, 668 F.3d 1008 (8th Cir. 2012), an accountant's S corporation paid him $24,000 a year in 2002 and 2003 while he was a primary earner at an accounting firm and roughly $200,000 was distributed to his corporation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed the lower court, which found $24,000 was not reasonable and adopted $91,044, the market value figure from the government's expert, as the reasonable amount.

Watson involved an accountant, not a medical practice, so it is not a medical ruling. It is still the case advisers most often cite, because it shows that a very low salary beside large distributions invites recharacterization of distributions as wages, with back payroll taxes, penalties and interest. The IRS lists this case on its own compensation page.

What a salary does to the rest of your tax picture

Salary is not only a payroll tax question. It touches other numbers on your return and your plan, so a very low salary can cost you elsewhere.

  1. Payroll tax. IRS Publication 15 sets the Social Security tax at 6.2 percent and the Medicare tax at 1.45 percent, each paid by the employer and by the employee, which adds up to 15.3 percent combined. Social Security tax applies only up to the wage base, which is $184,500 for 2026 per Publication 15. Medicare tax has no wage base limit. As an arithmetic illustration (15.3 percent of $10,000), each extra $10,000 of salary below the wage base adds about $1,530 of combined payroll tax. That is the cost side of a higher salary.
  2. Retirement plan room. Employer contributions to a retirement plan are tied to compensation. A salary near zero can shrink what you can put into a plan. The IRS compensation cap used for plan contributions is $360,000 for 2026 (Publication 560). The employee deferral limit for 401(k) plans is $24,500 for 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67).
  3. The QBI deduction. Reasonable compensation paid to you is excluded from qualified business income under Internal Revenue Code section 199A(c)(4). A higher salary can lower the QBI deduction, and wages paid also feed the wage limits. See the companion guide on the QBI deduction for practice owners.
  4. Health insurance. Premiums paid by the S corporation for an owner holding more than 2 percent of the stock are reported as wages on the owner's W-2 and can be deducted by the owner when the rules are met, per the IRS compensation page.
  5. Loans and mortgages. Lenders often look at W-2 income. That is a practical reason to avoid an unusually low salary.

S corp, LLC or partnership: when does the S corp pay off?

Whether the election saves money depends on profit, salary and state rules, and on what you give up. The savings come only from profit that exceeds a reasonable salary. If the owner's reasonable salary is close to total profit, there is little left to take as a distribution, so the payroll tax saving is small. This is common in smaller practices where the owner is the main clinician.

Costs to weigh on the other side:

  1. A payroll system and the filings that come with it, including quarterly payroll returns and year end W-2s.
  2. A separate business return, Form 1120-S, which has a line for officer compensation, plus state filings.
  3. Tighter rules on how owners are paid. Pay must run through payroll, not through random transfers.
  4. State rules. Some states require licensed professionals to form a professional corporation or similar entity, and some states tax S corporations differently. Ask your attorney about entity formation and your CPA about state tax.
  5. Stricter limits if the practice has several owners with different roles and pay.

Practices with associates and strong profit may benefit more. A lower profit practice may break even or lose money on the change. Ask your CPA to run both versions with your real numbers before you elect.

How to set and document your salary: a practical checklist

  1. List every role you perform: treating clinician, medical director, practice manager, owner. Note hours for each.
  2. Gather outside comparisons for each role, such as published salary surveys or offer letters for similar associates.
  3. Compare to the pay of your own employees in similar roles.
  4. Choose a figure and write a short memo with the logic. Date it.
  5. Run the salary through payroll on a regular schedule, with withholding, not as an occasional lump.
  6. Review it every year. Profit, hours and duties change. A raise in profit with the same workload may not call for a raise in salary, while a drop in clinical hours might call for less.
  7. Keep distributions in line. Large distributions beside a small salary stand out.
  8. Fund quarterly estimated tax. Distributions have no withholding, so the owner owes income tax on pass-through profit. The Lucid post on estimated tax safe harbors for practice owners covers timing.

For a second view on the same topic written for practice owners, read Lucid's post on how medical S corps set reasonable owner salaries.

What if you already took too little?

If past salary looks low, speak to your CPA before the IRS does. Options include setting a reasonable salary going forward, and where the facts support it, correcting prior payroll filings. The right step depends on the years involved, the amounts and your state. Do not try to fix it by moving money around at year end, since the compensation decision is judged on the facts for the whole year.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is a reasonable salary for an S corp owner in a medical practice?

It is about what the practice would pay another qualified person to do the owner's work. The IRS looks at training, duties, time worked, comparable pay and other factors. There is no set percentage, so your CPA should document the number with outside comparisons.

Can I take all my pay as distributions from my practice S corp?

No. The IRS says an S corporation must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder who works in the business before it makes non-wage distributions. Taking only distributions can lead the IRS to treat them as wages. That can bring back payroll taxes, penalties and interest.

Is there a 60/40 rule for S corp salary?

The IRS sources reviewed for this guide do not give a fixed split. The 60/40 idea is a rule of thumb you will see online, not a safe harbor. The right split depends on your role, your hours, your associates and your local pay levels.

Does a higher salary lower my QBI deduction?

It can. Reasonable compensation paid to the owner is not qualified business income under section 199A(c)(4). Wages also feed the wage limits that apply at higher income. Your CPA can model salary and the QBI deduction together.

Should my medical practice be an S corp?

It depends on profit, owner pay, state rules and your goals. Savings come only from profit above a reasonable salary. Ask your CPA to compare staying as you are with electing S status, using your real numbers.

What happens if the IRS says my S corp salary was too low?

The IRS can recharacterize distributions as wages and assess payroll taxes, penalties and interest. Courts decide on the facts, as in Watson, where a $24,000 salary was rejected. Keep a written basis for your figure each year.
