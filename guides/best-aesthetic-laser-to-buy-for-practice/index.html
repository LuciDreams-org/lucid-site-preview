Which laser should my practice buy first?

Short answer

Buy the device for the treatment your own patients already ask for, not the brand with the best demo. For most practices that means one of five categories: laser hair removal, IPL or broadband light, skin resurfacing, body contouring or RF microneedling. Hair removal and IPL bring steady repeat visits, resurfacing and RF microneedling earn more per treatment, and body contouring needs the most marketing. Whichever you pick, run the payback math on the volume you can book in month three, not the volume the sales rep shows you.

Start with demand, not the device

The national numbers point the same way. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons 2025 statistics report says neuromodulator injections were about half of all minimally invasive treatments, and hyaluronic acid fillers about another 30 percent. Laser skin treatments, hair removal and light-based therapies, tattoo removal and other fillers made up the rest. So if you do not offer injectables yet, start there: they need almost no capital. A Dermatology Times article on a first laser purchase quotes one dermatologist: "it's a lot cheaper to start with injectables."

Among devices, a Dermatology Times piece citing the 2019 American Society for Dermatologic Surgery procedure survey lists the most common laser, light and energy-based treatments as sun damage and wrinkles, then facial redness, laser hair removal, melasma and brown patches, acne, scars, tattoo removal and birthmarks.

National data only sets the frame. Apex Aesthetic Consulting's buyer checklist makes the better point: the best market research is your own patient base. Before you take a sales call, pull three numbers:

  1. How many patients asked for a treatment you do not offer in the last six months.
  2. How many you referred out for it.
  3. How many open provider hours and rooms you have each week.

Five categories and the devices in each

1. Laser hair removal

Hair removal is sold in a series, so one patient books many visits, and the work is often delegated under state rules. Two common platforms:

  1. Candela GentleMax Pro Plus. Candela calls it the latest version of its Gentle Pro series, and its brochure lists dual wavelengths to treat all skin types and large spot sizes for fast treatments.
  2. Lumenis Splendor X. Lumenis says it is the only FDA cleared system that fires Alexandrite (755 nm) and Nd:YAG (1064 nm) at the same time, with square spots up to 27 by 27 mm.

What patients pay: CareCredit's cost guide (October 23, 2026) puts the national average per session from $180 to $927 depending on the area, for example $195 for the chin and $927 for the back.

Limit: hair removal is the category where chains and discount offers compete hardest on price.

2. IPL, broadband light and vascular lasers

These treat redness, sun damage, brown spots and veins, which overlaps with what a medical dermatology practice already sees.

  1. Lumenis Stellar M22. Lumenis says it treats 30 plus indications with four laser and light technologies and 850 plus presets.
  2. Sciton BBL HEROic. Sciton's own launch post (April 2024) calls it the first device in aesthetics with Autodynamic Pulsing, an automated pulse delivery system.
  3. Cutera excel V+. Cutera describes it as a dual wavelength vascular and pigment laser for rosacea, facial and leg veins, angiomas and port wine stains.

What patients pay: CareCredit cites an ASPS average fee for IPL of $412 for 2020. That figure is old, so check local prices.

Limit: a lower price per session than resurfacing, so volume carries the payback.

3. Skin resurfacing

  1. Solta Fraxel FTX. Solta calls it the original fractional laser for wrinkles, surgical and acne scars and sun damage, with two wavelengths.
  2. Solta Clear + Brilliant. Positioned by Solta as a lighter, preventive resurfacing treatment for the practice.
  3. Alma Harmony XL Pro. A multi-application platform that Alma says covers ablative and non-ablative treatments across skin types.

What patients pay: CareCredit (June 12, 2026) gives an average of $2,509 for ablative and $1,445 for non-ablative resurfacing.

Limit: downtime and longer consults. Fewer treatments a month, so each one has to earn more.

4. Body contouring

  1. Allergan Aesthetics CoolSculpting Elite. Fat freezing; Allergan says the Elite system treats two areas at once.
  2. BTL Emsculpt NEO. BTL says it combines HIFEM for muscle with synchronized RF heating for fat in a 30 minute session.

What patients pay: CareCredit (July 24, 2026) puts the national average for one CoolSculpting treatment at $1,602, with a range of $1,213 to $2,599.

Limit: patients compare prices online and take longer to decide, so plan a bigger marketing budget than for the other categories.

5. RF microneedling

  1. InMode Morpheus8. InMode calls it the first and only FDA cleared fractional radiofrequency device for soft tissue contraction, with four interchangeable tips.

What patients pay: CareCredit (July 17, 2026) puts the national average for microneedling at $938. That figure covers microneedling in general, not only RF, so check local RF prices.

Limit: single use tips add a real cost to every treatment. Ask for the tip price in writing.

Used or certified pre-owned devices

A used device can cut the price sharply. Before you buy, confirm the maker will service it and transfer software, what warranty comes with it, the service history, and whether consumables are still sold for that model. Some makers will not service units sold outside their authorized channel.

What do lasers cost?

Most makers do not publish device prices. You get a quote from a rep. One exception on November 26, 2026: Candela's eStore listed its Glace facial system at a $21,900 total device price in a 12 month 0% payment example. Treat every quote as a starting point and compare the total cost: device, handpieces, consumables, service plan, shipping, installation and training.

For loans, leases and vendor plans, see should my med spa lease or buy a laser and medical equipment financing companies compared.

Payback math: compare two devices side by side

Payback depends on three numbers: contribution per treatment (price minus direct costs), treatments a month, and the full cost to launch. Here is the same math run on two device types.

Illustrative example, not a quote

Every number below is made up to show the math. None is a real price, quote or result from any vendor or practice.

Device price$100,000$80,000

Direct cost per treatment$60$250

Now cut the volume in half: 20 hair removal treatments and 7 RF treatments a month. Payback stretches to about 33 months and about 22 months. Same devices, same prices, only volume moved.

Three takeaways:

  1. Volume decides payback more than the device price does.
  2. A high contribution per treatment protects you when volume is slow.
  3. Direct cost matters. Tips, gel, numbing cream, provider pay and card fees all come out of every treatment.

Training, marketing and the costs reps leave out

  1. Service contracts. Dermatology Times warns that service contracts can run up to $10,000 a year, plus consumables, maintenance and follow-up visits that take up paid time.
  2. Training. Ask what training is included, how many staff it covers and what extra sessions cost. Until your team is confident, the device runs below capacity.
  3. Marketing. Practice Layer notes that published estimates of what it costs to win a new aesthetic patient vary widely, from a $68 median to bands of $100 to $350. Budget a launch campaign and a monthly line, and track cost per booked patient.
  4. Room and schedule. The same Practice Layer analysis cites device models where a $170,000 laser pays back in about 15 to 16 months at 35 treatments a month and about 47 months at 20. Check you have the room hours and staff for the volume you need.
  5. Site prep. Some devices need dedicated circuits, ventilation or plume evacuation. Ask for a site survey before you sign.

How to choose by practice type

  1. Medical dermatology adding cosmetic work. IPL or a vascular laser fits patients you already treat for redness, sun damage and brown spots. Book cosmetic revenue on its own line from day one. See Lucid for dermatology practices and accounting for dermatology practices.
  2. New med spa. Hair removal or IPL brings repeat visits that fill the schedule while injectables grow. See Lucid for med spas.
  3. Established med spa with injectable demand. RF microneedling or resurfacing sells well to existing patients, and the higher price per treatment pays back on less volume.
  4. Plastic surgery practice. Resurfacing and RF microneedling pair with surgical patients before and after procedures, and body contouring suits patients who are not ready for surgery. See Lucid for plastic surgery practices.
  5. Several locations. Decide whether one device rotates or each site gets its own, then run payback per site.

If you run a medical dermatology practice and are still deciding whether to add cosmetic work at all, read Should my dermatology practice add cosmetic services?

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is the best first laser for a med spa?

There is no single best device. Pick the category your patients already ask for. Hair removal and IPL bring steady repeat visits, while resurfacing and RF microneedling earn more per treatment. Run the payback math on bookings you can prove.

Which aesthetic device pays back fastest?

The one with the highest monthly contribution at a volume you can really book. In our illustrative example, RF microneedling paid back in about 10 months at 15 treatments a month and hair removal in about 15 months at 40. Your numbers will differ.

Should a dermatology practice buy a laser before offering injectables?

Usually not. Injectables need little capital, and ASPS data shows neuromodulators and fillers make up most minimally invasive treatments. Add a device once you have a cosmetic patient base to sell it to.

Do laser companies publish their prices?

Most do not. You get a quote from a sales rep. Compare the total cost: device, handpieces, consumables, service plan, installation and training.

Is a used laser a good idea?

It can be, if the maker will service it and the price reflects its age and warranty. Confirm the service history, software transfer and consumable supply before you buy.

How much should I budget for training and marketing?

Ask the maker what training is included and what extra sessions cost. For marketing, published estimates of the cost to win a new aesthetic patient vary widely, so set a launch budget and track cost per booked patient each month.

How do I know if my laser is profitable?

Give the device its own line in the books: revenue, direct costs, payment and service plan. Compare its monthly contribution with the payment each month, and run it per location if you have several.
