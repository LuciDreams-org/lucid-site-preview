How do I finance buying a medical practice?

Short answer

Most buyers finance a practice purchase with an SBA 7(a) loan or a conventional practice loan from a bank with a healthcare lending team, often with a seller note on top. SBA rules set a 10 percent minimum equity injection for a first acquisition and ban seller earnouts inside an SBA deal, while bank practice loans can fund up to 100 percent on some programs. Partner buy-ins use smaller, separate loans, and selling to a group is the alternative for owners on the other side. Whatever you choose, the lender decides on the seller's numbers and your forecast, so clean books come first.

SBA 7(a) loan

The SBA does not lend. It guarantees part of a loan made by a bank, which lets the bank take on goodwill, the biggest asset in most practice sales. SBA lists "changes of ownership (complete or partial)" as an eligible 7(a) use and caps most 7(a) loans at $5 million. Maturities run up to 10 years, or up to 25 years when the loan finances real estate.

Rates float within SBA caps. For loans over $350,000, the maximum is the base rate plus 3.0 percent, with higher caps for smaller loans. SBA also charges a guaranty fee that it resets each fiscal year, so ask the lender for the current figure.

The rules that matter most for buyers are in SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026:

  1. Equity. An Initial Acquisition (a new owner buying the practice) needs at least 10 percent of the total project cost, and that floor cannot be reduced.
  2. Seller debt. A seller note counts toward equity only if it is subordinated and on full standby, meaning no principal or interest payments for the life of the SBA loan. Standby debt, seller notes and small minority investors together can cover no more than half of the required injection.
  3. Valuation. The lender must order an independent business valuation from an accredited appraiser. If you pay more than the valuation supports, the gap must come from equity.
  4. Quality of earnings. Above a $3 million purchase price, the lender also needs a quality of earnings report, including a cash proof that ties bank statements to the P&L and tax returns.
  5. Cash flow. Initial Acquisitions need debt service coverage of at least 1.25 times, based on the last fiscal year or the average of the last two.
  6. The seller. In an Initial Acquisition, the seller may not stay on as an owner, officer or employee. The practice can contract the seller as a consultant for up to 24 months in total.

Rule 6 matters for practices where the seller planned to keep treating patients for a year or two. Raise it with your lender early.

Where SBA is strong: long terms on goodwill and lower monthly payments. Limits: more paperwork, slower closings and the equity rules above. Compare lenders in best SBA lenders for medical practices.

Bank practice loan

Several banks run healthcare lending teams that make conventional practice loans, with or without SBA. Published features vary. Bank of America says its dental practice loans offer "up to 100% financing based on the type of financing you are seeking" and that working capital is included with acquisition loans. U.S. Bank says it offers up to 100 percent funding for buying a dental practice, terms up to 15 years and up to six months of interest only payments. PNC lists acquisition loans for renovation, equipment upgrades and other transition costs. Rates on all three: see the lender's site.

Where they are strong: higher financing percentages, faster closings and the option to fund working capital in the same loan. Limits: rates and terms are by quote, and many banks ask you to move your operating accounts to them. Seller financing

A seller note means the seller is paid part of the price over time. It closes a gap between price and loan size, and it keeps the seller invested in a smooth handover.

Inside an SBA loan, the note counts toward your equity only on full standby, and seller debt from an SBA deal can be refinanced after it has been in place and current for 36 months. Outside SBA, the bank and seller agree on payments, interest and subordination.

Set the note's interest rate, schedule and what happens if collections fall in the purchase paperwork, and get both lawyers and a CPA to review it.

Earnouts

An earnout pays the seller more later if the practice hits targets, such as collections retained 12 months after close. It shifts risk to the seller when patients may follow the seller out the door.

SOP 50 10 8.1 says plainly: "Seller earnouts are prohibited" in 7(a) change of ownership deals. It allows the opposite, a buyer rebate based on performance, but any rebate must pay down the SBA loan. So an earnout only works with conventional or seller financing. Define the measure tightly (collections, not production) and who keeps the books it is measured on.

Partner buy-ins

Buying a share of an existing group is a different deal. You buy part of the equity, the selling partner usually stays, and the price is tied to the group's profit and distribution history.

Lenders treat it as its own product. Panacea Financial says its partner buy-in loan "does not require a lien on the existing practice, real estate, or equipment." PNC and U.S. Bank also list buy-ins among practice loan uses. SBA can fund some owner buyouts, with limits on how much equity an outside buyer can take. Before you sign, ask for three years of the group's financials and your expected distributions.

Selling to a group instead

For owners on the sell side, a group, dental support organization or private equity backed platform may buy the practice outright. That is not a loan, and the buyer sets the terms. The valuation logic is the same, so start with how a medical practice is valued.

What the buyer's lender needs

Every route above asks for the same core package, in two halves.

From the seller: three years of tax returns and financial statements, a current interim P&L and balance sheet, collections by provider and payer, AR aging, the add-back schedule behind the asking price, and the lease.

From you: personal tax returns, a personal financial statement, proof of the cash for your equity, a CV showing your production, and a projection. The projection should cover the payment, your own pay, staff, rent and the cash gap while the new entity gets paid.

The full checklist and the ratios lenders test are in what lenders look for in a practice loan.

How the purchase shows up in the books

This is where many buyers get surprised after close.

Purchase price allocation. In an asset purchase, the price is split across equipment, supplies, records, non-compete and goodwill. Buyer and seller both report the split to the IRS on Form 8594. The split drives your tax: goodwill is a section 197 intangible, amortized over 15 years (180 months), while equipment may be depreciated faster. Agree on the allocation before closing, not after.

The loan. The loan sits on the balance sheet. Each payment splits into interest, a P&L expense, and principal, which reduces the loan but is not an expense. That is why a profitable new owner can feel cash poor. Loan fees are usually spread over the loan term rather than expensed on day one. Your CPA confirms the treatment.

Opening balance sheet. The new entity starts with the acquired assets, the loan, any seller note and your equity. Set it up from the closing statement in month one, with a chart of accounts built for a practice.

Patient and insurance receivables. In many asset deals the seller keeps receivables from before close. Payments for the seller's visits will land in your account for weeks, so track them and pass them on as the paperwork says.

Payer timing. If the new entity needs new payer enrollment or contracts, insurance payments can slow for a period. Build that into the cash forecast and size working capital to cover it.

Earnouts and seller notes. Both create liabilities. How and when an earnout is recorded depends on your accounting basis, so decide it with your CPA at close.

How to choose

  1. First practice, most of the price is goodwill, limited cash: SBA 7(a), with a standby seller note if needed.
  2. Strong production history and a bank relationship: a conventional practice loan, which can fund more of the price.
  3. Price above what the lender will fund: a seller note, on standby if SBA is in the deal.
  4. Worried patients leave with the seller: an earnout or rebate, which rules out SBA for the earnout itself.
  5. Buying into a group: a partner buy-in loan, after reviewing the group's books.
  6. Dental buyer: start with best dental practice loans and lenders.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

How much down payment do I need to buy a medical practice?

With an SBA 7(a) loan, a new owner needs at least 10 percent of the total project cost, and SBA does not allow that floor to be reduced for a first acquisition. Some bank practice loans advertise up to 100 percent financing. Ask each lender what applies to your deal.

Can I use an SBA loan to buy a medical practice?

Yes. SBA lists changes of ownership as an eligible 7(a) use, up to $5 million. Expect an independent valuation, debt service coverage of at least 1.25 times for a first acquisition, and the rule that the seller cannot stay on as an employee.

Can the seller finance part of the purchase?

Yes. With SBA, a seller note counts toward your equity only on full standby, with no payments for the life of the SBA loan, and covers at most half of the required injection. Outside SBA, the terms are whatever you and the bank agree.

Can I use an earnout with an SBA loan?

No. SBA's SOP 50 10 8.1 prohibits seller earnouts in 7(a) change of ownership deals. A buyer rebate based on performance is allowed, but it must pay down the SBA loan.

Can the selling owner keep working after the sale?

In an SBA Initial Acquisition, the seller may not remain as an employee or officer. The practice can hire the seller as a consultant for up to 24 months. Conventional loans may allow more, so ask the lender.

What does a lender need from me to fund an acquisition?

The seller's tax returns, financial statements and add-backs, plus your personal financial statement, proof of equity and a projection. See what lenders look for in a practice loan.

How is goodwill from a practice purchase taxed?

Goodwill is a section 197 intangible, amortized over 15 years for tax. Equipment may be depreciated faster, so the price split on Form 8594 matters. Your CPA confirms the result for your deal.

Why does a profitable practice feel short on cash after a purchase?

Loan principal leaves the bank account but is not an expense on the P&L, and payer timing can slow collections after a change of owner. A cash forecast that shows both prevents surprises.
