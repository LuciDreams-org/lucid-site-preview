How much does a medical billing company cost?

Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

Most medical billing companies charge a percentage of what they collect, commonly 4 to 10 percent, with 5 to 8 percent typical for small and mid size practices. Others charge about $3 to $12 per claim or a fixed monthly fee, and many add minimums, setup fees and extras such as credentialing or patient statements. So 6 percent is a normal quote, but only the all in cost at your own collections tells you if it is fair or cheaper than an in house biller. Book the fee as its own expense line so you can track it as a percent of collections every month.

Percent of collections

This is the most common model. The vendor takes a share of what it collects, so it earns more when you are paid more. A Tebra survey of 190 US billing companies, as reported by Med Precision Billing, found 63 percent charge 7.99 percent or less, and only 4 percent charge 10 percent or more.

Published examples:

  1. Practolytics: behavioral health plans from 3.49 to 4.99 percent of net collections, with a $750 monthly minimum and setup fees waived on 12 month agreements.
  2. Med Precision Billing: 7 percent for solo practices and 6 percent for groups of 2 to 15 providers, no setup fee.
  3. AdvancedMD: managed RCM at 4 to 8 percent of collections, with setup from free to $1,000 for smaller practices, per Med Precision Billing's review.
  4. CareCloud: its annual report describes a percentage of revenue with a monthly minimum and a one time setup fee.

Watch for: what counts as a collection. Patient payments, old AR you hand over, secondary claims and payments that arrive after you leave can all be included.

Per claim

You pay a set fee for each claim. Tebra's guide puts many quotes at $3 to $10, and RCM Matter cites about $3 to $12. It is predictable if the paperwork defines a billable claim clearly.

Watch for: corrected, secondary, rejected and resubmitted claims billed again. A low per claim fee can end up near a percentage quote once add ons are counted.

Fixed monthly fee

A fixed amount each month, often set by provider count, location count or claim volume. RCM Matter lists about $500 to $6,000 or more. It suits practices with steady volume.

Watch for: volume bands. If you add a provider, the fee can jump to the next band. Ask what the fee covers when claim counts spike, for example in flu season or after a new provider starts, and whether the vendor can reprice in the middle of the term.

Hybrid and minimums

Some vendors charge a base fee plus a lower percentage, or a percentage with a monthly minimum. Minimums protect the vendor in slow months. Practolytics publishes a $750 minimum, and CareCloud describes one in its filings.

Watch for: the minimum in your slowest month, not your average one. A new location or a provider on leave can push collections under the minimum, and then the effective rate climbs fast.

Dedicated staff

Some offshore firms rent you billing staff by the month. OutsourceRCM, formerly MedBillingExperts, lists $1,300 to $1,800 per full time person a month and calls the price tentative. You get hours, not outcomes, so someone in house has to direct and check the work.

In house biller

An in house biller costs salary, payroll tax, benefits, software, training and cover for time off. The Bureau of Labor Statistics median wage for medical records specialists was $51,140 in May 2025, a broad occupation figure that RCM Matter cites. Med Precision Billing's example for a 2 provider family practice puts the all in cost at about $80,000 a year, or 10.7 percent of $750,000 in collections. Your numbers will differ.

What is usually included, and what is extra

There is no standard bundle. Ask the vendor to mark each item as included, excluded or priced separately.

Usually in the base fee: eligibility checks, charge entry, claim submission, payment posting, denial follow up and monthly reports.

Often extra:

  1. Setup, data conversion and clearinghouse enrollment. A Tebra survey found 39 percent of billing companies always or often charge setup fees.
  2. Credentialing and enrollment. 72 percent offer it as an add on, per the same survey.
  3. Patient statements, postage and patient call support.
  4. Coding review, prior authorization and appeals.
  5. Old AR cleanup, and work on open claims after you leave.
  6. Software and clearinghouse fees passed through to you.
  7. Early termination fees and annual price increases.

How to compare quotes

Turn every quote into one number: the effective rate.

Effective rate = (yearly billing fees + setup spread over the year + software + other extras) divided by yearly net collections.

Med Precision Billing's example: a 4.5 percent quote, plus $400 a month for software, $300 a month for patient billing and a $4,000 setup fee, on $80,000 in monthly collections. The effective rate is 5.79 percent, not 4.5.

RCM Matter's example for $75,000 in monthly collections and 650 claims a month: 6 percent is $4,500 a month, $6 per claim is $3,900, and a $4,200 flat fee sits between them. Add the extras before you pick.

Steps:

  1. Send every vendor the same 12 months of collections, claim counts and payer mix.
  2. Ask for the percent, minimum, setup fee and every add on in writing.
  3. Ask what counts as a collection, and what happens to open claims if you leave.
  4. Compute the effective rate for each quote and for your in house option.
  5. Compare service levels too: days in AR, denial rate and reports by payer and location.

How billing fees show up in the books

Billing cost is one of the larger overhead lines in many practices, but it often hides.

  1. Record gross collections as revenue and the billing fee as its own expense. If a biller nets its fee out of what it remits, gross it back up so revenue is not understated.
  2. Keep separate expense lines for the billing service, clearinghouse, patient statements and credentialing. Lumped together, they are hard to manage.
  3. For an in house team, add the biller's payroll and the billing software to get the true cost.
  4. On accrual books, match each month's fee to that month's collections. A setup fee is an expense when charged, but spread it over the term when you compare quotes.
  5. Track billing cost as a percent of collections and your net collection rate every month. If the fee rises while the collection rate falls, raise it with your biller.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is 6 percent of collections a fair price for medical billing?

It is inside the common 5 to 8 percent range. Whether it is fair depends on what is included, your specialty and your volume. Compute the effective rate with all extras before you decide.

Is outsourced billing cheaper than an in house biller?

Not always. A percentage grows with collections, while a biller's salary does not. Compare the full in house cost, including benefits, software and cover, against the effective rate at your own collections.

How much do medical billing companies charge per claim?

Vendor guides cite about $3 to $10 or $3 to $12 per claim. Check whether resubmitted and secondary claims cost extra.

Do billing companies charge a setup fee?

Many do. A Tebra survey found 39 percent always or often charge one. Some, such as Practolytics on 12 month agreements, waive it.

What is a monthly minimum?

It is the least you pay in any month, even if collections are low. Practolytics publishes a $750 minimum. Ask how often the minimum would have applied in the last year.

How should I record billing company fees?

Book collections as revenue and the billing fee as a separate expense. Keep clearinghouse, statements and credentialing on their own lines. Then track the total as a percent of collections.
