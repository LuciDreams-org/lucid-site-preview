Home / Guides / What is a quality of earnings report for a practice sale?

Short answer

A quality of earnings report, or QoE, is a financial review that tests whether the earnings a practice's price is based on are real, supported by records and likely to continue under a new owner. Buyers commission one on almost every institutional deal, and sellers can order their own sell side QoE before going to market. It tests revenue against bank deposits, collections and receivables, every add back, owner pay and working capital, and anything it cannot support comes out of adjusted EBITDA before the multiple is applied. You pass it with accrual monthly books, a documented add back file and clean receivables, ideally prepared a year or more ahead.

QoE, audit or valuation?

Owners often mix these up. They answer different questions.

ReviewQuestion it answersWho orders it
Buy side QoEAre these earnings real, and what should we pay for them?The buyer or its lender
Sell side QoEWhat will a buyer's QoE find, and can we fix it first?The seller, before market
AuditDo the financial statements follow accounting standards?Owners, lenders, regulators
ValuationWhat is the practice worth?Seller, buyer, a court or a lender

A QoE is a consulting engagement, not an audit, so no opinion or assurance is issued, per FISART's seller guide. A valuation usually relies on the earnings figure a QoE verifies, as the California dental CPA firm of Fazel Mostashari puts it.

What buyers check

Buyer QoE teams start from your reported EBITDA and build a bridge to the figure they will pay a multiple on. EY's health sector due diligence overview groups adjustments into four types: out of period items, noncash items, one time or unusual items, and pro forma items. In a practice, the review usually covers:

  1. Revenue and collections, tied to bank deposits.
  2. Receivables, and whether past balances were actually collected.
  3. Add backs and owner pay.
  4. Payer, provider and referral concentration.
  5. Working capital and debt like items owed at closing.
  6. Billing and coding, often by a separate expert.

How revenue and collections get tested

The core test is proof of cash. Fazel Mostashari's guide describes tying collections in the practice software to bank deposits month by month for at least the trailing twelve months, then tying both to gross receipts on the tax return. PYA's due diligence checklist adds collections as a percentage of charges by month and payer, days in accounts receivable, and charges and net revenue per procedure, payer and provider.

Then comes gross to net. EY notes that not all payers reimburse the same amount for the same service, many practices do not load contracts into their billing systems, and denials and bad debt complicate the estimate. So the QoE team checks whether your recorded revenue and receivables match the cash that later arrived. Revenue that was billed but never collected is removed.

HealthValue Group lists what a quality of revenue review flags as not recurring: one time payer settlements, relief funds, a surge right before the measurement date, and revenue tied to a retiring founder or a high producing provider who will not stay. Red flags in Fazel Mostashari's guide include software collections that do not match deposits or the tax return, a spike in collections just before the sale, and growing patient credit balances.

How add backs get tested

Each add back needs a document and a reason it will not recur. The QoE team reviews owner pay, benefits, retirement contributions and perks, related party costs such as rent to a building you own or family on payroll, and one time costs, which they confirm with invoices.

They also add pro forma or run rate adjustments that cut the other way: an open position the buyer will have to fill, recent raises, a new lease, or staff paid below market who will expect raises. Market pay for the owner's clinical role is deducted, because the buyer must pay someone to do that work.

Every supported dollar matters. Precision Dental Analytics gives the arithmetic: defending a single $100,000 add back at a 7x multiple preserves about $700,000 of value.

Common findings that cut the price

These come up again and again in the sources above.

  1. Add backs with no paperwork, or "one time" costs that recur every year.
  2. Collections that do not tie to bank deposits or the tax return.
  3. Cash basis books that put revenue in the wrong month, which hides real trends.
  4. Receivables that were never collected, so revenue and AR are overstated.
  5. Patient and insurer credit balances owed back, treated as debt.
  6. Prepaid packages, memberships or treatment plans, where the buyer must deliver care the seller was already paid for.
  7. Paid time off owed to staff, often settled by the seller at closing.
  8. Related party rent reset to market.
  9. Revenue that depends on one provider or one payer.
  10. Coding or documentation problems, which can turn into escrows, special indemnities or a lower price, per Med Contract Law.

Working capital matters too. EY's purchase price bridge shows that the price is enterprise value adjusted for working capital against a target, less debt and debt like items. A Forvis Mazars leader quoted by ACG gives the plain version: if you promised $100 of working capital and deliver $80, the price drops by $20.

A simple illustration, with made up numbers. A practice presents $900,000 of adjusted EBITDA. The buyer's QoE removes $40,000 of unsupported add backs, $25,000 of "one time" marketing that recurs, $18,000 to reset related party rent to market, and $30,000 for an open position. Adjusted EBITDA falls to $787,000. At 6x, the price drops from $5,400,000 to $4,722,000, a cut of $678,000. This is an illustration, not a client result.

Sell side or buy side, and what it costs

A sell side QoE is your own review before buyers see the numbers. CohnReznick recommends one to get a realistic view of value and beneficial add backs, and says that if done well it should more than pay for itself. ACG, reporting GF Data's review of 360 deals completed since Q3 2024, found an average 7.4x EBITDA multiple with a sell side QoE against 7.0x without one, with the lift mostly in deals above $50 million of enterprise value. Smaller deals tended not to see a valuation boost, but advisers quoted by ACG said a sell side report moves the deal faster over 90% of the time.

Buyers will still run their own QoE. Your report gives them a head start and shows you the problems while you can still fix them.

Who does it

Choose by deal size and specialty. Firm names below are examples from public sources, not endorsements.

  1. Boutique and specialty QoE firms: suited to smaller practices, often under $1 million of EBITDA. Ask for healthcare deals they have done.
  2. Regional and national CPA and advisory firms such as Forvis Mazars, Cherry Bekaert, Wipfli and Eide Bailly: suited to groups selling to institutional buyers.
  3. Healthcare specialist valuation and advisory firms such as VMG Health, PYA and Kroll: suited to larger or more complex healthcare deals, including compliance and fair market value questions.
  4. Dental specialists such as Precision Dental Analytics and StrategX Advisory: Precision Dental Analytics also offers a clinical QoE that tests procedure level coding patterns, which a financial QoE does not open.

Ask any provider how many practice deals they have done in your specialty, whether they test proof of cash and gross to net, how they treat cash basis books, and who on their team does the work. Your longtime CPA may be excellent at tax but new to transactions; ask.

How to prepare for a QoE

  1. Close books monthly on accrual, at least for reporting. See cash versus accrual.
  2. Reconcile practice system collections to bank deposits every month, and keep the reconciliations.
  3. Build an add back file as costs happen, with invoices and a one line reason.
  4. Move personal costs off the practice books.
  5. Run AR aging monthly, write off what is truly uncollectible and refund old credit balances.
  6. Track prepaid packages and memberships as deferred revenue.
  7. Report profit by provider, location and payer, so concentration is visible and explained.
  8. Use a clear chart of accounts. See chart of accounts for a medical practice.
  9. Consider a coding and billing review 6 to 12 months before market.

How it differs by practice type

  1. Dental: production versus collections by provider, PPO write offs and prepaid ortho cases get close attention. See dental.
  2. Dermatology: medical, cosmetic and retail revenue are tested and valued separately. See dermatology.
  3. Orthopedics: procedure margin, implant costs and surgery center or facility splits. See orthopedics.
  4. Med spa: packages, memberships and gift cards as deferred revenue, plus provider dependence. See med spa.
  5. Groups with several locations or entities: consolidation, intercompany balances and management services fees. See medical groups.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is a quality of earnings report in a practice sale?

It is a financial review that tests whether the earnings behind the price are real, documented and likely to continue. It builds a bridge from reported EBITDA to adjusted EBITDA, and the buyer applies its multiple to that adjusted number. It is not an audit and not a valuation.

Who pays for the quality of earnings report?

The buyer pays for its own buy side QoE. A seller can pay for a sell side QoE before going to market to find and fix issues first. Buyers usually still run their own review.

How much does a quality of earnings report cost?

Breakwater M&A publishes typical fees of $10,000 to $25,000 under $1 million of EBITDA and $15,000 to $40,000 from $1 million to $3 million, across industries. Healthcare work can cost more. Get a fixed fee quote in writing.

How long does a QoE take?

Breakwater lists 3 to 4 weeks for the smallest deals and 4 to 8 weeks for most others. Messy books add time. Organized reconciliations and an add back file shorten it.

What findings lower the price?

Unsupported or recurring add backs, collections that do not tie to deposits, uncollected receivables, credit balances owed back, prepaid care the buyer must deliver, and dependence on one provider or payer. Each dollar removed from EBITDA costs a multiple of that dollar in price.

Should I get a sell side QoE?

Often yes for institutional sales, because it shows you what a buyer will find while you can still fix it and tends to speed diligence. GF Data found the price lift mostly in larger deals, so for a small sale to a local buyer, clean books and a CPA review may be enough.
