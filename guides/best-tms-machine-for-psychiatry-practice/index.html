Short answer

Most psychiatry groups shortlist NeuroStar (Neuronetics) and BrainsWay Deep TMS, then compare MagVenture and Magstim Horizon, with Ampa One, CloudTMS, Apollo and Nexstim as other FDA cleared options. No trial has shown one maker's machine beats another for depression, so the real choice is the business model: buying, leasing, a monthly subscription or a per-session fee. A per-session model costs less at low volume and more at high volume, while a purchase or lease pays back faster once you treat a few courses a month. Run the payback math on your own payer mix before you sign.

Four ways to pay for a TMS device

The same machine can cost very different amounts over five years depending on how you pay. BrainsWay's own buyer guide lays out the four models.

  1. Purchase. Highest upfront cost, you own the system and you can depreciate it.
  2. Financing. Lower upfront cost, monthly payments with interest, and you own it at the end.
  3. Lease or subscription. Lower upfront cost and recurring payments, the vendor owns the system, and service is often included.
  4. Pay per session. Little or no upfront cost, and you pay the vendor for each treatment. BrainsWay warns this can cost more as volume grows, and that some payer contracts may not cover the fee.

For the lender side of a purchase or lease, and how Section 179 and bonus depreciation work, see our guide to medical equipment financing. This guide does not repeat that material.

The TMS systems

NeuroStar (Neuronetics)

NeuroStar was the first FDA cleared TMS system, in 2008. Neuronetics' annual report for 2025, filed March 2026, lists clearances for adult major depressive disorder after failed medication, adjunct treatment for adult OCD, adolescents aged 15 to 21 with depression, and anxiety symptoms in adults with depression.

Neuronetics says most of its revenue comes from clinics and recurring treatment sessions, and that it has sold mainly through a treatment session model. The same report says it is piloting a capital-only option and new lease options, directly and through financing partners. It does not publish prices. Device Pulse, an independent comparison site, estimated $80,000 to $150,000 for a new system in September 2026.

Best for: groups that want the largest installed base and practice support, and expect steady volume.

BrainsWay Deep TMS

BrainsWay uses an H-coil helmet. Its clearances include adult depression, adjunct OCD, smoking cessation, an accelerated protocol and, from November 2025, adolescents aged 15 to 21 as an adjunct. Its 2026 pricing guide puts a BrainsWay system at $100,000 to $150,000 and says it offers purchase, financing and leasing, with no pay-per-use model and no per-treatment fees beyond consumables. Its leases include technical service and maintenance for the term.

Best for: groups that want several indications on one device and a fixed cost per month or a one-time purchase.

MagVenture

MagVenture received the first U.S. clearance for theta burst (iTBS) treatment in 2018, which cut stimulation time to about three minutes. On May 26, 2026 it announced FDA clearance for accelerated TMS protocols. Pricing is not published.

Best for: high-volume clinics that want short sessions and accelerated schedules.

Magstim Horizon

Magstim's Horizon was cleared for adult depression in 2017, a navigated version in 2019, and adjunct OCD and adolescents in 2025, according to a device review by Dr. Beata Lewis updated October 2026. Pricing is not published.

Best for: groups that want built-in navigation from a long-running maker.

Ampa One

Ampa sells Ampa One only by subscription, at $3,000 a month with shipping, training, repairs, warranty and parts included, per its own comparison page dated July 27, 2026. It cannot be bought outright. It uses three-minute iTBS sessions with camera-based navigation.

Best for: practices that want a fixed monthly cost and no large purchase.

CloudTMS (CloudNeuro)

CloudTMS says its machines are FDA cleared for depression and OCD and ship with a certified laptop running its software. Pricing and financing are by request. Its page puts insurance reimbursement at $150 a session from Medicare up to $600 from private insurers, which is the maker's own figure.

Best for: smaller practices comparing a lower profile device.

Apollo (Mag & More) and Nexstim

Apollo, from Mag & More in Munich, holds FDA 510(k) clearances listed as K243539 (August 2025) and K260560 (May 2026). Nexstim, a Finnish company, sells navigated TMS that uses 3D imaging to target treatment for depression. Neither publishes U.S. pricing, so see the vendor's site.

Best for: groups that want navigation or a European device and will negotiate directly.

Reimbursement, in general terms

TMS bills on three CPT codes: 90867 for the first session with motor threshold and mapping, 90868 for each later session, and 90869 when the motor threshold is redone. Medicare coverage follows local coverage determinations. Novitas (L34998) and Noridian (L37086), for example, set different rules on how many medication trials a patient must have failed and how long treatment runs.

Commercial payers usually require prior authorization and cap the course. One billing guide updated October 2026 lists 30 sessions plus 6 taper sessions for Aetna and Optum. It also reports that Evernorth removed prior authorization for contracted providers on March 6, 2026. Everbright Health, which runs TMS programs, reports first-pass authorization approval below 50% in a typical practice and more than four hours of staff work per submission.

What this means for cash: you treat for six to nine weeks, and payment arrives after claims are filed, so the first courses are paid for out of the bank before any collections land. Neuronetics estimates about $9,000 of average provider revenue per commercially insured patient for a standard course. That is a maker's estimate, and your contracted rates decide your real number. Your biller and billing company handle the claims. See our guide to mental health billing services.

Payback math

Illustrative example only. These are round assumptions, not quotes from any vendor, payer or practice. Replace each one with your own numbers.

Assumptions: net collections of $6,000 per completed course, $200 of consumables per course, a technician at $4,500 a month, space and other costs at $1,500 a month.

ModelMonthly fixed costCourses a month to break even
Purchase, financed at about $2,400 a month plus $700 service$9,100About 1.6
Subscription at $3,000 a month, service included$9,000About 1.6
Per-session model, $80 a session, $800 a month for the device$6,800About 2.3

How the numbers work. In the purchase and subscription rows, each course leaves $5,800 after consumables. In the per-session row, the vendor fee takes $2,880 of each 36-session course, so each course leaves $2,920.

At 6 courses a month the gap is large. The purchase leaves about $25,700 a month before tax and the subscription about $25,800, while the per-session model leaves about $10,700. At 1 or 2 courses a month the per-session model risks less cash.

Paying cash for a $120,000 device works the same way. At 3 courses a month, the purchase row without the loan payment leaves about $10,700 a month, so the device pays back in roughly 11 to 12 months. Add two to three months if authorizations and claims run slow.

Tax changes the result. A purchase can be deducted faster under Section 179 or bonus depreciation, while lease and subscription payments are usually deducted as you pay them. Your CPA should run this against your year-end income.

How to choose

  1. Starting TMS with uncertain demand. A per-session or subscription model limits the cash at risk. Price what happens if volume doubles.
  2. Steady referrals of 4 or more courses a month. A purchase or lease usually pays back faster. Compare total cost over five years.
  3. Several clinics. Count devices per location, technician coverage and whether service covers every site.
  4. Accelerated or theta burst schedules. Short sessions raise chair capacity. Check which systems and protocols your payers cover.
  5. OCD, smoking cessation or adolescent patients. Match the device label to the patients you plan to treat, since coverage generally follows the label.
  6. Any model. Ask every vendor for the total cost over five years: device, coils, consumables, service, software, training and any per-session fee.
Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What is the best TMS machine for a psychiatry practice?

No trial has shown one maker's machine outperforms another for depression. NeuroStar and BrainsWay are the most common choices, and MagVenture, Magstim, Ampa, CloudTMS, Apollo and Nexstim are also FDA cleared. Choose on cleared indications, session length, service and the payment model that fits your volume.

How much does a TMS machine cost?

BrainsWay publishes $100,000 to $150,000 for its systems. Ampa One is $3,000 a month by subscription. Most other makers quote privately, and an independent site estimated $80,000 to $150,000 for a new NeuroStar in 2026.

Is it better to buy, lease or pay per session?

Per session costs less when volume is low or uncertain. Buying or leasing usually costs less once you treat a few courses a month. Compare the total cost over five years at your expected volume.

How many TMS patients do I need to break even?

In our illustrative example, about 1.6 completed courses a month on a purchase or subscription, and about 2.3 on a per-session model. Your rates, staffing and rent change the answer. Run your own numbers before signing.

Does insurance cover TMS?

Medicare and most commercial payers cover TMS for treatment-resistant depression when their criteria are met. Most commercial plans require prior authorization and limit the number of sessions. Coverage generally follows the device's cleared use.

How long until a TMS device pays for itself?

In our example, a $120,000 device paid in cash pays back in roughly a year at 3 courses a month. Slow authorizations and claims add months. Plan cash for the gap.

Can I deduct a TMS machine under Section 179?

Generally yes, if you own it, or treat it as owned under a loan or $1 buyout lease, and place it in service that year. Lease and subscription payments are usually deducted as paid. Ask your CPA, and see our guide to tax deductions for medical practice owners.
