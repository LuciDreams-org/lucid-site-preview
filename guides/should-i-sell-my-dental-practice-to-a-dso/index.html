Should I sell my dental practice to a DSO?

Home / Guides / Should I sell my dental practice to a DSO?

Short answer

Sell to a DSO if you want a larger headline price and are willing to keep working for three to five years with part of the price paid in equity and earnouts. Sell to an associate or an outside dentist if you want mostly cash at closing and a short exit. A phased associate buy-in or simply staying independent can beat both if you are more than five years from retirement. The deciding numbers are your normalized EBITDA, the cash you receive at closing and the terms you sign, not the headline multiple.

How DSO deals work

A DSO, or dental support organization, buys the business side of your practice and supports it with billing, HR, purchasing and other services. In most states the clinical side stays with a dentist owned entity, and the DSO is paid through a services agreement.

DSOs price practices on adjusted EBITDA. That is profit after paying every dentist, including you, a market clinical wage. A private buyer usually prices on collections or on seller's discretionary earnings, which includes your own pay. Dental Economics noted in November 2024 that DSO valuations can be far higher than dentist to dentist valuations on paper. The gap shrinks once you account for the pay you give up.

DSOs are a growing share of the market. The ADA Health Policy Institute reported in November 2026 that about 1 in 6 dentists were affiliated with a DSO as of 2025, up about one percentage point a year since it began tracking.

Three points stand out. Multiples are below their 2021 peak. A larger, less owner dependent practice earns a higher multiple. Buyers are more selective: Becker's reported in September 2026 that DSOs are looking harder at financial performance, projections, provider risk and reimbursement exposure.

McLerran and Associates lists what tends to move a practice up its range: owner production under about 70 percent of the total, hygiene above about 30 percent of collections, a mostly fee for service payer mix, and a manager who can run the office without you.

Deal structures: cash, rollover equity and earnouts

A DSO offer is rarely all cash. McLerran and Associates describes a common 2026 structure as 60 to 85 percent cash at closing, 10 to 30 percent rollover equity and an earnout of one to three years.

Cash at closing. The most certain dollar in the deal. Compare offers on this number first.

Rollover equity. Part of your price is paid as ownership in the DSO. It can grow if the DSO is later sold or recapitalized at a higher value, which sellers call the second bite. It is illiquid, you cannot sell it yourself, and it usually sits behind the investor's preferred equity.

Dental Economics (November 2024) describes three common forms:

  1. Practice level partnership. The DSO buys 51 to 80 percent of your practice and you keep 20 to 49 percent. Profit is shared after a management fee, typically 5 to 9 percent of revenue.
  2. Holding company equity. The DSO buys 100 percent for 65 to 90 percent cash and 10 to 35 percent stock in the parent company. This stock usually pays no distributions.
  3. Hybrid. About 60 to 70 percent cash, with the equity split between practice level and holding company stock.

Earnouts and holdbacks. Part of the price is held back and paid later if you stay through your employment term and the practice holds its revenue or EBITDA. McLerran and Associates notes that after closing the buyer controls expenses, staffing and fees, so ask how targets are measured and whether a near miss still pays part.

Staying on as an associate

Most DSO deals include an employment agreement. McLerran and Associates reported in October 2026 that a minimum five year working term is typical and more DSOs now require it. Dental Economics cites clinical pay of about 30 percent of collections for general dentists and 32 to 40 percent for specialists.

Your take home pay changes. Before the sale you earn clinical pay plus practice profit. After it you earn clinical pay, and the profit belongs to the DSO, which paid you for it up front. Model both sides before you compare a DSO offer with a private sale.

Read the noncompete closely. The ADA's guide to business services agreements with DSOs notes that a restrictive covenant can extend to other offices the DSO supports. Ask for that list before you sign.

Alternatives to a DSO

Sell to your associate. Your associate already knows the patients and team, so the transition is easier. Price is usually set on collections or SDE, and the buyer funds it with a bank or SBA loan. Precision Dental Analytics (July 2026) cites a common range of 60 to 100 percent of trailing 12 month collections for solo practices. See our guide to dental practice loans for how buyers fund it.

Sell to an outside dentist. McLerran and Associates (October 2026) reports these sales usually pay 80 to 100 percent at closing with a work back of about four to eight weeks.

Phased buy-in. An associate buys in over three to seven years. You get paid in steps, often 40 to 60 percent at the first step, and step back slowly.

Stay independent. You keep all profit and control. This is often the right call when you are many years from retirement, because you keep growing the EBITDA a future buyer will pay for.

Questions to ask a DSO before you sign

  1. What is the cash at closing in dollars, after the holdback?
  2. What is the equity, practice level or holding company, and how was it valued?
  3. Is my equity behind preferred equity, and is there any way to sell it if no recapitalization happens?
  4. How is the earnout measured, who controls the costs behind it, and does a near miss pay part?
  5. How long is my employment term, what is my pay formula, and what happens if I leave early?
  6. How far and how long does the noncompete reach, and which offices does it cover?
  7. Who decides treatment, schedules, fees and staffing after closing?
  8. Can I speak with dentists who sold to you two or more years ago?
  9. How is the price split between goodwill, equipment and the noncompete? That allocation affects your tax.
  10. What happens to my team, my lease and my name on the door?

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

What multiple will a DSO pay for my practice?

Published 2026 ranges run about 5x to 9x EBITDA for single practices and higher for large groups, per McLerran and Associates (August 2026) and Becker's (September 2026). Your multiple depends on size, owner dependence, hygiene and payer mix. It applies to adjusted EBITDA after a market wage for you.

Is a DSO offer better than selling to another dentist?

The headline is usually higher, but part is equity and earnout, and you keep working for years at clinical pay. A private sale is mostly cash and quicker. Compare after tax cash over five years on both paths.

What is rollover equity?

It is part of your price paid as ownership in the DSO or your practice. It can grow if the DSO is sold later at a higher value. It is illiquid and usually ranks behind investor preferred equity.

How long do I have to keep working?

Most DSO deals include an employment term. McLerran and Associates reports a minimum of five years is typical in 2026. Earnouts are often tied to staying through it.

What do DSOs check in diligence?

They check financial performance, projections, provider risk and payer exposure, per Becker's in September 2026. Many use a quality of earnings review. See our guide to quality of earnings reports.

How do I know my normalized EBITDA?

Start with accrual books, then add back owner pay above a market clinical wage, personal expenses and one time costs, each with documents. See how a practice is valued.

When should I start preparing?

Two to five years ahead is common advice, since clean books, a strong associate and a lower owner share of production all lift value. Our guide on preparing a practice for sale has a timeline.
