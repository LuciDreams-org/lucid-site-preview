What insurance does a medical practice need?

Short answer

Beyond malpractice, most practices with staff need general liability, property and business interruption (often bundled as a business owner's policy, or BOP), workers compensation, cyber, employment practices liability (EPLI) and an umbrella. Owners should also look at business overhead expense and disability buyout coverage, which keep the practice running if a key clinician cannot work. Travelers, The Hartford, CNA and Chubb write practice packages, Coalition and At-Bay focus on cyber, and Guardian and AMA Insurance sell overhead expense coverage. A broker who works with healthcare can quote several of them at once.

General liability

General liability covers third-party claims of bodily injury and property damage that are not about the care itself. The classic example is a patient who slips in the waiting room. Malpractice covers the clinical side; general liability covers the premises and the business. Landlords usually require it in the lease and ask to be named as an additional insured.

Insureon's median for healthcare customers is $32 a month, or $381 a year. It says profession matters most: optometrists pay a median of $42 a month and home healthcare providers $17.

Property and business interruption

Property coverage pays to repair or replace your office space, medical equipment, furniture and supplies after a covered loss such as fire, theft or vandalism, per Travelers. If you lease, you insure your tenant improvements and contents; the landlord insures the building.

Business interruption (business income) replaces lost income and pays ongoing costs while a covered loss closes the office. For a practice, this is often the policy that matters most after a fire or flood, because rent and payroll keep running while visits stop.

Add-ons worth asking about:

  1. Equipment breakdown. Travelers lists repair or replacement of X-ray and imaging systems after mechanical or electrical failure.
  2. Spoilage. Travelers gives the example of medical supplies lost in a power outage, under its Medical Dental Premier package.
  3. Flood and earthquake. Usually excluded from standard property forms and bought separately.

BOP vs separate policies

A business owner's policy bundles general liability, property and business income coverage into one package, as Travelers describes it. It is usually cheaper and simpler than buying the three separately.

A BOP does not include malpractice, workers comp, cyber, EPLI or commercial auto. Those are separate policies or endorsements.

When separate policies make sense:

  1. You outgrow BOP eligibility. Carriers set limits on size, revenue and type of operation. Larger groups and surgery centers often move to a commercial package policy.
  2. You need higher limits than the BOP allows, for example on expensive imaging or laser equipment.
  3. You have several locations with different property values or landlords.

Insureon's BOP median for healthcare customers is $91 a month, or $1,094 a year, on a sample policy with a $1,000 deductible. For healthcare facilities, an older Insureon page (July 2025) shows a median of $101 a month.

Workers compensation

Workers comp pays medical care and lost wages for staff injured on the job, such as a needlestick, a back injury lifting a patient or a fall. Most states require it once you have employees, and rules vary by state. Texas is the main exception: the Texas Department of Insurance says Texas does not require most private employers to carry it.

Premiums are based mainly on payroll and job class, so they grow with headcount. Insureon's median for healthcare customers is $63 a month. Practices that use a professional employer organization (PEO) often get workers comp through the PEO. See our guide to HR software and PEOs for medical practices.

Cyber

Cyber insurance pays for a data breach or ransomware attack: forensics, patient notification, legal costs, lost income while systems are down, and in some policies regulatory penalties. Travelers ties its cyber cover to telehealth and electronic health records, including HIPAA-related penalties.

Why it matters: IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach Report found healthcare had the highest average breach cost of any industry, at $7.42 million. Small practices face smaller numbers, but notification and downtime alone can be large.

Insureon's median for healthcare customers is $79 a month, or $954 a year. Carriers now ask about multifactor authentication, backups and email security on the application, and answers affect price and eligibility.

EPLI

Employment practices liability covers claims from staff about discrimination, harassment, wrongful termination and similar issues. Travelers notes sublimits for immigration and expense coverage. Practices with front desk, clinical and billing teams face this risk at every hire and exit. EPLI can be added to some BOPs or bought alone. No public median was found, so get it quoted.

Umbrella

An umbrella adds limits above your general liability, auto and employer liability policies when a covered claim exceeds them, per Travelers. It usually does not sit over malpractice, which has its own excess options. Umbrellas are often sold in $1 million steps.

Coverage for the owners

These protect the practice when an owner or key clinician cannot work.

  1. Business overhead expense (BOE). Pays office costs such as staff salaries, rent, utilities and premiums while an owner is disabled, per the AMA. AMA Insurance offers up to $20,000 a month, with waiting periods of 2, 3, 6 or 12 months. The AMA notes BOE premiums are tax deductible as a business expense.
  2. Disability buyout. Funds the purchase of a disabled partner's share under a buy-sell agreement. The AMA says premiums are generally not deductible and benefits are generally not taxed.
  3. Key person life or disability. Pays the practice if a high-producing clinician dies or is disabled, to cover lost revenue and recruiting.
  4. Personal disability. Replaces the owner's own income. Guardian and AMA Insurance both sell it to practice owners.

Buy-sell funding should match the valuation formula in your partnership paperwork. See practice partner buy-in loans.

Where to buy: the main options

Travelers

Travelers sells a BOP for medical offices plus cyber, EPLI, equipment breakdown, commercial auto and umbrella, and lists provider types from solo practices to multi-practitioner clinics, including med spas and mental health practices. Availability depends on underwriting and state rules.

The Hartford

The Hartford has a medical office program covering a range of providers, including dentists, counselors, chiropractors and optometrists. Best for: small practices that want to bundle a BOP with workers comp from one carrier.

CNA

CNA writes professional liability and business coverage for allied healthcare professionals, small businesses and practices, plus larger healthcare organizations. Best for: practices with many allied staff.

Chubb

Chubb writes coverage for hospitals, health systems and healthcare providers. Best for: larger groups that want property, liability and cyber from one global carrier.

Coalition and At-Bay

Both sell cyber insurance through brokers and pair it with security tools. Coalition describes its Active Insurance as helping to cover, prevent and respond to cyber risk, with a policyholder risk platform and an incident response affiliate. At-Bay pairs coverage with security to help prevent incidents. Best for: practices that want a cyber specialist instead of a cyber endorsement on a BOP.

Guardian and AMA Insurance

Both sell disability and overhead expense coverage for practice owners. AMA Insurance publishes its benefit limits and waiting periods; Guardian lists practice owner disability protection. Best for: owners pricing BOE and disability together.

Online marketplaces and healthcare brokers

Insureon is a marketplace that quotes several small business carriers online and publishes the medians above. A healthcare-focused broker, such as CPK Insurance, can place malpractice and business lines together and coordinate renewals. Best for: groups with several locations or complex needs.

How to buy

  1. List what you own and lease: equipment values, tenant improvements, vehicles and each location.
  2. Gather payroll by role, headcount, revenue and past claims (loss runs).
  3. Read your lease and payer and hospital paperwork for required limits and additional insured wording.
  4. Decide BOP or package with your broker, and ask what each quote excludes.
  5. Line up renewal dates so all policies renew together, ideally away from your malpractice renewal.
  6. Review every year, and after any new location, big equipment purchase or hiring wave.

How to choose by practice type

  1. Small single-location practice. A BOP plus workers comp and cyber covers most risk. Add EPLI once you have a real team.
  2. Group with several locations. Schedule each location's property and income, check BOP size limits, and add an umbrella. See our medical group page.
  3. Dental. High equipment values make equipment breakdown and business income key. See our dental page.
  4. Med spa. Lasers and devices raise property values, and product liability can apply. See our med spa page.
  5. Physical therapy. Lifting injuries make workers comp a large line. See our physical therapy page.
  6. Behavioral health and telehealth. Cyber matters most, because records are sensitive and visits run on software. See our behavioral health page.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is a BOP enough for a medical office?

No. A BOP covers general liability, property and business income, but not malpractice, workers comp, cyber, EPLI or auto. Most practices need a BOP plus those separate policies.

How much does business insurance cost for a medical practice?

Insureon's healthcare medians are $91 a month for a BOP, $63 for workers comp and $79 for cyber, mostly for businesses under five employees. Groups with more staff, equipment and locations pay more.

Do I need workers comp for my practice?

In most states, yes, once you have employees. Texas does not require most private employers to carry it, but going without it has other legal effects. Check your state's rules.

Does general liability cover malpractice?

No. General liability covers non-clinical injuries and property damage. Clinical errors are covered by malpractice, a separate policy with its own limits and tail rules.

Do small practices need cyber insurance?

Most do. Healthcare data is a common target, and a breach brings notification, legal and downtime costs even at a small office. Many carriers now require security controls such as multifactor authentication to quote.

What is business overhead expense insurance?

It pays the practice's fixed costs, such as rent and staff pay, while an owner is disabled. It is sized to monthly overhead, not to the owner's income.

Can I get all my coverage from one company?

Some carriers write most lines, but malpractice is often placed with a specialty carrier. A healthcare broker can coordinate everything on one renewal calendar.

When should I review my coverage?

Every year at renewal, and whenever you add a location, buy major equipment, hire a wave of staff or take on a partner.
