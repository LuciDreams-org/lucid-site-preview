Short answer

Most ABA and behavioral health groups fund growth with an SBA 7(a) loan or a bank term loan for big moves (a new center, an acquisition, a partner buyout) and a line of credit for the gap between payroll and payer deposits. Live Oak Bank has a team that lends to ABA, mental health and substance use businesses, and your own bank or a regional bank is the next call. When payers pay slowly, receivables financing from firms such as Thrivory (claims-based advances) or Alleon Healthcare Capital (AR lines) can fill the gap, but Medicaid rules limit how those deals work. Lenders will ask for clean books and an AR aging report before any of it.

Why ABA and behavioral health borrowing is different

Three things set these practices apart from a typical medical office, and lenders know them.

  1. Payroll comes first, collections come later. ABA groups pay RBTs and BCBAs every pay period, while insurance and Medicaid deposits arrive weeks later. A growing group can be profitable on paper and still short on cash, because every new hire adds payroll before the first claim is paid.
  2. Revenue depends on payer rules. Many payers require prior authorization for ABA services, and new clinicians must be credentialed before they can bill. Delays in either push revenue out, and a lender will ask how you handle them.
  3. Rates can change by notice. State Medicaid programs set many ABA and behavioral health rates. Indiana, for example, cut its maximum fees for individual ABA services by 6% on April 1, 2026, with another 4% scheduled for April 1, 2027, per Indiana Health Coverage Programs bulletin BT202627. A lender will look at how much of your revenue comes from one state program.

Your service model also changes the math. Center-based and in-home ABA carry different costs for space and travel. See our guide to center-based vs in-home ABA margins.

Medicaid-heavy receivables: what to know before you borrow

If Medicaid or managed Medicaid is a large share of your revenue, read this before you sign with any funder.

  1. Medicaid limits who can be paid. Federal rules bar Medicaid from paying a provider's claims to a factor, with narrow exceptions (42 CFR 447.10). Medicare has a parallel rule. Funders usually work around it with a collections account in the practice's own name that the funder then sweeps. Our medical receivables financing guide explains the structures and the rules in full.
  2. Old claims may not count. AR lines lend against eligible receivables, usually those under a set age. Slow or denied Medicaid claims can shrink your borrowing base just when you need it.
  3. Payer concentration matters. If one state plan or one managed care plan pays most of your claims, expect a lender to ask about it, and possibly to lend less against it.
  4. Clean AR is the price of entry. Every option here starts from an AR aging report by payer. If your aging is messy, fix the books before you apply.

The options

SBA 7(a) loans

The SBA 7(a) program is the main government-backed loan for practices. The SBA sets a maximum of $5 million, and terms usually run up to 10 years for working capital, acquisitions and buyouts, and up to 25 years for real estate. Rates are negotiated with the lender but capped by SBA. The full rules, fees and the lenders that made the most healthcare practice loans in fiscal 2026 are in our SBA lenders guide.

Best for: a new center, buying a practice, or buying out a partner. Limits: SBA paperwork is heavy, and you will usually sign a personal guarantee.

Live Oak Bank

Live Oak has a behavioral health lending page that names ABA, intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD), mental health and substance use disorder businesses. It lists acquisition, commercial real estate, construction, expansion and partner buyout financing. Live Oak is also the largest SBA 7(a) lender to healthcare practices by dollars in fiscal 2026, per SBA data, and its SBA page lists 7(a) loans up to $5 million. More on Live Oak is in our medical practice loans guide.

Best for: ABA and behavioral health groups planning an acquisition or a new location. Limits: Live Oak leans toward larger loans. Small working capital needs may fit a line better.

Your bank or a regional bank

The bank that holds your operating account already sees your deposits, which makes it the easiest first call for a term loan or a line. Some regional banks publish behavioral health pages too. Firstrust Bank, for example, has a behavioral health practice page that covers cash flow management and financing, with business term loans and lines of credit.

Best for: owners with a few years of deposits at one bank. Limits: terms vary by bank and are rarely published. Get quotes from two or three.

A bank line of credit

A line is the right tool for the gap between payroll and payer deposits. Chase, for example, publishes lines from $10,000 to $500,000, with a 5 year draw period and then 5 years to repay. Our line of credit guide compares bank and online lines, covenants and renewal.

Best for: groups with steady collections and a timing gap. Limits: banks review the line every year, and a bad year can shrink it.

Bluevine

Bluevine is a financial technology company, not a bank. Its line of credit is issued by Celtic Bank and goes up to $250,000. It is faster than a bank line, but online lines usually repay over weeks or months, so they suit short gaps, not long ones.

Best for: smaller groups that need a modest line quickly. Limits: higher cost per dollar drawn than a bank line, and short repayment.

Thrivory (claims-based advance)

Thrivory says it typically advances 80% of the expected value of eligible claims, in your account the day you submit, and names behavioral health groups and therapy and rehab among the practices it serves. It lists Medicaid and managed Medicaid among the payers it funds. Setup means opening a collections account in your name and switching payer deposits to it, which Thrivory says typically takes 3 to 4 weeks. Fees and the full structure are covered in our receivables financing guide.

Best for: fast-growing groups with clean claims and slow payers. Limits: it costs more than a bank line, and it changes where your deposits land.

Alleon Healthcare Capital (AR line)

Alleon says it funds any medical provider in the U.S. that bills third-party insurance carriers, from behavioral health to home health, and it has published financing facilities for ABA and behavioral health providers. Its AR line advances up to 85% of eligible receivables, from $100,000 to $10 million per provider.

Best for: larger groups with steady insurance AR that a bank will not lend against. Limits: the line moves with your receivables, so aging and denials affect how much you can draw.

How to choose

  1. Opening your first clinic. Start with an SBA lender or your bank, and see our medical practice startup loans guide for what a lender wants from a new practice.
  2. Adding a center or buying a group. An SBA 7(a) or bank term loan. Get quotes from Live Oak and one regional lender. See our practice acquisition loans guide.
  3. Buying out a partner. See our partner buy-in loans guide.
  4. Payroll ahead of collections. A bank line sized to your gap. Bluevine if you need it fast and the gap is small.
  5. Payers paying slowly and growth outrunning the bank. Thrivory or Alleon, after you read the Medicaid rules above.
  6. Mostly Medicaid revenue. Talk to a healthcare attorney before signing any receivables deal, and expect a lender to look closely at state rate risk.

Fixing collections often beats borrowing. If claims are slow because of billing errors, compare ABA billing companies or mental health billing services first.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Can I get an SBA loan for an ABA clinic?

Yes. SBA 7(a) loans can fund a new center, an acquisition, a partner buyout or working capital, up to $5 million. Live Oak Bank names ABA among the behavioral health businesses it lends to. Expect to show financial statements, tax returns and a projection.

How do ABA owners cover payroll while payers pay slowly?

Most use a bank line of credit sized to the gap between payroll and payer deposits. Groups that outgrow the line sometimes add a claims-based advance or an AR line. Size the need with a cash forecast first, so you do not borrow more than you need.

Can I borrow against Medicaid receivables?

Sometimes, but with limits. Federal rules bar Medicaid from paying claims to a factor (42 CFR 447.10), so funders use a collections account in your name instead. Have a healthcare attorney read the paperwork.

How much should a behavioral health group borrow?

Enough to cover the gap between payroll and collections at your planned size, plus a cushion. A forecast that shows payer lag by month is the best way to size it. Borrowing far more than the gap adds cost without adding safety.

What do lenders look at for a behavioral health loan?

Clean monthly financial statements, tax returns, an AR aging report by payer, payer mix and debt service coverage. See what lenders look for in a practice loan.

Is a claims-based advance a loan?

Often it is structured as a purchase of receivables, not a loan, so there may be no interest, only fees. The cost can still be higher than a bank line. The accounting differs too, so ask your CPA how to record it.

Should a therapy group take a merchant cash advance?

Treat it as a last resort. Daily or weekly repayments come out of deposits that may already be slow. Compare a bank line, Bluevine or an AR line first.
