Should my practice offer a membership plan?

Updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

A membership plan is worth it when the fee covers the cost of the care members actually use, enough patients join and stay, and your books record the fees correctly. For a dental office, an in house plan gives uninsured patients a yearly price for preventive care and is a common bridge when you drop a PPO. For a med spa, a monthly membership turns one time visits into recurring revenue, but unused credits and discounts can quietly eat the margin. Check your state's rules before you launch, and treat prepaid fees as deferred revenue, not profit.

Why owners are asking

Dental offices look at memberships when they leave or cut back on PPOs and want to keep the patients who lose coverage. The ADA describes these plans simply: the patient pays the practice a fixed amount monthly or yearly, generally gets preventive services at no charge, and gets a discount on other procedures. It also notes research showing that patients with a benefit plan are more likely to seek care and accept treatment plans.

Med spas use memberships to smooth revenue between seasons and to bring clients back every month for injectables, facials or laser maintenance. The appeal is predictable cash. The risk is that the plan is priced on hope rather than on what members actually use.

If you are weighing a membership as part of leaving insurance, our guide to dropping dental PPOs runs those numbers first.

The economics, dental

A dental plan pays off when the yearly fee, plus the extra treatment members accept, beats what you would collect from the same patients without the plan.

Illustrative example: an adult plan at $35 a month, or $420 a year, includes two cleanings, two exams and routine x rays, plus 15 percent off other work.

  1. Cost of the included care. Use your real cost of hygiene time, supplies and x rays, not your full fee. If those visits cost you $220 a year to deliver, the plan leaves $200 to cover overhead and profit before any discounts.
  2. The discount. If an average member accepts $600 of other treatment a year, 15 percent off is $90 of revenue given up.
  3. The upside. The plan wins if members accept treatment they would otherwise delay, and if they would not have come in at all without it.
  4. Platform and card fees. Most plans run on a membership platform that charges a monthly fee, a per member fee, or both.

At these numbers, each member brings about $110 a year in margin on the plan itself, plus the margin on any added treatment. A child plan or a perio plan needs its own math, because the included care costs more.

The economics, med spa

Prospyr's membership guide gives a clear way to run the numbers, using an illustrative $149 monthly fee: subtract the service cost times the share of members who redeem each month, payment fees and other variable costs. In its example, $149 minus $45 of service cost at 0.8 redemptions, $4.50 of payment fees and $8 of other costs leaves $100.50 per member. At 1.2 redemptions, the contribution drops to $82.50.

Three things move that number in a med spa:

  1. Redemption rate. The more members use their credit, the higher your product and injector cost.
  2. Member discounts. A members only price on neurotoxin or filler lowers the margin on every extra unit they buy.
  3. Rollover credits. If unused credits carry forward, you owe that care later. The cash is in the bank, but the work is not done.

Run the math in reverse to set the fee: start from the contribution you need per member, add expected service cost and fees, then check it still holds if members use more than you planned.

Pricing a plan

  1. Price from cost, not from competitors. Start with the real cost of what the plan includes at expected use.
  2. Keep tiers few. Two or three tiers are easy for the front desk to explain and easy for you to track.
  3. Offer yearly and monthly. Yearly fees bring cash early; monthly fees are easier to sell and spread revenue evenly.
  4. Set a cap on discounts. A discount on other work should still leave margin after injector pay, lab or product cost.
  5. Review prices every year. Your costs rise; plan prices should follow.

Churn and retention

Members who cancel after a few months are where most plans lose money, because the first months carry the setup and marketing cost. Track monthly:

  1. Active members and new sign ups by plan.
  2. Cancellations and the reason given.
  3. Redemption or visit rate per member.
  4. Revenue per member, including treatment outside the plan.
  5. Upgrades and downgrades between tiers.

Phorest's membership guide recommends a quick review every few months and a deeper one every 6 to 12 months, adjusting perks or adding a freeze option when cancellations rise. Clear cancellation terms also help. Members who know how to pause or cancel are less likely to dispute a charge.

Legal and insurance points

These points are general. Rules differ by state, so check yours with a healthcare attorney before you launch.

  1. A plan is not insurance, but some states regulate it. Some states treat certain prepaid or discount arrangements as insurance or as regulated discount plans. The ADA's in office plan toolkit includes a list of regulations by state. The Washington State Dental Association worked with its state insurance regulator to describe two compliant models: a direct care agreement for preventive services, and a simple discount or voucher.
  2. Patients with government coverage. Federal rules limit discounts and free services offered to patients covered by Medicare, Medicaid and other federal programs. Many practices limit plans to patients without coverage. Ask your attorney how this applies to you.
  3. Insurance and the plan do not mix. Most plans are for patients without dental insurance. Do not bill an insurer for care the member already paid for in the plan.
  4. Automatic renewal laws. Many states set rules on how a recurring charge is disclosed, agreed to and canceled. California's automatic renewal law is one example, and state legislative research notes both state and federal rules apply to online sign ups.
  5. Med spa medical rules still apply. A membership does not change who may perform a treatment or the exam and oversight your state requires before it.
  6. Write the terms down. Plan terms should list what is included, the price, how and when it renews, how to cancel, and the refund policy.

How membership revenue is recognized

This is where many practices get the books wrong. Lucid treats memberships as deferred revenue, and the rules below are the basics.

  1. Monthly fees are revenue in the month they cover.
  2. Yearly fees paid up front are a liability on the day they arrive, called deferred revenue, then moved to revenue each month across the plan year.
  3. Banked credits and prepaid packages stay in deferred revenue until the treatment is delivered. A credit used in March is March revenue, even if it was paid for in January.
  4. Credits that are never used are called breakage. Under the revenue standard, a business that expects some credits to go unused can recognize that amount in proportion to the credits members do use; otherwise it waits until the chance of use is remote. State unclaimed property rules may apply to some unused balances, so ask your CPA.
  5. Discounts are not an expense. Record the treatment at the member price you actually charge.
  6. Platform payouts arrive net of fees. Record the gross fee as revenue and the platform and card fees as an expense, so payouts tie to the bank.
  7. Refunds and cancellations reduce the deferred balance, not current month revenue.

Tax timing can differ from your books. A cash basis practice generally reports fees when received. An accrual basis practice may be able to defer part of an advance payment to the next tax year under IRS rules. Our cash vs accrual guide explains the difference, so confirm the method with your CPA.

How to choose

  1. Dental office with many uninsured patients, or leaving a PPO: an in house plan is worth modeling first.
  2. Dental office mostly in network: a small plan for uninsured patients can still help, but keep it simple.
  3. Med spa with steady repeat clients: a monthly membership with a clear credit and a cap on rollover.
  4. Med spa with seasonal or one time clients: prepaid packages may fit better than a membership.
  5. Any practice that cannot track it yet: fix the books first. A plan you cannot measure is a plan you cannot price.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is a dental membership plan worth it?

It often is for practices with many uninsured patients or those leaving a PPO, if the fee covers the real cost of the included care. Run the math on cost per member, expected treatment and platform fees first. A plan priced below cost loses more as it grows.

How much should a med spa membership cost?

Set it from your own numbers: the contribution you need per member, plus service cost at expected use, plus payment fees. Prospyr's illustrative example uses $149 a month. Check that the plan still makes money when members use more than you planned.

Is an in house dental plan insurance?

It is a package of your own services for a fee, not an insurance policy. Some states regulate discount or prepaid plans, so check your state's rules with an attorney.

Can patients with Medicare or Medicaid join?

Federal rules limit discounts and free services for patients in federal programs, so many practices offer plans only to patients without coverage. Ask a healthcare attorney before you include them.

How do I book a yearly membership fee?

As deferred revenue when it arrives, then earned monthly over the plan year. Monthly fees are revenue in the month they cover.

What happens to credits members never use?

They stay a liability until used or until the practice can reasonably expect they never will be, under breakage rules. State unclaimed property rules may also apply. Your CPA sets the policy.

How many members do I need?

Divide your monthly fixed plan costs, such as the platform fee and staff time, by the margin per member. At $150 a month in fixed costs and $20 margin per member, eight members cover the plan.
