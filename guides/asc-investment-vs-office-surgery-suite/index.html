Should I invest in an ASC or build an office surgery suite?

Home / Guides / Should I invest in an ASC or build an office surgery suite?

Short answer

You have four paths: buy into an existing ambulatory surgery center (ASC), build a new ASC with a management partner such as USPI, SCA Health, Regent Surgical Health, Surgery Partners, Compass Surgical Partners or Blue Chip Surgical Center Partners, build an accredited office surgery suite, or keep operating at the hospital outpatient department. A buy-in to a busy center is usually the lowest risk way to share in facility income, because the center already has cases, staff and payer contracts. A new ASC can earn the most but needs millions in capital, 12 to 24 months and steady volume. An office suite costs less and suits cash-pay or office-level procedures, but Medicare pays no separate ASC facility fee for work done there.

Path 1: buy into an existing ASC

The center already runs, so you can see its history before you pay. Ask for three years of financial statements, case volume by surgeon and payer mix, and the operating agreement.

What the trade press says. In Ophthalmology Management in 2020, one surgeon advised that a buy-in should be offered within about two years of operating at a center, since waiting means paying for value your own cases helped create. In a 2021 article in the same magazine, an AMSURG executive said partners who do their diligence can expect a full return on their investment in two to four years. That is one operator's view, not a promise.

What to check:

  1. The price and how it was set. A buy-in should be priced at fair market value, not on how many cases you will bring.
  2. Capital calls. Ask how often partners have been asked for more money and for what.
  3. Debt. Find out what loans the center carries and whether partners guarantee them.
  4. Exit terms. Know how your shares are bought back at retirement, relocation or a sale.
  5. Your vote. A small partner in a center run by a corporate partner or private equity firm may have little say over equipment, vendors and staffing.

Many lenders will lend against ASC shares. See our guide to practice partner buy-in loans.

Path 2: build a new ASC with a management partner

A new ASC can give surgeons the most ownership and the most upside, but it carries the most risk.

Capital. Sigmatic's October 2026 cost guide puts construction for a two operating room single-specialty center at $2 million to $3 million and larger multi-specialty centers at $4 million to $8 million, or $550 to $850 or more per square foot, before equipment, land and working capital. It estimates 12 to 24 months from feasibility to first case, longer in certificate of need states. A 2022 article in Cataract and Refractive Surgery Today noted that 35 states have certificate of need laws and that payer credentialing may not be done on opening day.

Volume. The same CRST Today article said about 1,000 cataract cases a year has traditionally been the rough threshold for some profit in an eye ASC. The two biggest risks named by an ASC consultant in Ophthalmology Management were overbuilding and unrealistic case projections.

Management partners. Most groups work with a developer or manager. Each company sets its own structure:

  1. Compass Surgical Partners writes that it typically buys 51% of an ASC's equity at fair market value, leaving the surgeons 49%.
  2. USPI describes two-way and three-way partnerships, the third partner being a health system.
  3. Regent Surgical Health says it has worked with hundreds of surgeons to buy, build, develop or manage centers.
  4. SCA Health, Surgery Partners and Blue Chip Surgical Center Partners also partner with surgeons. See each company's site for current terms.

Ask every partner for its management fee, who controls the board, how new partners are brought in later, and what happens to your shares if the partner sells. Several of these companies are also buyers of practices, covered in our guides on who is buying orthopedic practices and ophthalmology and optometry practices.

Path 3: build an accredited office surgery suite

An office surgery suite sits inside your practice. QUAD A describes office-based surgery as invasive outpatient surgery in a fully equipped operating room or dedicated suite, often under sedation. An ASC, by contrast, is a separate entity that operates only for surgery and has an agreement with CMS to take part in Medicare.

Cost and control. BuildYourOR, a New York design and build firm, wrote in October 2026 that office suites generally need less upfront investment, and that ASCs face more licensing and certification steps. You keep full control.

The trade-off is payment. Medicare pays an ASC a separate facility payment for nursing, recovery, drugs and supplies, and pays the surgeon's professional fee on top, according to MedPAC. Office procedures have no separate ASC facility payment. That is why office suites suit cash-pay cosmetic surgery and procedures payers cover in the office.

State rules vary. Many states require accreditation for office surgery above a set anesthesia level. Check your state medical board.

Path 4: stay at the hospital outpatient department

You put in no capital and take no facility risk. You also earn no facility income and have less control over block time, staff and supplies. For low volume or complex cases this can still be the right answer. It is also the baseline every other path should beat.

Accreditation

ASCs that want Medicare need certification under the Conditions for Coverage at 42 CFR Part 416, usually through an accreditor with CMS deeming authority. As of August 2026, five organizations held that authority for ASCs: AAAHC, The Joint Commission, ACHC, QUAD A (formerly AAAASF) and DNV. AAAHC and QUAD A also accredit office-based surgery. A CMS rule published June 16, 2026 limits consulting by accreditors before a survey, from June 2027.

Payer mix

Facility income depends on who pays. For anterior segment eye surgery, a consultant in Ophthalmology Management put Medicare at around 60% to 70% of volume, which makes Medicare ASC rates central to the math. Orthopedic centers lean more on commercial and workers compensation cases, and on whether total joints are on the Medicare ASC list. Before you invest, model the center's income under its real payer mix, not a best-case one.

Distributions and tax, in general terms

Most ASCs are LLCs or partnerships, so each owner gets a K-1 for their share of profit. You owe tax on your share whether or not the cash is paid out. Distributions are the cash the center actually pays partners, often monthly or quarterly. Part of each distribution may go to repaying a buy-in loan, and some banks want ASC loans repaid in three to four years, so early cash flow can be thin.

Three planning points:

  1. Hold back cash for tax on K-1 income, including state taxes where the center sits.
  2. Keep reserves for capital calls and equipment.
  3. Plan owner pay with ASC income kept apart from practice income, because buyers and lenders price them differently.

This is general information, not tax advice. Have your CPA run your numbers.

Compliance points

ASC investments sit inside federal fraud and abuse law. The federal anti-kickback statute has a safe harbor for ASC investments at 42 CFR 1001.952(r). In general terms it covers surgeon-owned, single-specialty, multi-specialty and hospital and surgeon centers, and its conditions include:

  1. Each investor earns at least one-third of their medical practice income from ASC-list procedures.
  2. In multi-specialty centers, each investor performs at least one-third of their procedures at the center.
  3. Investment terms are not tied to past or expected referrals.
  4. Returns are directly proportional to capital invested.
  5. Neither the center nor other investors lend money to, or guarantee a loan for, an investor's buy-in.

Missing a safe harbor does not make an arrangement illegal, but it raises risk. Federal self-referral rules and state laws also apply. Get a healthcare attorney to review any buy-in, joint venture or office suite plan before you sign. See how to choose a healthcare attorney.

How to choose

  1. You already operate at a well-run ASC. Ask for a buy-in, and diligence it like any investment.
  2. Your group has the volume to fill a center. Compare a new ASC with two or three management partners on fees, control and exit terms.
  3. Mostly cash-pay or office-level procedures. An accredited office suite may pay back faster with less risk.
  4. Low volume or complex patients. Stay at the hospital and revisit once volume grows.
  5. You plan to sell the practice in a few years. ASC ownership can lift value, but ask how buyers will price ASC income.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Is buying into an ASC a good investment?

It can be, if the center has steady volume, a healthy payer mix and fair partner terms. One ASC operator has said diligent partners can expect a full return in two to four years. Review the financials, debt and exit terms first.

How much does it cost to build a surgery center?

One 2026 cost guide puts a two operating room single-specialty ASC at $2 million to $3 million to build, and larger centers at $4 million to $8 million. Equipment, land and working capital come on top. Expect 12 to 24 months to open.

Is an office surgery suite cheaper than an ASC?

Usually yes, to build. But Medicare pays no separate ASC facility fee for office procedures, so the income is lower for insured cases. Office suites fit cash-pay and office-level work best.

Does an office surgery suite need accreditation?

It depends on your state and the anesthesia you use. Many states require accreditation above a set sedation level. AAAHC and QUAD A both accredit office-based surgery.

How are ASC distributions taxed?

Most ASCs pass profit through on a K-1, and you owe tax on your share even if the cash is not paid out. Distributions are the cash you receive. Your CPA should plan estimates around both.

What is a capital call?

A request that partners put in more money, for example for new equipment, an expansion or a cash shortfall. Ask how often capital calls have happened. Keep reserves in case one comes.

Do I need a healthcare attorney for an ASC buy-in?

Yes. ASC ownership must fit federal anti-kickback and self-referral rules and state laws. An attorney should review the operating agreement, the buy-in price and the exit terms.
