PLLC vs S corp for a medical practice

Most owners choose S corp tax once the payroll tax they'd save is clearly more than the extra cost of running one. A PLLC and a professional corporation (PC) are legal forms your state lets a medical practice use. An S corp is a tax choice you make with the IRS, and a PLLC or a PC can make it.

Key points

  • In Example 1, an owner with $150,000 of profit and a $90,000 salary saves about $4,400 after costs.
  • A PLLC isn't an S corp. You choose S corp tax by filing Form 2553 with the IRS.
  • As an S corp owner, you pay Social Security and Medicare tax only on the reasonable salary you pay yourself through payroll.
  • For 2026, you pay the 12.4% Social Security part only on the first $184,500 of your earnings, so the saving doesn't keep growing as your profit grows.
  • To be taxed as an S corp for all of 2027, you'll generally file Form 2553 by March 15, 2027.

What's the difference between a PLLC, a PC and an S corp?

A PLLC and a PC are set up under state law; an S corp is a federal tax status. Your state decides which legal form a licensed medical practice can use. The IRS then taxes that form by default, unless you choose S corp or C corp tax instead.

A PLLC (professional limited liability company) is an LLC that your state reserves for licensed professionals. A professional corporation (PC) is a corporation your state reserves for licensed professionals. Self-employment tax is the 15.3% Social Security and Medicare tax you pay on your own business profit. A reasonable salary is the pay an S corp owner gives themselves through payroll for the work they do.

Legal form How the IRS taxes it by default Federal return
Sole proprietor (no company) Your personal return Form 1040 with Schedule C
PLLC with one owner As if there were no company Form 1040 with Schedule C, or Form 1120-S as an S corp
PLLC with more than one owner As a partnership Form 1065, or Form 1120-S as an S corp
Professional corporation (PC) As a C corp Form 1120, or Form 1120-S as an S corp

The IRS explains the default rules on its LLC page.

How does an S corp lower a practice owner's tax?

As a default PLLC owner, you pay self-employment tax on nearly all of your profit. As an S corp owner, you pay Social Security and Medicare tax only on your salary, and you take the rest of the profit as distributions without that tax. Your income tax changes less, though your QBI deduction can shift, as explained below.

Self-employment tax is 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. For 2026, you pay the Social Security part only on the first $184,500 of combined wages and self-employment earnings. You also pay an extra 0.9% Medicare tax above $200,000 if you're single, or $250,000 married filing jointly (IRS, self-employment tax).

Example 1: one owner, two profit levels. A single owner runs a PLLC, has no other wages and uses 2026 rates. Self-employment tax applies to 92.35% of profit. The salaries are assumptions, not a guide to what you should pay yourself. The table leaves out income tax.

Self-employment tax plus the 0.9% added Medicare tax, as a default PLLC $21,194 $33,361

What does it cost to run a practice as an S corp?

As an S corp, you'll run payroll for yourself, file a separate business return on Form 1120-S, and pay any fees your state charges S corps. Your CPA's fee for the extra return and your payroll service are the main new costs, and you'll pay them every year, including the years profit is low.

  • Payroll for yourself: you'll file Form 941 each quarter (IRS Publication 15).
  • A business return: you'll file Form 1120-S by March 15, with a Schedule K-1 for each owner.
  • State costs: some states charge S corps a minimum tax or fee, and some cities don't recognize S corp status.

At what profit does an S corp start to pay off?

No single profit number fits every practice. You'll save roughly 15.3% of the profit you take as distributions, up to the Social Security limit, and only 2.9% to 3.8% above it. When those costs eat most of the saving, S corp tax isn't worth it yet.

To estimate your own, with your CPA:

  1. Estimate the practice's yearly profit before you pay yourself.
  2. Subtract a salary you could defend for your clinical and management work.
  3. Figure the payroll and self-employment tax both ways, as in Example 1.
  4. Subtract the yearly cost of payroll, the extra return and state fees.

A lower salary raises the saving on paper, but the IRS can treat distributions as wages if your salary is too low for the work you do (IRS, S corporation compensation). For how owners set that salary, read how medical S corps set reasonable owner salaries.

S corp tax also changes the 20% qualified business income (QBI) deduction. The QBI deduction is a deduction of up to 20% of business profit for owners of pass-through businesses. Health care is a specified service, so you lose some or all of it above an income threshold the IRS sets each year, and your S corp salary doesn't count as QBI (Instructions for Form 8995).

What S corp rules does a practice owner have to follow?

The IRS lets a business be an S corp only if it has 100 or fewer shareholders, one class of stock, and no nonresident alien or business-entity shareholders. As an S corp owner, you'll also follow special rules on your own health insurance and on ending the election (IRS, S corporations).

The 2% rule. If you own more than 2% of an S corp, the practice adds the health insurance it pays for you to your W-2 wages, and you deduct it on your personal return.

Example 2: the 2% rule. An S corp practice pays $14,400 a year for its sole owner's health insurance under a plan for its employees, with the policy in the owner's name or the practice's.

Where the $14,400 shows up Amount
The S corp's deduction, as wages $14,400
The owner's W-2, Box 1 (wages for income tax) $14,400
The owner's W-2, Boxes 3 and 5 (Social Security and Medicare wages) $0
The owner's self-employed health insurance deduction on Form 1040 Up to $14,400

The five-year rule. If you end your S corp election, you'll generally need IRS consent to choose S corp tax again before the fifth tax year after it ended (Instructions for Form 2553).

How and when can a practice switch to S corp tax?

You switch by filing Form 2553, signed by every owner. You'll file it no later than 2 months and 15 days after the start of the tax year you want it to cover, or at any time in the year before. For a calendar year 2027 election, that's Monday, March 15, 2027.

When you want S corp tax to start You file Form 2553 by
January 1, 2027, for a practice already open March 15, 2027
July 1, 2027, for a new PLLC that opens that day September 15, 2027
A date you've already missed Ask your CPA about late election relief

A PLLC doesn't need a separate form to be treated as a corporation first; the IRS treats a timely Form 2553 as that choice. Every 2027 filing date is in the 2027 tax deadlines for medical practices guide.

Should a medical practice be a C corp instead?

Few practice owners choose C corp tax. A C corp pays a flat 21% federal tax on its profit, and you pay tax again when it pays you dividends. Most owners take the profit as salary to avoid that, which leaves little profit in the company.

Who can own a med spa if you aren't a licensed clinician?

In many states, only a licensed medical professional can own the part of a med spa that does medical treatments. If you aren't one, you can't simply own the whole med spa as one PLLC. Owners in that position often set up two companies instead.

Corporate practice of medicine is the rule, in states that have it, that a company owned by people without a medical license can't provide medical care or employ the clinicians who do. A management services organization (MSO) is a company that runs the business side of a practice, such as staff, space, marketing and billing, under a contract with the medical practice.

In a common setup, a licensed clinician owns the medical practice, and you own the MSO. The practice pays the MSO a management fee. Your share of the profit lands in the MSO, so the MSO's tax choice, whether PLLC, S corp or C corp, decides how you're taxed on it. A healthcare attorney should draw up the fee and the contract, because states set limits on both.

For the books of a two-company setup, see bookkeeping for med spas.

How do state rules change the answer for dental and physical therapy practices?

Your state's rules can change both the legal form you can use and the tax you'll pay each year. Dental boards and physical therapy boards set their own ownership rules, and some states don't offer a PLLC to every profession.

  • Dental practices: check which forms your state dental board allows. See bookkeeping for dental practices.
  • Physical therapy practices: check whether your state lets a PT practice form a PLLC. See bookkeeping for physical therapy practices.

Questions, answered

Questions practice owners ask about PLLCs and S corps

This guide is general information, not tax advice for your practice. Tax rules change and depend on your state and your numbers. Talk to a CPA before you act on it.

Is a PLLC an S corp?

No. A PLLC is a legal form your state creates, and an S corp is a way the IRS taxes a business. By default, the IRS taxes a one-owner PLLC like a sole proprietor and a multi-owner PLLC like a partnership. If you file Form 2553 on time, you can have your PLLC taxed as an S corp.

Should my medical practice be an LLC or an S corp?

You can be both. In most states, you'd form a PLLC (or a PC), then decide whether to elect S corp tax. If your profit is modest or uneven, the default is usually simpler. If your profit is steadily well above a salary you could defend, ask your CPA to run the numbers both ways, as in Example 1.

At what income does an S corp make sense for a practice owner?

The IRS sets no income level. You'd compare the payroll tax you'd save on distributions with the yearly cost of payroll, the extra return and state fees. In Example 1, an owner with $150,000 of profit and a $90,000 salary saves about $4,400 after costs.

Can someone without a medical license own a med spa?

It depends on your state. Many states don't let someone without a medical license own the part of a med spa that does medical treatments. Owners in that position often own a management company that runs the business side under contract with a clinician-owned practice. Have a healthcare attorney in your state review the setup before you open.

What is the 2% rule for S corps?

If you own more than 2% of an S corp, the IRS treats the health insurance the practice pays for you as wages on your W-2, usually without Social Security or Medicare tax. You then deduct the premiums on your personal return. A 2% owner also can't join a small employer health reimbursement arrangement (QSEHRA).

Can I switch my practice to an S corp this year, and what is the deadline?

Usually, yes. To be an S corp for all of 2027, you'll file Form 2553 by March 15, 2027. A new practice has 2 months and 15 days from the day its tax year starts. If you've missed the date, the IRS may still accept a late election under Rev. Proc. 2013-30 if you have a reasonable cause.
