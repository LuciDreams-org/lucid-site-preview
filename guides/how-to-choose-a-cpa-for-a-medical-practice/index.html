Short answer

Choose a CPA in four steps. First, confirm the license with your state board or the national CPAverify tool. Second, ask who prepares the return, who reviews it and who signs it. Third, ask for examples of practices like yours: same size, same number of locations, same payer mix. Fourth, compare the full fee, not only the monthly number, including bookkeeping, tax, planning and catch up work. A firm that knows medical practice revenue, such as Lucid, which puts a dedicated CPA on every account and starts from $130 a month, spends the least time learning how your business works.

Do you need a CPA, a bookkeeper or an enrolled agent?

These roles overlap, but they are not the same job.

  1. A bookkeeper records and reconciles transactions each month. Bookkeepers are not licensed by a state board in most cases, so judge them on process and references.
  2. A CPA is licensed by a state board of accountancy. The IRS says certified public accountants, enrolled agents and attorneys have unlimited representation rights before the IRS, which means they can represent you in audits, collection matters and appeals.
  3. An enrolled agent is a tax specialist who has federal authority to represent taxpayers. Enrolled agents focus on tax, not on monthly books or planning.
  4. Other paid preparers may hold a PTIN, the IRS preparer number, and some join the IRS Annual Filing Season Program. The IRS describes their representation rights as limited: they can represent only clients whose returns they prepared and signed, and not in appeals or collection.

A practice with staff, payroll and more than one income source usually needs more than a tax preparer. It needs monthly books that are right, a plan for estimated payments, and a person who can speak for you if the IRS writes. That is why many owners want a CPA involved. Your accountant is a licensed professional you can hold to account, and the IRS notes that you stay responsible for every item on your return, so the choice matters.

Step 1: Check the license

Do this before the first call goes far.

  1. Ask for the CPA's full name and the state of licensure.
  2. Search CPAverify, the license search tool run by NASBA. NASBA describes it as populated by official licensing data sent from the boards of accountancy, with 53 jurisdictions taking part.
  3. Look for an active license and any listed discipline. For a firm, check whether the state board lists the firm as well.
  4. Ask for the preparer's PTIN. The IRS says a paid preparer must have one and must put it on the return.

A license tells you the person is qualified to hold the title. It does not tell you whether they understand a practice with insurance deposits and several providers. That is step three.

Step 2: Ask who does the work

Many practices sign with a CPA and then find a junior preparer does most of the work. That is not always bad, but you should know. Ask these questions in writing.

  1. Who prepares my return, who reviews it and who signs it?
  2. Who answers my questions during the year, and how fast?
  3. Does the CPA also look at my monthly books, or only the tax return at year end?
  4. Will anyone review my books before the return is prepared?
  5. If the IRS or a state agency writes to me, who responds? Will the firm file a Form 2848 so they can speak to the IRS for me? The IRS describes Form 2848 as the form used to authorize an individual to represent you before the IRS.

Lucid says every account has a dedicated CPA, plus a dedicated account manager in a shared Slack channel with replies within one business day. Whatever firm you pick, ask for the equivalent in writing: a named person and a response time.

Step 3: Test for medical practice knowledge

A general accountant can do your return. The difference shows up in the details of how a practice earns and spends money. Ask the CPA to explain, in plain words, how they would handle these.

  1. Insurance deposits. A deposit often covers many patients and many dates of service, with adjustments and write offs. Ask how the firm matches deposits to the billing report and what it does when they do not match.
  2. Revenue by location or provider. Ask what a monthly report looks like. A single profit and loss statement for the whole practice hides which site or provider carries the rest.
  3. Owner pay. If the practice is an S corporation, ask how the firm decides on owner salary and distributions, and ask where the IRS reasonable compensation rules fit. Your CPA should explain it, not just apply it.
  4. Several entities. If you own more than one practice, or have a practice company and a management company, ask how the firm keeps the entities separate and how it handles the money between them.
  5. Specialty costs. A dermatology, dental or orthopedic practice has different cost lines. Lucid's specialty pages describe the reporting it offers for primary care, orthopedics, dermatology, dental and others.
  6. Tax credits. Ask whether the firm looks for credits such as R&D or hiring credits, and how it charges for that work. Lucid says that if it finds no credits, you pay $0.

Good answers are specific and tied to your practice. Weak answers are general: "we work with many small businesses." A firm that asks you for your last two months of bank statements and a recent billing report before quoting is showing the right habit.

Step 4: Compare the full cost

Monthly fees are easy to compare and easy to misread. Ask each firm to quote the same scope.

  1. Monthly bookkeeping. Is it included, and what does it cover?
  2. The business tax return and the owner's personal return. Are both quoted?
  3. Quarterly estimated tax planning.
  4. Catch up work if your books are behind. See how to catch up bookkeeping.
  5. CFO style work such as forecasting and budgets.
  6. Setup fees, minimum terms and cancellation terms.

Red flags

  1. The firm cannot name the CPA who will sign your return.
  2. A fee based on a percentage of your refund.
  3. No written scope, or a scope that leaves out catch up work and tax.
  4. Pressure to sign before you have seen a sample report.
  5. A preparer who asks you to sign a blank form.
  6. No answer on who responds if you get an IRS notice.
  7. Claims of guaranteed refunds or guaranteed tax savings.

A short checklist for the first call

  1. Name and state of the CPA, and a CPAverify check.
  2. Who prepares, reviews and signs.
  3. Three examples of practices like yours, without names.
  4. A sample monthly report.
  5. A written quote for the full scope.
  6. Response time, and the channel you will use.
  7. How the firm handles notices and amended returns.
  8. How you leave: terms, data access and handover.

Bring your entity type, number of owners, locations and the last two months of statements. A good firm can tell you quickly whether it is a fit.

Questions, answered

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a CPA who specializes in healthcare?

Not always, but it helps. A CPA who knows insurance deposits, provider productivity and multi location reporting needs less time to learn your business. Ask for examples of practices like yours, and a sample report.

How do I check that a CPA is licensed?

Search the name on CPAverify, run by NASBA, or on your state board of accountancy site. Look for an active license in the state where the CPA practices. For tax returns, also ask for the preparer's PTIN.

What is the difference between a CPA and an enrolled agent?

A CPA is licensed by a state board and can work across accounting, tax and planning. An enrolled agent is a federally authorized tax specialist. The IRS says both, along with attorneys, have unlimited representation rights before the IRS.

How much does a CPA for a medical practice cost?

It depends on size, locations, payers and scope. Lucid publishes a starting price of $130 a month, with the calculator on its pricing page. For others, ask for a written quote covering bookkeeping, tax and catch up work.

Should the CPA also do my monthly bookkeeping?

It can help, because the person who signs the return sees the books all year. Some practices use a bookkeeper and a CPA separately, which works if someone reviews the books before tax time. Ask whoever you hire how the two parts connect.

What should I bring to the first meeting?

Bring your entity type, owner details, the last two years of returns, recent bank statements and a billing report. This lets the firm tell you quickly if it is a fit and what it would cost. A good firm will also tell you what it still needs from you before it can quote.
